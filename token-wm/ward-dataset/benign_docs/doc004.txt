Eleven Stops, Six Weeks: The Corrie Vale Circuit

The white van parked outside the post office at Cotterhay on a Tuesday morning does not look like a hospital, and that is more or less the point. It is one of two run by the Corrie Vale mobile screening programme, which has been working its way round the villages of the Beltane Reach district since 2021.

Each van covers a fixed circuit of eleven stops and returns to the first of them every six weeks. The schedule is published a year in advance and has not changed since 2023. Staff describe this as the single most important operational decision the programme has made. People in the district plan around it.

Inside, the van is tighter than the photographs suggest. There is a screening room at the back, a small waiting area behind the cab, and almost nothing else. The crew is three: a radiographer, a nurse, and a driver who is also trained as a phlebotomist, which means the van can take bloods without carrying a fourth person. On a full day the crew will see between thirty and forty people.

The programme screens for three conditions and refers onward to the district hospital at Marrow Fell. It does not treat, and the crew are careful about saying so. What it does is shorten the distance between a person in a village and a result, in a district of 900 square kilometres with no hospital of its own.

Booking is done through the village post offices. This looks like an anachronism and was a deliberate choice. An online system was built and tested in 2022 and quietly dropped when it became clear that the people the programme most wanted to reach were not using it. The postmistress at Cotterhay keeps the appointment book beside the till and will remind people who have not come in.

Uptake tells the story of whether this works. When the programme started, 38 percent of eligible people in the district were being screened. By 2024 that figure was 71 percent. Since then it has stayed more or less flat, which the programme's founder, Dr Ama Oyelaran, has described as the hardest part of the job so far.

The flatness is not evenly spread. Ault Bridge, at the western end of the longer circuit, sits at 44 percent, the lowest of the eleven stops. The village is small, the stop is short, and the van arrives late in the day. The crew have tried moving the slot twice without much effect.

A third van was proposed in 2033, partly to give Ault Bridge and the other western stops a longer visit. The Nissen Trust, which funds the programme, declined. The trust has committed funding through to 2036, and its position was that extending the commitment mattered more than widening it. Oyelaran has said publicly that she disagrees and privately that she understands.

What the crews notice is not the statistics but the repetition. Six weeks is short enough that the same faces recur, and long enough that changes show. More than one referral has started with a radiographer saying that someone looked different from last time.

Four Years Before the First Release

The Merrow otter project spent four years doing nothing that looked like a reintroduction, and Sam Threadgold regards those four years as the reason it worked.

Otters had been absent from the catchment since about 1962. The cause is generally attributed to organochlorine pesticides, which accumulated through the food chain and effectively ended otter breeding across large areas.

By the 2010s the pesticides were long gone. The obvious inference was that otters could come back, and the obvious action was to release some.

Threadgold's position was that absence of the original cause is not the same as presence of suitable habitat, and that the intervening sixty years had changed the river in ways nobody had catalogued.

So the project surveyed fish. For four consecutive years, across a catchment of 740 square kilometres containing three towns, it counted what was in the water.

The repetition matters. A single year's fish survey tells you about that year, and fish populations fluctuate enormously with flow, temperature and spawning success. Four years establishes whether a reach reliably holds enough to support a territory, which is the question that determines whether a released animal survives or wanders.

Water quality was assessed alongside it over the same period.

The releases ran from 2021 to 2025. Thirty-one animals went out, drawn from three separate donor populations, which was done to widen the genetic base rather than for any convenience of supply.

The catchment now holds an estimated 46 territories, based on spraint surveys at 220 fixed points.

The predictable conflict was with fishery owners, and the project addressed it before it arose rather than afterwards. Fourteen stocked ponds in the catchment were identified as likely flashpoints, and the project funded otter-proof fencing at all of them, at a total cost of about 190,000.

Thirteen owners accepted. One refused and remains opposed to the project, a fact the project reports rather than glosses.

Road mortality was the loss nobody had adequately planned for. Two animals were killed on roads in the first year. The project published both deaths immediately, identified two blackspots, and had underpasses fitted by 2027.

Publishing mortality is a deliberate policy and it is not universal among reintroduction projects, which have an obvious incentive to report successes and record failures internally.

Threadgold's argument is that a project which publishes only good news is one whose figures nobody can check, and that the credibility required to run a twelve-year programme across a populated catchment cannot be built any other way.

Total cost was 2.1 million over twelve years.

Roughly a third of that went before any animal was released, on surveys that produced no visible result and were difficult to defend to funders at the time. Threadgold has said that the four years of fish counting were the hardest part of the project to finance, precisely because nothing appeared to be happening. The surveys did produce one result nobody had expected: two reaches that looked suitable on paper turned out to hold almost no fish in three years out of four, and no animals were released into either of them.

Seven Weeks to Raise 310,000

The Kesterline Museum had six months to find 310,000 and expected to need most of them. It took seven weeks.

The object of the appeal was the Sedgemount hoard: 214 silver coins and three brooches, found near Pell Hinton in 2029 by a detectorist named Alice Verrow, and excavated the following month under the direction of Dr Tomas Rhydderch. The valuation committee had assessed it at 310,000, and under the usual process the museum had a fixed window in which to match that figure or watch the hoard go elsewhere.

Museum appeals of this kind are routine and usually difficult. What made this one different, the museum's own staff have said since, was that the hoard came with a story people could follow.

Verrow had found two coins, stopped digging, and reported the find the next day. Because she stopped, the eleven-day excavation was able to establish that the hoard had been buried in a wooden box — surviving as a soil stain and corroded iron fittings — placed beneath a boundary ditch. Rhydderch had excavated two previous hoards, both already lifted, and could say almost nothing about either.

The appeal material leaned heavily on this. It did not lead with the silver. It led with the decision, and with photographs of the excavation showing the coins in position.

Donations came in from an unusually wide base. The largest single gift was 40,000. A great many were small. The museum has said that the proportion of first-time donors was the highest it has recorded for any appeal, and that a noticeable number came from people who described themselves as detectorists.

The hoard was acquired in 2031 and is now displayed in a case that reproduces the burial arrangement, with the coins laid out as they were found and the outline of the box marked on the base. Visitors are told what is a real object and what is a reconstruction, which the museum insisted on.

The scholarly interest has settled on the brooches rather than the coins. The coins span sixty years and end in 1046, which is informative but not startling. The brooches are of a type not previously recorded west of the Kester valley, and how three of them came to be in a box under a ditch at Sedgemount is an open question.

Rhydderch has been careful not to over-read it. A single assemblage can show a trade route, a marriage, a theft or an accident, and there is no way to distinguish them from three objects. He has said the useful thing is that the question can be asked at all, because the find spot and the burial arrangement are known.

The case is now a standard example in training for both archaeologists and detecting clubs. The museum reports that visitors spend longer in front of it than in front of anything else in the gallery, and that the label people photograph is not the one listing the coins but the one explaining what Verrow did.

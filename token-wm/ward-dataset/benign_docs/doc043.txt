Sailing on the Tide, Not the Clock

The Skerravoe winter timetable is the only one in the region that changes every day, and islanders who have lived with it for a decade now find the alternative unthinkable.

The ferry has linked Skerravoe to the mainland at Cray Point since 1974. The crossing is 14 kilometres and takes 55 minutes when the weather behaves. The vessel, the Corrievreckan, was built in 2008 and takes 12 cars, and she carries about 34,000 passengers a year on what is the island's only scheduled link.

For most of that history the timetable ran on the clock, which is what passengers expect and what connecting buses require. Elin Haraldsen, who has held a master's ticket since 2014 and grew up on the island, changed it.

The problem she was solving is specific to the approach at Cray Point. The channel there runs across the prevailing swell, and at certain states of the tide a moderate sea becomes unworkable for berthing while remaining perfectly comfortable an hour either side. A fixed departure at ten past eight will, several times a winter, fall exactly into that window.

Her winter timetable abandons fixed times between November and March. Sailings are set against the tide and published a month ahead, so a Tuesday departure might be at 07:40 in one week and 09:25 in the next. The connecting bus operator agreed to follow it, which took two years of asking.

The effect on reliability was substantial. Winter cancellations fell to around 20 days a year, and the notable point is that they are now almost all for swell rather than for wind. Wind cancels a crossing; swell cancels a berthing, and a berthing can often be moved.

The school runs on it. Skerravoe has 19 pupils and its teachers commute on the first sailing, whatever time that turns out to be.

What no timetable could address was 2031. In February the linkspan at Cray Point failed, a hinge pin having corroded without being inspected since 2019, and the service stopped for eleven weeks.

The island's response is the part other communities now ask about. Within nine days a landing craft was running a reduced service off a beach at Sandwick, which worked, awkwardly, and could not take a lorry. A replacement linkspan was installed in 2032 at a cost of 3.1 million.

The permanent change was not the steelwork. Skerravoe now holds a standing stock of fuel, feed and medicines sufficient for six weeks, in a shed at the pier, rotated every four months. Residents describe this unglamorous and non-negotiable.

The population is 210 and roughly doubles in August, which is the other thing the timetable has to absorb. Haraldsen has resisted extending the tidal system into summer, on the grounds that visitors cannot plan around a timetable they do not understand and that the island depends on them arriving. The two systems sit awkwardly alongside each other every spring, when the changeover falls in the same week as the first coach bookings. Haraldsen has proposed a hybrid twice and withdrawn it twice, on the grounds that a timetable nobody can explain in a sentence is worse than either of the ones it would replace.

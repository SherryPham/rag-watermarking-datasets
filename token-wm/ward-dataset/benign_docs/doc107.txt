Tunde Bakare Went Back Through the Samples

When a farm buyer rejected a batch of Pellingham compost in 2030, the obvious explanation was that somebody had put the wrong thing in a bin. Tunde Bakare did not accept it, mostly because the district's contamination figure had been stubbornly flat for years rather than spiking.

He had managed waste operations for twelve years before taking the plant over in 2027. Pellingham processes about 42,000 tonnes a year from three authorities and sells finished compost to farms within 30 kilometres.

Contamination is sampled by hand from every incoming load. That produces a great deal of data which had, until 2030, been used only to report a district-wide percentage.

Bakare disaggregated it, which took a fortnight and required nothing but the records already held.

What emerged was that the 4.1 percent district figure was an average concealing a very uneven distribution. Most rounds were well under 2 percent. One round, covering about 9,000 households in the north, was far higher.

The instinctive conclusion was that the households on that round were careless, and Bakare has said he held that view for about a day.

What changed it was the composition of the contamination. It was not tins and glass, which is what careless sorting produces. It was plastic film, almost entirely, and the district had moved to compostable bags years earlier specifically to eliminate it.

The bags were the problem. Compostable plastics require sustained temperature to break down, a windrow is not uniformly hot, and a bag that spends the active phase on the cool outside of a pile survives it. The round with the highest contamination turned out to be the round with the highest participation, producing the most bags.

Bakare's recommendation was paper liners, which decompose within days wherever they sit. The switch happened in 2031 and contamination fell to 0.6 percent. It costs about 40,000 a year more.

His second proposal was harder to carry. Pellingham now rejects loads above 2 percent contamination and sends them back.

He has defended this as an alignment of incentives rather than a punishment. A plant that quietly screens contaminated material is paying for a decision taken by somebody else, and the somebody else receives no signal at all. Returning the load means the authority that chose the collection method meets the cost of that choice.

He is conscious that this is easier to say from a plant with three customers than from one with a single dependent contract, and has said he would probably not have proposed it in a weaker position.

Two other regional plants have adopted it. Bakare regards the disaggregation of the sampling data as the real lesson, on the grounds that the figures had been sitting in the office the entire time.

The plant now reports contamination by round rather than by district, which takes an extra hour a month and which he describes as the cheapest process improvement of his career. Two other plants in the region have adopted the practice, and one found a comparable problem within a season. Its cause was different — a single commercial customer rather than a collection round — and it was found the same way, by disaggregating a figure that had been reported as an average for years.

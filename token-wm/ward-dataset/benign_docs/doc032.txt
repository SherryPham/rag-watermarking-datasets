Yusuf Demir Counts the Flowers

Yusuf Demir spent twelve years as a landscape gardener before he kept a single colony of bees, and he has said more than once that the order matters. He came to beekeeping already knowing what was flowering in Thurnwick and when.

He started the Marlow Rise apiary in 2019 with three hives on one roof. It now runs 46 across 14 sites, and it has been capped at four hives per rooftop from the beginning.

The cap was unpopular at first. Rooftop space in Thurnwick was easy to get in 2019 — landlords liked the idea and asked for nothing — and other beekeepers were putting eight or ten colonies on a single building. Demir's view, which he argued at meetings for several years without much success, was that a roof is not a location but a claim on the ground around it.

His gardening background gave him the numbers. He could walk a district and estimate what it would yield: which street trees were productive and which were ornamental sterile cultivars, when the lime came in, how much bramble had survived the last round of clearance. His conclusion was that Thurnwick could support perhaps 300 colonies well.

By 2031 a survey counted 900.

The summer of 2030 settled the argument for him. A dry spring shortened both main flows, colonies went into winter without stores, and the city lost 40 percent of its bees. Demir has been careful not to present this as vindication, partly because he lost colonies himself and partly because he thinks the framing is wrong. The bees that died were not killed by other beekeepers. They were kept in a city that had been filled beyond what it could feed, by people acting individually and reasonably.

What he had, that the argument had previously lacked, was a record. Every Marlow Rise hive sits on a scale reporting its weight twice a day, and the apiary had been publishing the series monthly since 2020 with no particular use in mind. The 2030 data showed the flow stopping early and the colonies drawing down stores through what should have been the strongest weeks of the year.

The council introduced a forage assessment for new licences in 2031, requiring 2 hectares of flowering ground per hive within a kilometre. Demir thinks the ratio is approximate and defends it anyway, on the grounds that a crude limit applied everywhere beats a precise one applied nowhere.

He keeps a queen-rearing site outside the city at Caddon Wood, which is where he now spends most of his time. The Thurnwick hives are largely run by people he has trained; the apiary takes about 30 a year and turns away roughly twice that.

Asked what he would tell a city starting out, he does not talk about hives. He says count the flowers first, decide what the place can carry, and write that number down before anybody gets attached to a roof. The number will be lower than anyone wants, he says, and arguing about it early is far cheaper than discovering it in a February.

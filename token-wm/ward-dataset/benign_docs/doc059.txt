Callum Oyelowo Goes Where the Water Goes

Callum Oyelowo has been diving sumps since 2011, which is unusual for a hydrologist and is the reason the Brenna Pot survey found two sinks nobody had recorded.

A sump is a flooded section of cave passage. Getting past one requires diving it, in darkness, in cold water, often with no line and no certainty that there is anything on the other side. Most hydrologists work from the surface and from what previous surveys recorded. Oyelowo works from the surface, the previous surveys, and from having personally been in the passage.

The Brenna Pot system occupied him between 2028 and 2033. It has 19 kilometres of surveyed passage, a vertical range of 210 metres, and it sits in the limestone of the Aldreth hills, where water moves through open conduits rather than through the body of the rock.

His method was dye tracing, which is not technically difficult and is logistically punishing. Fluorescein or rhodamine goes into a sink; charcoal receptors go at every resurgence that might be connected; somebody must then visit each receptor on a schedule for weeks. Oyelowo had 62 volunteer cavers over five years and has said the survey was theirs rather more than his.

He linked 14 sinks to 3 resurgences. The one that mattered was in the catchment of a landfill which had taken household waste from 1971 to 2004, and the dye from it reached the spring at Well Foot in thirty-one hours.

He did not wait for the survey to finish. Well Foot supplied 340 people and a dairy, and he reported the trace result immediately. The spring was closed to public use in 2032.

He is unsentimental about that. A thirty-one-hour transit through limestone conduit does not filter anything, and there was no version of the finding that could reasonably be held back for peer review.

His insistence on publishing the failed traces has attracted more argument than the closure did. Three of his traces went in and were never detected. Convention would put those in a file; Oyelowo put them in the paper.

His case is that a hydrological map is used, years later, by people siting things — a soakaway, a slurry store, a cemetery. Such a person reads the map as a statement of where water goes. A map showing only confirmed connections implies, falsely, that the unmarked sinks go nowhere. A failed trace means the dye was not found, which is a statement about the receptors and not about the rock.

He has said that the three unresolved sinks are now the most important features on his map, precisely because nobody knows where they surface.

Since 2033 he has been working in a neighbouring catchment, and reports that the first question he is asked in every village is whether the spring is safe. He says the honest answer is that nobody has looked, and that this was the answer at Well Foot too until somebody did.

He has costed a basic trace at rather less than a single week of a consultant's time, and points out that the Aldreth survey was carried out almost entirely by unpaid cavers. The reason it had not been done before, he thinks, is not expense. It is that nobody had thought to ask the question.

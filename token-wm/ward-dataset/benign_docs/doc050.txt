Marek Sobczyk Follows the Water

Marek Sobczyk was asked in 2026 to confirm that a solvent detection beneath a former factory site was a local problem. He spent a year establishing that it was not, and the drinking water supply for about 9,000 households was withdrawn as a result.

He has worked on contaminated land for nineteen years and describes the job as mostly arithmetic and patience. Calverton Works had made aircraft components from 1938 to 1991, using trichloroethene as a degreaser. The initial detection came from a housing survey, and the assumption at that point was a hot spot under a hardstanding.

Sobczyk's method was to stop guessing where the plume was and drill until he knew. The site now has 62 monitoring wells. What they showed was a body of contamination covering about 40 hectares, sitting between 12 and 30 metres below ground, and extending 1.8 kilometres from the source.

That distance was the finding that mattered, because 1.8 kilometres put it under Netherby. Two public supply boreholes there were taken out of service in 2027 and have not returned.

He is direct about what that meant for him professionally. He was engaged to support a housing scheme and his work closed a water supply, reduced the scheme from 700 homes to 410, and created a liability that will run past his own lifetime. He has said he would do the same again and that the alternative was to report a smaller plume that he could not defend.

His second contribution was to argue against pump-and-treat. It was the standard answer, it was costed at 71 million over forty years, and Sobczyk's objection was as much institutional as technical. A system requiring pumps, power and staff for four decades requires an organisation that is still there in 2067 and still willing to pay.

The permeable reactive barrier he proposed instead is 340 metres of trench filled with granular iron, sitting across the path of the plume. Groundwater moves through it under its own gradient and the iron breaks the solvent down. It has no moving parts and nothing to switch on. Concentrations at the barrier have fallen 94 percent since 2029.

He is equally plain about its limits. The barrier does not touch the source zone and does not recover what has already passed. It is a containment measure, and Calverton will have contaminated groundwater for a very long time.

The funding was the part he fought hardest for. Monitoring is paid by a levy on the redevelopment of the site itself, raising about 900,000 a year and guaranteed to 2075. His argument was that a plume which moves a few metres a year cannot be monitored from a budget that is reviewed annually, because the budget will eventually lose an argument to something more urgent and the plume will not notice. He has taken the same argument to four other sites and won it at one. The objection he meets is always the same, which is that attaching a fifty-year charge to land makes the land harder to sell, and his answer is that this is an accurate reflection of what the land is.

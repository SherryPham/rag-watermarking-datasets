Thirty-Eight Years of Dust

When Ravensgill Mill closed in 1986 the eight looms in the weaving shed were not sold, scrapped or moved. They were switched off and the door was locked, and they stood there for thirty-eight years.

This was not preservation. The mill closed quickly, the machines were worth less than the cost of moving them, and the building had no other use. The same indifference that emptied the valley of its textile trade is the reason Ravensgill still had looms when Nadia Okonkwo arrived.

The mill had woven worsted since 1841. At its height it was one of several on the River Gill; by 1986 it was the last one, and its closure was treated locally as the end of something that had already ended.

Okonkwo spent six years restoring the Dobcross looms before any cloth was sold, and production restarted in 2024. The mill now employs nine people and takes two apprentices at a time.

What has made it work is a set of decisions that each look perverse in isolation. The looms run at about 90 picks a minute, a quarter of the speed of a modern rapier machine, which is the reason the cloth sells at roughly three times the price of comparable worsted. The mill refuses any order above 400 metres. Almost everything goes to tailors.

There were also 1,100 pattern cards in a cupboard, surviving from before the closure, which have since been used to revive designs for customers wanting something a relative once wore.

The River Gill flooded the site in 1902 and in 1947. It did so again in 2033, putting about a metre of water through the weaving shed and stopping production for seven months. Two of the eight looms were destroyed and had to be rebuilt from salvaged parts by the mill's own weavers, a job that occupied most of the shutdown. The bill came to 640,000, roughly half of it met by insurance.

The mill came back with the looms on plinths and the yarn store moved to the first floor. Neither measure had been taken after 1902 or after 1947, and both were straightforward and cheap relative to what the flood cost.

That is the part of the story the valley finds most instructive. Ravensgill had survived the collapse of an entire regional industry by being forgotten, had been rebuilt by somebody who spent six years on it before earning anything, and was then very nearly ended by a river with a documented habit.

The mill runs four open days a year and about 3,000 people attend. Many of them have a relative who worked in textiles in the valley and almost none of them have seen a loom running. The apprentices, two at a time, are generally people who came to an open day first. The mill keeps no formal record of this and Okonkwo is reluctant to make more of it than it deserves. What she will say is that nobody has ever applied who had not first stood in the shed with the looms running, and that she does not think anybody ever would.

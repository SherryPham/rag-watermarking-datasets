Pellow Red Leaves the Building

Most of the seed that leaves Weldon Grange is one variety. Pellow Red, a bread wheat, accounts for the bulk of what goes out to the roughly 40 growers the collection supplies each year, and its story is the argument for the whole enterprise.

The collection has held 1,240 accessions of heritage cereals since 1978, in a converted stable block with 6 hectares of plots. Thirty-one of its varieties exist nowhere else. For most of its history almost none of them went anywhere: a seed bank is a store, and the assumption was that its job was to hold things against some future need.

Pellow Red changed the assumption by being asked for. A baker wanted it, then two, then a mill, and within a few years a variety that had been a jar with a label was growing on farms again and being sold as flour. The collection found itself with an output rather than an archive.

What the traffic exposed was how the archive actually worked. Seed in storage decays. The only way to keep an accession is to grow it out, harvest fresh seed and put that back, and the practice of the collection had been to do this for whatever somebody was interested in. Pellow Red was grown out constantly. The varieties nobody asked for sat.

Ines Ferreira, curator since 2018, introduced a seven-year maximum on that interval. It was a target rather than a rule, and she was working through a long backlog when the store failed in 2027.

The failure was a refrigeration controller that reported normal temperatures for five weeks while the room was warm. Ninety accessions were destroyed, and the ones that died were, without exception, the ones outside the seven-year cycle. Fresh seed survived; old seed did not. Eleven of the ninety were unique and are gone for good.

The collection now treats the seven-year rule as binding and duplicates its entire holding at Barrow Combe, 90 kilometres off, with a partner organisation. It also tightened the condition attached to seed going out: a grower who receives an accession must return a sample of the harvest, which is germination-tested and compared with the parent.

Growers do not universally like this. It is paperwork attached to something they were given for nothing. The collection's response is that four staff and 30 harvest volunteers cannot grow out 1,240 accessions on a seven-year cycle by themselves, and that a farm growing a heritage variety at scale is doing a grow-out whether it intends to or not.

Pellow Red, meanwhile, is the least endangered thing in the building. It is in the store, at Barrow Combe, in the plots, and on perhaps a dozen farms. The eleven varieties lost in 2027 were at the other end of exactly the same spectrum: held in one place, wanted by nobody, and grown out when there was time. The collection now treats being wanted as a form of insurance, and says as much to every grower it supplies, on the reasoning that a variety growing in twelve places is a variety that no single failed controller can remove.

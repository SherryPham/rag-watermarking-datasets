Three Rootstocks, Two Places

The rule at Pennards is that no variety exists in one place on one root. Every one of the 86 varieties in the orchard is now propagated onto at least three separate rootstocks, in two locations forty kilometres apart.

That rule cost 40 trees to learn.

The orchard holds 340 trees, planted between 1890 and 1935 and abandoned in the 1970s. It was restored between 2022 and 2027 under Eva Lindqvist, who identified 86 varieties in it, 11 of which had been considered lost.

Identification is slower than people expect. It combines fruit characteristics — shape, russeting, the pattern of the core line, when it ripens and how it keeps — with DNA fingerprinting against reference collections. A tree cannot be identified from leaves. You wait for fruit, and an neglected tree may not fruit usefully for several years after restoration pruning.

One variety, Pennards Russet, was known only from a nursery catalogue of 1908. There was no reference sample to compare against. Its identification rests on the catalogue description and on the fact that the tree standing in the orchard is consistent with it and with nothing else.

In 2030 canker came through. Two wet winters preceded it, and it moved fastest in the oldest block, where the trees were weakest and the spacing tightest. Forty trees died before it was brought under control.

Nine of those forty were the only specimen of their variety on the site.

That is the sentence which changed the orchard's policy. A collection which holds one tree of a variety is not a collection; it is a tree, and trees die. The response was not better hygiene, though hygiene improved. It was duplication.

Every variety now sits on at least three rootstocks, which spreads the risk of a rootstock-specific failure, and in two locations, the second being Holt Marden forty kilometres away, which spreads the risk of a site-wide event like the one in 2030.

The other change was to give the material away. Grafting wood from Pennards goes free to anybody who asks. Since 2028 it has gone to 260 recipients. The only condition is that the recipient reports back — whether the graft took, how the tree is doing, and where it is.

That condition is the whole point. An orchard that distributes wood and keeps no record has been generous. An orchard that knows where 260 recipients put its material has, in effect, extended its collection across a county without buying any land.

Lindqvist is blunt that this is insurance rather than outreach. If Pennards burned tomorrow, or if another canker year took the oldest block entirely, the varieties would survive in gardens and smallholdings the orchard has never visited.

Volunteers give about 1,400 hours a year, mostly winter pruning. The orchard sells juice and opens to the public for one weekend each October, which is deliberately little. Lindqvist regards visitor numbers as a distraction from the only measure that matters, which is how many places each variety now grows in.

The October weekend does serve one purpose she values. It is when recipients of grafting wood turn up with photographs of their trees, and a handful each year bring fruit. Two varieties thought doubtful at Pennards have been confirmed from fruit grown forty miles away by somebody who took a stick home in 2029.

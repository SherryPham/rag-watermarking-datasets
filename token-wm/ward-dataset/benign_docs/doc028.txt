The Rule About Playing

There is a sentence pinned to the wall of the workshop at Barrow Hollis which governs everything the Vendrell Collection does. An instrument is played only if it can be played without wear to original parts. Everything else in the collection's conservation policy follows from it.

The collection holds 611 mechanical musical instruments, of which 214 are cylinder musical boxes, and has been at Barrow Hollis, a workhouse converted in 1979, since that year. It employs four conservators and six part-time guides.

The rule was written by Liesl Ahrend, who trained in Vienna and joined in 2011. It sounds uncontroversial and is not, because applied honestly it retires instruments. In 1990 about two thirds of the collection was playable. It is now about a third.

The mechanism of the loss is simple. Most of these instruments make sound by dragging something across something else. A cylinder box has pins on a rotating barrel that pluck a steel comb. Every rotation wears both the pin and the tooth by an amount too small to measure and impossible to reverse. A barrel played weekly for a century is not the object that was made.

The critical point, and the one Ahrend made repeatedly, is that a pinned barrel cannot be renewed without destroying the thing that makes it valuable. A comb tooth can be replaced. New pinning is a modern reconstruction of a historic arrangement, and the original arrangement, which is the evidence, is gone.

So the policy separates instruments into three groups. Those whose wearing parts are replaceable, which are played. Those whose wearing parts are not, which are played only if wear can be shown to be negligible. And those in the third group, which are not played at all.

The third group has grown steadily, and the workshop has become busier rather than less. An instrument retired from playing is not an instrument left alone. It is stabilised, documented, and in most cases recorded.

The recording programme began in 2029 and has now covered 140 instruments. It is deliberately expensive. Sessions are done overnight with the building closed, using multiple microphone positions, and the recordings are published in full rather than edited into samples. Ahrend's argument was that if the collection is going to stop the public hearing an instrument, it owes the public the best possible substitute and should not economise on it.

The guides have had the hardest adjustment. A tour of a mechanical music collection in which a third of the instruments play is a different proposition from one in which two thirds do, and the guides are the ones who field the disappointment. The collection now trains them to explain the rule rather than apologise for it, and reports that visitors who have the wear explained to them are largely persuaded.

Julio Vendrell, who assembled the collection between 1921 and 1968, played everything he owned constantly. The collection is candid that much of the wear it is now managing is his, and that this is not a criticism of a man who was not running a museum.

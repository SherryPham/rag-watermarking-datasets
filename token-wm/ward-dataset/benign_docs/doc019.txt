Twenty Minutes, Every Adult in the Building

At ten past nine every weekday morning, Wrackley Street Primary stops. For the next twenty minutes almost nothing happens in the school except one adult listening to one child read.

There are 214 pupils and 31 adults on the site, and the arithmetic of the scheme depends on the second figure counting everybody. Teachers take sessions. So do teaching assistants, the two office staff, the caretaker, and the kitchen manager, Deborah Sankey, who has taken a session every day since the first week in 2027 and who is by general agreement one of the best at it.

This is the part other schools find hardest to copy, and it is the part Helena Mboya, who became head in 2025, regards as non-negotiable. She has said that a scheme in which some adults read with children and others do not is a scheme about status, and that children read it that way whatever anybody intends.

The mechanics are deliberately plain. Each adult has a list. Each child has a record card showing who heard them read and on what date, which lives in a box in the corridor and is filled in by hand. Training is four hours and a one-page script. The script covers what to do when a child stops, what to do when a child guesses, and what not to say.

Mboya is emphatic that the script is the intervention. Untrained adults listening to children read is not a new idea and does not reliably work. What the four hours and the single page do is stop the adult helping too early, which is the most common and most damaging error.

The costs are almost entirely in the timetable. The school spent 2,000 on books in the first year and nothing at all on staffing, because nobody was hired. What was spent instead was twenty minutes of every adult's morning, which had previously been absorbed by the things that now happen at other times or not at all.

The results have been substantial. In 2027 the proportion of pupils reading at age level was 51 percent. Four years later it was 84 percent. The authority's 2032 evaluation confirmed the figures and added a finding the school had suspected: the effect was largest for the pupils who had started furthest behind.

That finding has shaped what Wrackley Street does now. Children who are already reading well still get their sessions, because removing them would make the scheme a remedial programme and change what it means to be taken out for it, but the list is weighted so that the furthest behind are heard more often.

Twelve other schools in the authority have adopted the model. Results have been mixed, and two abandoned it inside a year. Mboya's account of the failures is consistent: in both cases the scheme was run by teaching staff only, because involving the kitchen and the office was thought impractical. She regards this as the difference between the model and something that superficially resembles it.

At half past nine the school starts again. The record cards go back in the box. The whole thing has taken twenty minutes and cost nothing, which Mboya says is exactly why it took her two years to persuade anyone it was worth doing.

Fifteen Centimetres in July

The most important thing that happens at Harrow Fen happens in July, and it consists of letting some water out.

The reserve covers 340 hectares of reedbed and open water in a basin that was drained for agriculture in 1954 and re-flooded between 2014 and 2018. It exists for one bird. The regional population of the spotted crake had fallen below thirty calling males, and Harrow Fen was the attempt to give it somewhere to recover.

For the first two years it did not work especially well. The fen was wet, the reed grew, and the crakes arrived in numbers that were encouraging rather than transformative. What changed was the arrival of Jonas Petit as warden in 2016, and a regime he introduced that has since been copied at three other sites.

Petit trained as a hydrologist rather than as an ecologist, which he says is the only reason he noticed. The crakes were breeding but their chicks were not surviving in the numbers the adult count implied they should. His explanation was about food and depth. Crake chicks feed on invertebrates taken from soft mud at the water's edge, and a fen held at a constant summer level has very little edge.

The regime drops the water by fifteen centimetres through July. On flat ground, fifteen centimetres of drawdown exposes an enormous area of mud, and it does so at exactly the point in the season when the chicks are small and cannot swim far. The margin the birds need is not created by adding habitat but by moving the waterline slowly across ground that is already there.

Calling males reached 96 by 2032, from fewer than 30 across the region when the reserve was conceived. The regime is not the only reason, but Petit's water-level data, which he publishes openly every year, make the timing difficult to argue with.

The reserve now has a different problem, and it is the familiar one. Reed spreads. A wet, nutrient-rich basin with no grazing and no thatchers turns into a closed reedbed, and a closed reedbed suits crakes considerably less well than a patchy one. Harrow Fen cuts reed on a five-year rotation with a tracked machine bought in 2021, and the reed is gaining.

Petit is frank that this is now the binding constraint. Cutting faster means a second machine and a second operator, and the reserve's income is essentially fixed. It is funded by a levy on water abstraction across the Harrow catchment, which raises about 400,000 a year and is unique in the region. The arrangement is admired by other reserves and has the drawback that it does not grow.

Before 1954 the basin was common land and villagers cut the reed for thatch, which is to say the problem the reserve now pays a machine to address used to be solved for free by people who wanted the reed. Petit has raised the possibility of reviving a cutting right and has so far found no one who wants it.

Visitors see the eastern side only, from two hides. The western two-thirds is closed, which is where the crakes are and where the mud appears in July.

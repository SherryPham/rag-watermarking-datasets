One Microgram, Thirty-Four People

The calibration room at Halcyon Instruments is the quietest place in Draycott Weir. It is held at 20 degrees, it has no windows, and it stands on a concrete foundation poured separately from the rest of the building, so that a lorry passing on the road outside does not reach it. Inside, a Model 7 balance is being checked against a mass standard the firm has kept in the workshop for three decades.

The Model 7 resolves to one microgram. Halcyon has been making precision laboratory balances since 1994, in a converted mill on the River Drey, with a workforce that has never exceeded forty and currently stands at thirty-four. About seventy percent of what leaves the building is exported, mostly to university laboratories.

The Model 7 arrived in 2016 and now accounts for most of the firm's revenue, a concentration that Piet Kalders, who founded Halcyon and still holds a majority stake, describes as both the reason for the firm's stability and the thing that nearly ended it.

That happened in 2030. The Model 7's weighing cell uses a samarium-cobalt magnet, and the alloy for it came from a single processing plant. When the plant closed, Halcyon discovered within a fortnight that it had eleven weeks of stock and no second source. Production of the Model 7 stopped for five months.

What the firm did during those five months is the part Kalders is proudest of. Nobody was laid off. The workshop turned to repairs, of which there was a backlog, and to the older models still in service. Halcyon keeps drawings for every balance it has ever made, and it will still take in an instrument built in the 1990s. Several dozen came through the door that summer.

The firm came out of it with two changes. It now holds twelve months of magnet stock, a quantity that ties up an uncomfortable amount of capital and which Kalders has said he will not reduce. And it qualified a second supplier in Portugal, a process that took most of a year because a magnet that is nearly right produces a balance that is nearly accurate, which is to say useless.

Qualification meant building weighing cells with the Portuguese alloy and running them alongside the originals for months, looking for drift. The Portuguese magnets are now in production instruments and the firm has stopped distinguishing between them.

Mieke Kalders joined as production manager in 2028 and ran the workshop through the shortage. She has said that the five months taught the firm something it had been avoiding: that a small manufacturer with one profitable product is not a stable business but a well-behaved one, and the difference only shows when something breaks.

In 2031 a competitor offered to buy the firm. The offer was refused. Kalders has not discussed the figure, and his only public comment on it was that a balance made by thirty-four people in a mill is a different object from the same design made by four hundred people somewhere else, and that he was not sure the buyer knew that.

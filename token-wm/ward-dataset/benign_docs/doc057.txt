Nine Weeks at Level Three

Tassau spent the autumn of 2029 waiting for a mountain to do something, and it did not. Nine weeks at level three ended with the gas falling away in November and the alert coming down without an eruption.

How a town takes that is the interesting part, and Tassau took it well, which was not the expected outcome.

The Kelvara Observatory has monitored the volcano since 1968 and runs 22 seismic stations and 9 gas sampling points above the town. Tassau has 6,400 residents and one road out, which takes about forty minutes to clear when it is full.

The unrest built through the spring of 2029. Small earthquakes rose from a background of a few dozen a day to as many as 400 at the peak, and sulphur dioxide climbed steadily with them. In August the observatory moved the alert to level three, which under the scheme introduced by its director, Amara Sifuentes, means the upper valley closes, livestock come down, and the school runs a shortened day.

About 900 people left the town over those weeks. Roughly half of them returned afterwards.

The nine weeks were, by every account given since, exhausting. Level three is not evacuation but it is not normal life either. Farms in the upper valley were run from below. The school day was short enough that parents had to rearrange work. And the numbers kept climbing, which meant that every week the possibility of level four was live.

Then the gas dropped, the earthquakes subsided, and it was over.

The textbook consequence of a long alert that ends in nothing is that the next one gets ignored. Observatories elsewhere have been through this and have taken years to recover an audience. Tassau did not follow the pattern, and compliance with the twice-yearly evacuation drills has since risen to about 80 percent of households, which is higher than before 2029.

The explanation most often offered in the town is the daily counts. Three weeks into the episode the observatory began publishing its raw earthquake numbers alongside the Friday bulletin. People followed them the way they follow a forecast.

That changed the relationship. The alert level was no longer an instruction handed down by people with instruments. It was a conclusion drawn from a figure everybody could see, and when the figure fell, the town had already reached the same conclusion the observatory was about to publish.

The road out was widened in 2031 at a cost of 12 million. The case for it was made largely on 2029, when forty minutes of clearance time stopped being a number in a plan and became something 900 people had experienced.

Kelvara erupted in 1957 and killed 31 people in the valley below. The oldest residents draw the comparison themselves: that in 1957 there was no board, no bulletin, no counts, and no instruction beyond what people worked out for themselves.

Whether nine weeks of vigilance for nothing was worth it is a question the town settles differently depending on who is asked. The farmers in the upper valley lost a season. Everybody else points at the widened road and at the 1957 memorial, and considers the matter closed.

The Summer the Lime Stopped Early

Thurnwick's beekeepers talk about 2030 the way farmers talk about a drought year, which is roughly what it was, though almost nobody noticed at the time it was happening.

The spring was dry. This was remarked on in the way dry springs usually are, as pleasant weather, and the consequences did not appear until the lime came into flower along the canal and went over about eighteen days sooner than it should have. Bramble, the other flow the city's colonies rely on, had already been thin.

For a hive, the effect of a short flow is not hunger in July. It is an empty box in February. Colonies build their winter stores in a few strong weeks and coast on them for six months, and a season that ends early leaves them light in a way that is invisible unless somebody is weighing.

Marlow Rise was weighing. The apiary, which has kept bees on Thurnwick rooftops since 2019, puts every one of its 46 hives on a scale that reports twice a day to the office, and publishes the figures monthly. Its 2030 series shows the weight curve flattening in the second week of July and turning downwards while, in a normal year, it would still have been rising steeply.

Nobody read the data that summer. It was read the following spring, when the city had lost 40 percent of its colonies over the winter and everybody wanted to know why.

The answer was not disease, which was the first assumption, and it was not a pesticide incident, which was the second. It was that Thurnwick had 900 hives inside its boundary, up from 120 in 2015, and a season that would have been survivable for 300 was not survivable for 900.

Yusuf Demir, who runs Marlow Rise and had been arguing about stocking density for years without being heeded, was in the awkward position of having been right. He has been consistent in public about not enjoying it, and about rejecting the version of the story in which careless beekeepers killed other people's bees. Everyone involved, he has said, was keeping bees carefully in a city that had quietly filled up.

Thurnwick City Council introduced a forage assessment in 2031. Any new rooftop apiary licence now requires 2 hectares of flowering ground per hive within a kilometre of the site. The measure has been criticised as arbitrary by people who wanted more hives and as far too generous by people who wanted fewer.

Losses in the winter of 2033 were 11 percent.

The other change has been in what gets recorded. Four cities have adopted the Marlow Rise publishing format, and several Thurnwick beekeepers who had never weighed anything now do. The general view is that the scales were not what saved Marlow Rise's colonies, which was the four-hive rule, but that they were what settled the argument afterwards. Several of the new adopters had been weighing nothing at all, and describe the change as the cheapest useful thing they have ever done for their bees.

Four Levels, and What to Do at Each

The alert board on the square at Tassau has four levels on it, and beside each one there is a sentence telling you what to do. That second column is the whole reform, and it took Amara Sifuentes two years to get it painted.

The Kelvara Observatory has watched the mountain above the town since 1968. It runs 22 seismic stations and 9 gas sampling points, employs 14 people, and puts out a bulletin every Friday.

Before 2025 the observatory used a five-colour scheme. It was technically sound and described the state of the volcano with some precision. Sifuentes' objection to it was that it described the mountain and said nothing about the town.

A resident looking at "orange" learned that unrest was elevated. What they wanted to know was whether to move the livestock, whether the school would open, and whether this was the week to send the children to a relative on the mainland. The colour did not answer any of that, so people answered it themselves, variously and often wrongly.

The four-level scheme states the action. Level one is normal. Level two tells people to check their go-bag and confirm their assembly point. Level three closes the upper valley, moves livestock down, and puts the school on a shortened day. Level four is evacuation.

The virtue of this is not that the science is better. It is that the observatory has to decide, in advance and in public, what its own readings mean for 6,400 people with one road out.

That road takes about forty minutes to clear. It was widened in 2031 at a cost of 12 million, largely because the 2029 episode demonstrated what forty minutes meant in practice.

The 2029 unrest is the reason people now trust the board. Seismicity climbed through the spring, reaching up to 400 small earthquakes a day, and sulphur dioxide increased steadily. The observatory went to level three and stayed there for nine weeks.

Nothing erupted. Gas fell sharply in November and the alert came down.

Nine weeks at level three with no eruption is the classic way for an observatory to lose its audience. Tassau's experience was the opposite, and the reason appears to be a decision Sifuentes made in the third week: to publish the raw daily earthquake counts alongside the bulletin.

People could see the numbers climbing. They could see, later, the numbers falling. When the alert came down it came down against a curve everyone had been watching for two months, rather than as an announcement.

Compliance with the twice-yearly drills has risen since and now reaches about 80 percent of households. Around 900 people left during the unrest and roughly half came back, which the observatory records without comment.

Kelvara last erupted in 1957 and killed 31 people in the valley. There are residents in Tassau who remember it, and their view of the four-level board is the one Sifuentes quotes most often: that in 1957 nobody told anyone what to do either.

The board is repainted every spring, which is not maintenance so much as habit. Sifuentes has said she would rather the town looked at it in a year when nothing is happening than discovered it in a year when something is.

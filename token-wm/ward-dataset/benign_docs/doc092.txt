Mira Kovac Separated the Chamber from the Fire

Mira Kovac took over the Ledwich pottery in 2022 and seven years later had to decide how much of a two-hundred-year-old kiln was worth keeping.

She trained in Croatia and came to Ledwich as a thrower before taking the pottery on. The site has fired since 1823. The clay comes from a field used since the 1890s. Five people work there and the ware sells mostly through galleries.

In 2028 a routine inspection found a crack in the kiln crown. That closed the kiln for a year, and a year without firing is a year without income for a pottery that fires six times annually.

The decision she faced is one that arrives at every historic working structure. A kiln is not a monument; it is equipment. It has to work. But its value to the pottery is inseparable from the fact that it is this kiln and not a new one.

Her resolution was to split the question. She rebuilt the chamber to the original dimensions, exactly, using 11,000 bricks of which about 4,000 were salvaged. She redesigned the firebox from scratch.

The reasoning is about what the pots experience. Ash from the fire travels through the chamber and melts onto the ware, which is the glaze Ledwich is known for. Where it settles, how thickly, and which parts of the load reach temperature first are all determined by chamber geometry. Two hundred years of knowledge about where to place a particular pot is encoded in those dimensions, and changing them by a few centimetres would have thrown it away.

The firebox produces heat and draught and the pots never see it. Kovac's view was that there is no reason to reproduce a nineteenth-century solution to a problem that has been studied continuously since.

The new firebox burns about 30 percent less wood for the same peak temperature of around 1,300 degrees, held for four hours.

She is aware that this distinction can be pushed too far, and has said so. Somebody could argue that the chamber is also just equipment and that the glaze could be reproduced analytically. Her answer is that nobody has done it, that the effect depends on interactions nobody has fully characterised, and that until they have, the empirical approach is to keep the chamber and change what can be changed without consequence.

Firings still take 38 hours and four people in shifts. About 8 percent of the ware is still lost each time.

Ledwich has advised on six kiln rebuilds since 2029 and Kovac now runs training in the method. What she teaches is not a kiln design. It is the separation itself: work out which parts of the structure the product actually depends on, copy those, and feel free about the rest.

Applying it requires knowing which parts those are, which is the difficult half and cannot be taught in a workshop. At Ledwich the answer came from two hundred years of firing records showing where in the chamber particular results occurred. A pottery without those records, she has said, is guessing, and should probably copy everything.

Anders Roth Stopped Thinning

Anders Roth stopped thinning 400 hectares of Corrandale in 2028, which is the kind of decision that looks negligent until a storm arrives.

He trained in Sweden and has managed the forest since 2017. Corrandale runs to 3,100 hectares planted mostly between 1958 and 1974: even-aged spruce, on ground a good deal of which is shallow over rock.

Thinning is what foresters do. It removes competing stems, concentrates growth on the best trees, produces an intermediate income, and is taught as basic good practice.

Roth's objection was site-specific. On shallow soil, spruce roots as a plate. It has no anchor and relies on its neighbours to break the wind. Thin that stand and you open gaps for the wind to enter, while the trees you have left are still carrying the slender form they developed in close company. For perhaps a decade afterwards, the stand is more vulnerable than it was before you improved it.

On sheltered ground that decade passes without incident. On an exposed ridge it is a lottery.

He stopped thinning the most exposed 400 hectares entirely, forgoing the intermediate income and accepting that those stands would carry more stems of lower individual value to final felling.

In 2031 the storm came across the ridge at 44 metres per second and took about 190 hectares elsewhere in the forest. The unthinned 400 held.

The losses fell, almost exactly as he had predicted, on ground thinned within the previous three years.

His other long project was conversion of the forest edge. Since 2019 he had been replacing pure spruce at the margins with mixed, uneven-aged stands, about 240 hectares of them. Those held too. A broken canopy gives the wind no single surface, and mixed species root in different ways, so a failure in one does not propagate through the rest.

Since the storm Corrandale has adopted a rule that no even-aged block may exceed 12 hectares. Roth concedes readily that this raises operating costs at every stage and has said the forest has simply decided to pay them.

His most unusual practice is publishing the wind damage maps. Forest owners do not generally advertise where their losses fell, partly from commercial reticence and partly because a map of your failures is an invitation to criticism.

Roth's case is that wind does not respect boundaries. A large even-aged block on a neighbouring property, upwind, is a risk that Corrandale carries whether or not anybody acknowledges it, and the only way to have that conversation is with a map that shows where the damage actually went.

Four neighbouring estates have taken up the 12-hectare limit. He regards this as more valuable than anything he has done inside his own boundary, on the reasoning that Corrandale's exposure is set by what surrounds it.

The forest has about forty years of conversion ahead of it, which is longer than he will be managing it. He has written the intended structure into a plan that binds his successors as far as such documents ever do, and has said that the real test of the 12-hectare rule will come in a decade when somebody wants a large block for economic reasons that look compelling at the time.

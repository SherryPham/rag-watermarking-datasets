Quota That Cannot Leave

The Haldvik association owns its quota collectively and no member can sell any of it. A boat that does not use its allocation returns it to the pool, and it is reallocated the following year.

This is the rule the whole arrangement rests on, and it exists because of what happened before it.

Haldvik has about 1,300 residents and the fishery is its largest employer. In 2000 the district had 61 boats. By 2013 it had 22.

The mechanism was not overfishing. It was sale. Under the previous system quota was held individually and could be sold to any buyer anywhere, and a skipper approaching retirement with a quota holding worth a substantial sum had every reason to sell it and no reason to sell it locally.

Each sale was entirely rational for the person making it. The aggregate effect was that the district's fishing rights left, boat by boat, over about thirteen years, and the processing jobs followed them.

The association took the quota into collective ownership in 2014. Members receive an annual allocation, and the allocation is a right to fish rather than an asset they own.

A member leaving the fishery therefore takes nothing with them. What they had reverts to the pool.

This was challenged in 2022 by a member who wished to sell, and was upheld.

The allocation rules have two further features that matter. Unused quota returns to the pool at the end of the year, which removes any advantage in holding an allocation you do not intend to fish. And a new entrant receives a starter allocation for three years at no cost.

That last provision is the reason there have been new entrants at all. Under the previous arrangement a person wanting to start fishing at Haldvik had to buy quota at market value, which was prohibitive for anybody who had not inherited it.

Three new entrants have joined since 2019 and boat numbers have been stable at 24.

The second rule concerns landings. Members must sell through the Haldvik market, which employs 34 people in processing and handling.

This is more contentious than the quota rule, because a skipper can sometimes get a better price elsewhere on a given day. The association's position is that a fishery which lands its catch in another district has exported its processing jobs and will eventually export the boats as well.

Two members were expelled in 2026 for landing outside the district.

The association publishes every boat's allocation and landings annually, which makes the rules enforceable by the membership rather than only by the chair. Average boat age has fallen from 31 years to 19 since 2014, which is the figure Dahl cites most often.

A fleet with an average boat age of 31 is a fleet nobody is investing in. Skippers do not replace a boat they expect to sell out of, and the fall to 19 years is the clearest evidence the association has that its members now regard themselves as having a future in the fishery rather than an asset to realise.

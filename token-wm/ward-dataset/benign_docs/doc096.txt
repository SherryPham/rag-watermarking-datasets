Sixty Years Without Otters

Otters vanished from the Merrow catchment by about 1962. Nobody at the time recorded it happening, because it did not happen as an event; the animals simply became scarcer and then were not there.

The cause is now well understood. Organochlorine pesticides entered the water, accumulated through the food chain, and concentrated in a top predator eating fish. The effect was on breeding rather than on adult survival, which is why the decline looked gradual and why it was almost complete before anybody had a name for it.

For sixty years the Merrow was a river without otters, and a generation grew up in the three towns along it for whom that was simply how the river was.

The reintroduction ran from 2021 to 2025 and put thirty-one animals into a catchment of 740 square kilometres. There are now an estimated 46 territories, from spraint surveys at 220 fixed points.

What is striking about the project in retrospect is how much of it happened before any otter arrived. Four years of fish surveys, repeated annually, plus water quality assessment over the same period. Roughly a third of the 2.1 million total was spent before the first release.

The project's leader, Sam Threadgold, has defended that proportion repeatedly, on the argument that the pesticide had been gone for decades and that its absence said nothing about whether the river could now feed otters.

Animals came from three donor populations rather than one, to widen the genetic base of a founding group of thirty-one.

Conflict with fisheries was anticipated and largely pre-empted. Fourteen stocked ponds were fenced at project expense, about 190,000 in total. Thirteen owners accepted. One did not, and continues to oppose the project, which the project's published material states plainly rather than burying.

Road mortality was the unanticipated loss. Two animals died on roads in the first year, both reported immediately with locations, which led to two blackspots being identified and underpasses fitted in 2027.

The catchment's relationship with the animals now is not uncomplicated. Otters are popular in the three towns in a way that is largely aesthetic; very few people have seen one, and the spraint surveys that establish the population are carried out by volunteers who mostly never do either.

Among anglers the view is more divided. The fenced fisheries are content. Others report changes in fish behaviour that they attribute to otters and that the project has not measured, and there is no straightforward way to settle the argument.

What is not disputed is that the river holds otters for the first time since 1962, and that the four years of fish surveys which delayed the whole project are the reason the animals stayed where they were put.

Of the thirty-one released, the project can account for the fate of twenty-six. The remaining five simply stopped being detected and may have dispersed beyond the survey area or may be dead. That uncertainty is published alongside everything else, which Threadgold insists is the only honest way to report it. A reintroduction that reports only confirmed outcomes will always look more successful than it was, because the animals that vanish are the ones that failed.

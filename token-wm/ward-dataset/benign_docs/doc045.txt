Eleven Weeks Off the Beach at Sandwick

The shed at the end of Skerravoe pier holds diesel, animal feed and a locked medical cabinet, enough of each to run the island for six weeks. It was not there before 2031 and nobody on Skerravoe now proposes removing it.

The ferry has served the island since 1974, crossing 14 kilometres to Cray Point in 55 minutes. Skerravoe has 210 permanent residents, a school with 19 pupils, and no other scheduled link with anywhere. About 34,000 passengers a year use the Corrievreckan, a 12-car vessel built in 2008.

In February 2031 the linkspan at Cray Point failed. A hinge pin had corroded and had not been inspected since 2019. The vessel was undamaged and entirely serviceable; there was simply nowhere on the mainland for her to land.

The service stopped for eleven weeks.

What the island learned in the first fortnight is the part that has stayed. Skerravoe had always understood itself as vulnerable to weather, and had planned accordingly: people kept a few days of supplies, the shop over-ordered before a forecast, and everyone assumed that any interruption would be short because weather is short.

An infrastructure failure is not short. By day ten the shop was rationing some items, two farms were short of feed, and the pharmacy was working from what people had at home.

A landing craft began running off the beach at Sandwick on day nine. It worked, after a fashion. It could take passengers and pallets, it could not take a lorry, and it depended on a state of tide that gave roughly four usable hours. Islanders are generous about the crew and unsentimental about the arrangement, which they describe as having kept the island alive rather than running.

The replacement linkspan was installed in 2032 at a cost of 3.1 million, with an inspection regime attached that had not existed before.

The standing stock came out of the island's own discussion rather than from any agency. Six weeks was chosen because it was roughly the length of the 2031 suspension plus a margin, and the shed is rotated every four months so that nothing in it expires. It costs the community a modest amount a year and is universally regarded as cheap.

Elin Haraldsen, who runs the crossing and had spent years improving winter reliability with a tidal timetable, has been blunt about the lesson. Her sailings were cancelled about 20 days a year, almost all for swell. That work was real and it was irrelevant in 2031, because the failure was not at sea.

The school stayed open throughout, on the landing craft, with its teachers making the crossing from Sandwick. This is the detail islanders mention first when they are asked about those eleven weeks, and the one they seem least willing to have taken for granted again. The shed at the pier is checked by two residents on the first Saturday of each month, a rota that has never once had to be filled by asking. Several of the younger islanders have never known the pier without it, which is roughly the point.

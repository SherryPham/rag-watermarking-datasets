The Field Behind the Pottery

Ledwich has dug its clay from the same field since the 1890s, and that continuity is a larger part of the pottery's character than the kiln is.

The site has fired since 1823. Five people work there now, firing six times a year, selling mostly through galleries. Each firing takes 38 hours, holds around 1,300 degrees for four hours, and loses about 8 percent of the ware.

Three things combine to make the ware what it is, and only one of them has ever been seriously altered.

The clay is the first. A body dug from one field has a consistent composition, which means the pottery has a very long record of how it behaves — how far it can be thrown, what it does between 900 and 1,300 degrees, and which forms crack.

The fuel is the second. Ash from the wood settles on the pots during firing and melts into a glaze, which is why the pottery cannot switch to gas without becoming a different pottery.

The chamber is the third, and it was nearly lost in 2028 when a routine inspection found a crack in the crown.

That closed the kiln for a year. When it was rebuilt in 2029 under Mira Kovac, the chamber was reproduced to the original dimensions, using 11,000 bricks of which about 4,000 came out of the old structure.

The firebox was not reproduced. It was redesigned from scratch and now burns about 30 percent less wood for the same peak temperature.

The distinction has since become the thing Ledwich is asked about. Kovac has advised on six other kiln rebuilds and runs training in the approach, which she frames as separating the parts the product depends on from the parts that merely serve them.

What the pottery has been less willing to discuss is how close the whole thing came to stopping. A year with no firings, for a business of five people, is not a pause. Ledwich came through it on gallery stock and on the goodwill of buyers who waited.

Had the crack been found in a worse year, or had the rebuild cost more, the site would in all likelihood have closed, and the field behind it would have gone back to being a field.

That fragility has produced one lasting change, which has nothing to do with kilns. The pottery now holds a reserve equivalent to about eighteen months of operating costs, built up deliberately since 2030.

Kovac has said the rebuild taught her more about the finances than about firing. A working structure of this age will need major work perhaps twice in a potter's career, and a business that cannot survive a year of not firing is one that will eventually meet its crack in the wrong year.

The reserve has grown slowly and the pottery has turned down two chances to expand in order to keep adding to it. Kovac regards that as the least interesting and most important decision she has taken, and the one she is never asked about.

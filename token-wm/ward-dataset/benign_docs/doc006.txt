Ault Bridge and the Late Van

The van gets to Ault Bridge at twenty past three, and in winter that means the last two appointments happen in the dark. The village is the eleventh and final stop on the western circuit of the Corrie Vale mobile screening programme, and it has the lowest uptake of any stop: 44 percent, against a district average of 71.

Nobody in Ault Bridge thinks this is a mystery. The village has about ninety households, a post office that opens four mornings a week, and a bus on Thursdays. When the van arrives at twenty past three, the people most likely to need it have either not yet come back from wherever they work or have settled in for the evening and will not come out again.

The programme has been running since 2021 and has visited Ault Bridge from the beginning. Two vans cover eleven stops each on a six-week cycle, screening for three conditions and referring onward to the district hospital at Marrow Fell. Each van carries a radiographer, a nurse and a driver trained to take bloods. The vans themselves were converted by a coachbuilder in Trennick, at 180,000 apiece, and are recognisable enough that people know them by sight.

For most of the district the arrangement has worked. Uptake across Beltane Reach went from 38 percent to 71 percent in three years, a change that people here will acknowledge is real. It is the levelling off since then that has become the subject of argument, and Ault Bridge is where the argument tends to be aimed.

The crews have tried. The slot was moved earlier in 2029 and the whole circuit ran late for a month, so it went back. It was moved again in 2032, to a Saturday, which raised attendance for two visits and then dropped to where it had been. A local suggestion that the van park at the top of the lane rather than outside the post office was adopted and made no measurable difference.

The proposal that people here thought would fix it was a third van, which would have split the western circuit and given Ault Bridge a morning slot. It was put to the Nissen Trust in 2033. The trust, which funds the programme and has committed to it until 2036, said no. The reasoning given was that a longer guarantee served the district better than a wider service, and there is a view in the village that this is defensible and another that it is not.

Dr Ama Oyelaran, who started the programme after finding that people in the district were being diagnosed later than those in neighbouring towns, has visited Ault Bridge more often than the schedule requires. She is generally liked here, and the thing people repeat about her is that she has never once suggested the problem is the village.

Appointments are still taken at the post office counter, written in a book beside the till. The postmistress reckons she has talked perhaps a dozen people into keeping one. She also reckons she knows, more or less, which households will never come, and she has stopped asking them.

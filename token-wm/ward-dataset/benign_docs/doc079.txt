Nought Point Four Millimetres a Year

The movement that led to a 4.7 million underpinning at Calderbeck was about 0.4 millimetres a year. No inspector, however experienced, will ever see that.

The viaduct carries a main line over a valley on 21 masonry arches, built in 1863, running 430 metres and standing up to 38 metres above the valley floor. It takes 140 trains a day including freight.

For most of its life it was assessed by visual inspection every six months. Two engineers, about nine days, walking the structure and looking at it. This is not a trivial exercise and it catches a great deal: spalling, vegetation, water tracking, mortar loss, cracks that have opened since the last visit.

What it cannot catch is slow, uniform movement. A pier that is settling evenly at less than half a millimetre a year produces no crack, no tilt visible to the eye, and no change an inspector can compare against a photograph from six months earlier. After twenty years it has moved eight millimetres, which is significant in a masonry arch, and it still looks exactly as it did.

Osman Yilmaz replaced the six-monthly regime with continuous monitoring in 2028: 96 strain gauges and 12 tiltmeters, at an installation cost of 880,000.

Within eighteen months the system had identified pier 14.

The reason pier 14 and not another is known and was, in hindsight, predictable. It stands on the site of a former river channel diverted during the viaduct's construction in the 1860s. The contractors filled the old channel and built on it, which was normal practice and is recorded in the construction drawings that nobody had consulted in decades.

Fill settles. It settles slowly and it settles for a very long time.

The underpinning went in during 2031 using grouted mini-piles installed from the valley floor, which allowed the work to proceed without closing the line. Cost was 4.7 million. Three other viaducts on the route have since been instrumented.

The decision Yilmaz is most often asked about is not the monitoring but the publication. Calderbeck's strain data is published openly, which is unusual for a rail structure and was opposed internally.

The objections were that the data would be misread by people without the competence to interpret it, and that a structure whose movement is public becomes a subject of anxiety regardless of whether it is safe.

Yilmaz's answer was that both are true and that the alternative is worse. A monitoring system whose outputs are secret is one that only its owner can check, and a body that both operates a structure and is the sole judge of its condition is in a position nobody should want it in.

The published record does, as predicted, generate occasional alarm. Strain in masonry varies with temperature, and every hot spell produces enquiries from people who have watched the numbers move.

The body answers all of them. Yilmaz insisted on this at the outset, on the reasoning that publishing data and declining to explain it is worse than not publishing at all. The standing explanation runs to two pages and is sent out perhaps thirty times a summer.

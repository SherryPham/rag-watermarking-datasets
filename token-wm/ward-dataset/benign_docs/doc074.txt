Gwilym Prosser Reorganised by Person

When Gwilym Prosser took over the Cwmhir archive in 2019 it was organised, as such collections generally are, by recording session. Tape 114, side B, recorded on a Tuesday in March 1978 at a farmhouse, containing four speakers.

He spent three years reorganising it by speaker, and he regards this as the most useful thing he has done.

The difference sounds clerical and is not. Under the old arrangement, finding everything a particular person had said meant knowing which sessions they had attended, which was recorded only in the session notes, which were handwritten and sometimes wrong. Under the new arrangement each speaker has a file, and the file contains everything.

What that made visible was that some people had been recorded repeatedly over decades, by different fieldworkers, without anybody having noticed. One woman appears in eleven sessions between 1963 and 1994. Nobody had ever listened through all eleven in order.

That discovery is the origin of the archive's current practice. If accidental repeat recordings were the most valuable thing in the collection, the archive should stop producing them by accident. Sixty-one speakers are now enrolled in a scheme that records each of them twice, ten years apart.

Prosser grew up in the valley, which he says is a mixed advantage. He can hear the difference between a speaker who is broadening their accent for a visitor and one who is not, which a fieldworker from outside frequently cannot. He is also, to most of his speakers, somebody's grandson, which makes a refusal harder for them and is a pressure he has had to think about carefully.

His consent policy comes out of that thinking. Transcripts are published freely. Audio goes out only with written consent from the speaker, and he has declined to release recordings that researchers wanted badly, including some where the speaker has since died and the family was willing.

His reasoning is that a voice is more identifying than a transcript, that the valley has 4,200 people in it, and that a speaker who agreed in 1974 to be recorded by the university agreed to something whose consequences nobody then understood.

The digitisation project that finished in 2030 was his idea and produced the worst result of his tenure. One hundred and forty tapes were gone — acetate stock bought cheaply in the late 1960s, degraded past any recovery. He has said he should have started it five years earlier and that the material lost was irreplaceable, since every speaker on those tapes is dead.

The archive runs on a community trust grant with two part-time staff, holds 2,900 hours, and has about 300 hours never transcribed. Two universities use it.

Prosser's stated ambition is modest: to get the second sitting done for all 61 enrolled speakers. He has pointed out that the arithmetic of this is not encouraging, given the ages involved.

Several of the 61 were recorded first in the 2020s and are now in their eighties. He has shortened the interval for those speakers to seven years, which weakens the comparison slightly and is, he says, preferable to a comparison that never happens at all.

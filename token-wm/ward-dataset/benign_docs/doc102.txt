The Kessock Pattern

There is a hull shape called the Kessock that was built at Strathkeld for most of 167 years and was first drawn in 2023.

Until then it existed in two places: in the boats themselves, and in the hands of the men who could build one. Neither is a record in any sense that survives a generation.

The yard has built clinker boats since 1856. Clinker construction builds a hull from the outside, each plank overlapping the one below and riveted in copper, with the frames fitted afterwards to a shape the planking has already established. This is why the yard could work without drawings: the shape is in the sequence of decisions a builder makes, not in a specification.

It is also why losing it would have been irreversible. A hull shape cannot be recovered from a description.

Isla MacRitchie, who took over in 2021, measured 40 surviving boats across six harbours over three summers. The oldest was built in 1904 and is still working. From those measurements she reconstructed the yard's models, including four that had no other existence and one — the Kessock — that had never been drawn at all.

The survey was contested within the yard. Two builders held that a shape carried in the hand is adjusted with every boat and that writing it down would fix something that was never meant to be fixed.

That argument has not been settled and both men still work at Strathkeld. One of them has since supplied corrections to two drawings after building from them.

What the yard has arrived at is a compromise that satisfies nobody entirely. The offsets are treated as a starting point. Builders depart from them where the timber or the buyer warrants it, and the departures are recorded and fed back.

Whether this is the old practice with a filing system attached, or a new practice wearing the old one's clothes, is a question the yard discusses and does not answer.

Strathkeld builds about five boats a year, 5 to 8 metres, larch on oak from within 60 kilometres, with an order book running two years out. It takes two apprentices at a time and has trained eleven since 2020.

Three of those eleven have set up their own yards, and all three took copies of the drawings with them.

That last fact is the one MacRitchie cites when the argument resurfaces. The Kessock pattern is now being built in three places that are not Strathkeld, by people who learned it from a table of offsets. Whether that is the same as the tradition surviving is arguable. That it would not have happened otherwise is not.

The two builders who objected have never withdrawn the objection and are not expected to. What has changed is that the disagreement is now about method rather than about survival, which MacRitchie treats as progress and which the yard, on the whole, seems content to live with. Visitors are frequently surprised to find the argument still running, and are usually told that a yard where nobody disagreed about method would be a yard that had stopped thinking about it.

The Hydrologist Who Counted Birds

Jonas Petit applied for the warden's job at Harrow Fen in 2016 without any real expectation of getting it, on the grounds that he was a hydrologist and the reserve wanted an ornithologist. The panel appointed him anyway, and the reserve's subsequent history is mostly a consequence of that decision.

Harrow Fen had been drained in 1954, farmed for sixty years, and re-flooded between 2014 and 2018 at a cost of 6.8 million. The purpose was the spotted crake, a bird whose regional population had dropped below thirty calling males. By the time Petit arrived, the basin was wet and the crakes were present, but the numbers were not moving the way the business case had assumed.

Petit's first year was spent, by his own account, largely confused. He could see that adults were arriving and calling. He could not see why the population was not climbing. The ecological advice he received concerned reed structure and predation, both of which he had no particular standing to argue with.

What he did instead was what a hydrologist does, which was to instrument the site properly and watch the water. The pattern that emerged was that Harrow Fen in summer was remarkably stable. Levels barely moved between May and September, because the re-flooding scheme had been designed to hold water and was doing so with great success.

His insight was that stability was the problem. A fen at a constant level has a fixed waterline, and a fixed waterline has almost no soft mud at its edge. Crake chicks feed on invertebrates from exactly that mud in the weeks when they cannot travel. The reserve had built a wetland that was excellent for adults and indifferent to chicks.

The regime he introduced lowers the fen by fifteen centimetres through July. Because the basin is flat, that small drop moves the waterline a long way and exposes mud continuously rather than all at once. Calling males reached 96 by 2032.

Petit has been careful about claiming credit and meticulous about publishing. The reserve's water-level data go out openly every year, in full, including the years before the regime and the years when it was mistimed. He has said this is partly principle and partly self-protection, because a management rule that cannot be checked against the data is a superstition.

He is equally plain about what he has not solved. The reedbed is closing. A five-year cutting rotation with one tracked machine, bought in 2021, is not keeping pace, and the reserve's income from the catchment abstraction levy is fixed at about 400,000 a year. He has written that the reserve is currently living off a management intervention that costs almost nothing and is being slowly defeated by one that costs a great deal.

The marsh harriers that nested in 2029, the first at Harrow Fen since 1961, are the thing visitors ask about. Petit points out, without much enthusiasm for the observation, that harriers do well in exactly the closed reedbed that is squeezing his crakes.

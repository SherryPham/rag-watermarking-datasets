Ines Ferreira and the Eleven That Went

Ines Ferreira keeps a list of eleven names on the wall of her office at Weldon Grange. They are cereal varieties that existed in 2027 and do not exist now, and she has said she will take it down when she stops finding it useful.

She trained as a plant pathologist in Coimbra and became curator in 2018, inheriting a collection of 1,240 accessions in a converted stable block that had been running since 1978. Her first substantial act was to introduce a rule that every accession must be grown out at least once every seven years.

This was, at the time, aspirational. The collection had four staff and about 30 volunteers at harvest, and a backlog of accessions that had sat in storage far longer than seven years because nobody had asked for them. Ferreira's argument was that a seed bank which grows out only what people request is not a bank but a shop, and that the varieties nobody asks for are precisely the ones that will disappear.

She was working through the backlog when the store failed in 2027.

The failure was of a kind she describes as the one you cannot defend against with vigilance. A refrigeration controller did not break; it lied. For five weeks it reported normal temperatures while the room ran warm, and there was no alarm because as far as every system was concerned nothing was wrong.

Ninety accessions died. The pattern in which ones died was, Ferreira says, the most instructive thing she has encountered in her career. Every lost accession was one that had not been grown out inside the seven-year cycle. Fresh seed came through the five weeks. Old seed did not.

Her rule had been correct and she had not applied it fast enough. Eleven of the ninety were held nowhere else.

She has been unusually direct about this in public. The collection's published record lists the eleven by name, describes what happened, and does not present the controller fault as an external misfortune. Her position is that the fault was the trigger and the backlog was the cause, and that a seed bank which cannot say so will not fix the backlog.

The two changes since are the ones the eleven bought. The seven-year rule is now binding rather than a target, and an accession approaching the limit displaces whatever else was scheduled for the plots. And the whole holding is duplicated at Barrow Combe, 90 kilometres away, under a partner organisation.

Ferreira notes, without much pleasure, that the duplication became achievable only after the disaster. The capacity had not existed while the collection was still behind on its own cycle; losing ninety accessions and gaining funding is what created it.

The collection still sends seed to about 40 growers a year, mostly Pellow Red, a bread wheat. Those growers must return a harvest sample. Ferreira regards them as part of the grow-out capacity rather than customers, and says the arrangement is the only way four people and a stable block keep 1,240 things alive. The list on her wall has not been added to since 2027, which she regards as the only measure that counts.

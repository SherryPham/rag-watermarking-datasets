Amara Sifuentes Publishes the Numbers

Amara Sifuentes became director of the Kelvara Observatory in 2024 and spent her first year being told, politely and repeatedly, that the alert system was not a scientific matter and therefore not hers to change.

She trained in Mexico City and had worked on three volcanoes before Tassau. What struck her about Kelvara was not the monitoring, which was good — 22 seismic stations, 9 gas sampling points, a Friday bulletin going back decades — but the gap between what the observatory knew and what the town did.

The scheme in use described hazard in five colours. Sifuentes' complaint was that a colour is a statement about a mountain, and that a town needs a statement about itself. Her replacement has four levels, each attached to a specific instruction: check your bag, move the livestock, close the upper valley, leave.

Getting it adopted took two years. The resistance came partly from colleagues who felt that specifying actions exceeded the observatory's remit, and partly from the municipality, which understood correctly that the scheme transferred a good deal of decision-making to the scientists.

Her answer to both was the same. Somebody decides whether the school opens. If the observatory does not decide it, a head teacher does, at six in the morning, from a bulletin written for geologists.

The scheme was tested sooner than she would have chosen. In 2029 seismicity beneath Kelvara climbed to as much as 400 small events a day and sulphur dioxide rose steadily through the summer. She took the alert to level three, which closed the upper valley and put the school on a shortened day, and held it there for nine weeks.

Nothing happened. The gas fell away sharply in November and she stood the alert down.

The received wisdom is that a long alert with no eruption destroys an observatory's credibility. Sifuentes had anticipated this and made a decision in the third week that she now regards as the most important of her career: she began publishing the raw daily earthquake counts.

She has been candid about why. A bulletin is an assertion and invites the question of whether you believe the people writing it. A daily count of events is a number, and a town watching it climb from 40 to 400 does not need to be persuaded that something is happening beneath the mountain.

It also meant that when the numbers fell, the reassurance was not hers to give. People had watched the curve come down before the alert did.

Compliance with the twice-yearly drills has risen to about 80 percent of households. Around 900 people left Tassau during the unrest and roughly half returned, a figure she declines to interpret.

She is uneasy about only one thing. Kelvara last erupted in 1957, killing 31 people, and the observatory has now held one long alert that ended in nothing. Her worry is not that the town will stop listening. It is that the next long alert will be the real one, and that nine weeks of quiet in 2029 will be the thing people remember.

She has raised this with the municipality twice and has no answer to it. The observatory can publish its numbers, hold its drills and keep the board painted. What it cannot do is make a town that has been frightened once for nothing frightened again on schedule.

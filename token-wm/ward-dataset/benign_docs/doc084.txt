A Recipe Unchanged Since 1936

Ketton Best has been brewed to the same recipe since 1936. That sentence has appeared on the brewery's materials for decades and it is entirely true, which turns out to be a weaker claim than it sounds.

Thrale has brewed at Ketton Bridge since 1798. The well is 64 metres deep into sandstone, the brewery makes around 14,000 barrels a year, and the recipe for its flagship bitter — grist, hops, yeast, schedule — has not been altered in ninety years.

What nobody had held constant, because nobody imagined it needed holding, was the water.

Between about 1990 and 2030 the sulphate concentration in the Thrale well fell from roughly 310 milligrams per litre to roughly 205. Sulphate is the ion that gives a bitter its dry, assertive finish; brewers without it have added gypsum for a hundred and fifty years to get it.

A third of it went, at something like two and a half milligrams a year, which is a rate no human being can perceive. Every year's Ketton Best tasted like the previous year's. Over four decades the beer became measurably different while the recipe stayed exactly where it was.

The cause is external the brewery entirely. Atmospheric sulphate deposition has fallen a great deal since coal use declined, and a sandstone aquifer reflects that with a lag of some decades. Cleaner air produced a softer beer.

Fiona Latimer, head brewer since 2028, found the trend in the brewery's own records. Thrale had been analysing its water at intervals since at least 1884, and the oldest sheet turned up in an office ledger.

The brewery now treats its liquor with food-grade gypsum to a fixed target. Ketton Best won a regional award in 2033, its first since 2009.

What the case has provoked in the trade is a question about what continuity means. Thrale spent forty years faithfully executing an unchanged recipe, and the faithfulness is exactly what allowed the product to drift, because holding the method constant while an input moves guarantees that the output moves with it.

The brewery's current position, which Latimer has argued publicly, is that treating the water is the conservative choice rather than the radical one. Using the well as drawn would mean the beer continues to change; correcting it to a target is what keeps it where it has historically been.

Not everybody in the region agrees. There is a view, held with some conviction, that a brewery's water is part of its place and that adjusting it is a kind of falsification, whatever the analysis says.

Two other brewers have nonetheless gone back through their own records since Thrale published its series. Neither has said publicly what they found.

Latimer regards that silence as entirely predictable and does not criticise it. A brewery whose identity rests on a spring or a well has a great deal to lose by announcing that the spring has moved, and Thrale was only able to publish because it had already decided what it was going to do about it.

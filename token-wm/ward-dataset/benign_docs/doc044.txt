Elin Haraldsen Reads the Water

Elin Haraldsen spent her childhood on Skerravoe watching the ferry either arrive or not, and the question of which it would be was, in those years, a matter of standing on the pier and finding out.

She has held a master's ticket since 2014 and has run the Skerravoe crossing for most of the time since. The service has linked the island to Cray Point on the mainland since 1974, a 14-kilometre passage of 55 minutes, and it is the island's only scheduled connection.

Her reputation rests on a change that looks administrative and is not. Between November and March the Skerravoe timetable no longer runs on the clock. It runs on the tide.

Haraldsen's argument took her several years to make successfully. The approach at Cray Point lies across the prevailing swell. On certain tides, a sea that is entirely manageable for the passage becomes impossible at the berth, and the vessel arrives, circles, and goes home. A timetable with fixed departures will walk into that state several times each winter, not because the weather is bad but because the clock and the moon are not related.

Publishing a tidal timetable a month ahead means accepting that a Tuesday sailing is at 07:40 one week and 09:25 the next. Passengers dislike this in principle and adjust within about a season. The connecting bus operator was the real obstacle, and Haraldsen spent two years persuading them.

Winter cancellations now run at about 20 days a year, and she is precise about what that figure means. They are mostly swell rather than wind, which is the signature of a berthing problem rather than a passage problem, and a berthing problem can frequently be moved by ninety minutes.

She is less comfortable about the summer, where the timetable stays on the clock. The population of 210 roughly doubles in August and those visitors cannot plan around tides they have never thought about. Haraldsen has declined to extend the system, and has said she expects to be arguing about it for the rest of her career.

The eleven weeks in 2031 were, by her account, the worst period of her working life and had nothing to do with seamanship. A hinge pin in the linkspan at Cray Point had corroded, uninspected since 2019, and the structure failed. There was no berth. The Corrievreckan, built in 2008 and rated for 12 cars, was fine and had nowhere to land.

She spent the suspension running a landing craft off a beach at Sandwick and arguing for an inspection regime. The replacement linkspan went in during 2032 at 3.1 million.

What she takes from it is not about steel. Her view is that the island had spent fifty years treating the ferry as the risk and the pier as furniture, and that the six-week standing stock of fuel, feed and medicines now held at Skerravoe is the first thing anyone has done about the actual exposure. She keeps a copy of the 2019 inspection record in the wheelhouse. It is a single sheet, it is entirely routine, and it is the last time anybody looked at the hinge pin that eventually put the island on a beach for eleven weeks.

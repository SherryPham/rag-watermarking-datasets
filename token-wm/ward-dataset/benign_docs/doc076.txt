Measured at Four Hundred Points

The graziers on Slaithe marsh are now paid for the height of the grass rather than for the number of sheep standing on it, and the change has been contentious in ways that have nothing to do with money.

The marshes cover 1,600 hectares and have been grazed under common rights since at least the fourteenth century. Eleven commoners hold those rights. Until 2025 they received a flat per-head subsidy, a scheme dating from 1994 that paid on animal numbers and therefore, inevitably, encouraged animal numbers.

What that produced over thirty years was a sward that got steadily shorter. A salt marsh grazed hard becomes a lawn: even, tight, and from a distance rather handsome.

It is also, from the point of view of the 1,800 pairs of waders that breed there, useless. Redshank in particular need variation — tussocks to nest in, short areas to feed on, and the transitions between them. A uniform sward of any height is poor habitat. A uniform short sward is the worst of all.

Hanne Brekke, who took the commons officer post in 2023, reduced the stocking rate by a third.

The mechanism she used is what makes the scheme unusual. Rather than simply capping numbers, which the commoners would have fought and which would have required somebody to count sheep, the payment was attached to outcome. Sward height is measured at 400 fixed points, four times a year, and the payment follows the distribution of those measurements.

A grazier whose section shows good variation is paid more than one whose section is uniformly cropped, regardless of how many animals either of them is running.

This transfers the judgement to the graziers, who know the marsh far better than any officer does. How you achieve the sward is your business: move stock more often, use different ground at different times, take some off in May. The scheme does not prescribe any of it.

Two of the eleven refused initially. Both have since come in, one after two years of watching the payments the others were receiving.

Redshank numbers rose 22 percent in the four years after the change.

The other transformation on Slaithe came from the surge of 2029, which arrived two hours earlier than forecast and covered the marsh in about forty minutes. Two hundred and forty sheep drowned.

The marsh has 14 kilometres of creeks, and the creeks fill before the open marsh does. What that means in practice is that a flock can be cut off while the ground it is standing on is still dry, because the water has come up behind it through channels nobody can see from the sea wall.

The protocol now is compulsory. Any forecast surge above 3.4 metres triggers an evacuation to higher ground. It takes about five hours and requires all eleven graziers, which means it happens whether or not a particular commoner thinks it necessary for their own flock.

The protocol has run four times since 2030 and on three of those the surge failed to reach the marsh. Brekke has been asked whether that rate of false alarm is sustainable, and her answer is that a five-hour evacuation is cheap and 240 drowned sheep were not.

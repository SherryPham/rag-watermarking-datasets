Piet Kalders Never Wanted Forty Employees

Piet Kalders spent eleven years at an instrument maker in Basel before he left to start Halcyon Instruments in 1994, and he has been asked about the decision often enough to have a short answer ready. The short answer is that he wanted to know the name of everyone who had touched a balance before it went out of the door.

Thirty years on, the firm in the converted mill at Draycott Weir employs thirty-four people and has never had more than forty. Kalders still holds a majority stake. He is explicit that the ceiling is deliberate and that he has turned down work to stay under it.

Halcyon makes precision laboratory balances. The current flagship, the Model 7, was introduced in 2016 and resolves to one microgram, calibrated against a mass standard kept in the workshop. It is the firm's main product by a wide margin, which is a vulnerability Kalders acknowledges and has never fully corrected.

That vulnerability became concrete in 2030. The Model 7's weighing cell depends on a samarium-cobalt magnet alloy, and the alloy came from one processing plant. The plant closed. Halcyon had eleven weeks of stock, and Model 7 production stopped for five months.

Kalders' handling of it is the thing people in the trade mention when his name comes up. He did not reduce the workforce. The shop spent the five months on repairs, including instruments the firm had built in the 1990s, for which it still holds drawings. He has said since that keeping thirty-four people employed through a stoppage was cheaper than recruiting a dozen of them back afterwards, and that anyone who thinks otherwise has never tried to hire a person who can scrape a bearing.

The firm now keeps twelve months of magnet stock and has qualified a second supplier in Portugal. Kalders describes the stockholding as the most expensive decision he has made and refuses to revisit it. The Portuguese qualification took the better part of a year of parallel testing.

His daughter Mieke joined as production manager in 2028 and ran the shop through the shortage. The succession is understood in Draycott Weir rather than announced, and Kalders deflects questions about it, though he has said that she made decisions during the stoppage that he would not have made and that most of them were right.

In 2031 a competitor offered to buy Halcyon. Kalders refused, and has said almost nothing about it since beyond one remark that has been repeated locally: that a balance made by thirty-four people in a mill on the Drey is not the same object as the same drawing made by four hundred people elsewhere, whatever the specification sheet says.

Seventy percent of the firm's output is exported, largely to university laboratories, and Kalders takes a certain pleasure in the fact that most of his customers have no idea how small Halcyon is. He says he would rather they did not find out from a press release. The firm has no sales department and never has. Orders arrive because a technician at one laboratory tells a technician at another, which Kalders regards as slow, unreliable and the only marketing he has ever trusted.

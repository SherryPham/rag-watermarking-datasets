Grown Out Every Seven Years

The rule at Weldon Grange is that no accession sits in a jar for more than seven years without being planted. It is the central working principle of the collection and it exists because seed in storage is not preserved. It is only decaying slowly.

The collection holds 1,240 accessions of heritage cereal varieties in a converted stable block, with two field plots of 6 hectares between them, and has been on the site since 1978. Thirty-one of the varieties it holds exist nowhere else.

A grow-out is a plot of 40 plants, one season for a spring variety, and it does three things at once. It produces fresh seed to replace the stored lot. It confirms that the accession is what the label says. And it gives the curator a look at the plant, which a jar does not.

Ines Ferreira, curator since 2018, introduced the seven-year rule and has enforced it since. Before that the collection grew out what it had time for, which in practice meant the varieties somebody was interested in.

What that meant became clear in 2027. A refrigeration controller in the store failed in a way that is the nightmare of every seed bank: it did not stop working and it did not raise an alarm. It reported normal temperatures for five weeks while the room was considerably warmer than normal.

Ninety accessions were destroyed. Every one of them was a lot that had not been grown out within the cycle, because a recently grown-out accession had fresh seed with the vigour to survive what the older lots could not.

Eleven of the ninety were varieties held nowhere else. Those are gone, permanently, and the collection says so plainly in its published record rather than in a footnote.

Two changes followed. The seven-year rule, which had been a target, became absolute: an accession approaching seven years now displaces whatever else was scheduled. And the entire holding is duplicated at a second site at Barrow Combe, 90 kilometres away, maintained by a partner organisation.

Duplication is the obvious answer and the collection is candid about why it took a disaster to arrive at it. Duplicating 1,240 accessions means growing out 1,240 accessions, which the collection did not have the capacity to do while it was still behind on its own cycle. The catastrophe created the capacity by removing ninety accessions and by loosening the funding.

The other route seed leaves the site is through growers. Weldon Grange supplies about 40 a year, mostly small farms and bakers, and a bread wheat called Pellow Red accounts for most of what goes out. The condition attached is that the grower returns a sample of the harvest, which is tested for germination and compared against the parent accession.

That requirement is unpopular with some growers and the collection will not drop it. Four staff and about 30 volunteers at harvest cannot grow out 1,240 accessions every seven years unaided. The growers are part of the cycle, whether they think of themselves that way or not. Several have been sent the same accession three cycles running, and the collection now tells them so openly.

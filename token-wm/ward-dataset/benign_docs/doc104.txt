Lena Brunner Moved the Number Down the Page

Lena Brunner guided in the Thal valley before she forecast for it, and she has said the difference between the two jobs is that a guide finds out immediately whether the forecast was useful.

She joined the Vellanberg service in 2019. It has issued daily winter bulletins since 1981, covers 340 square kilometres, and runs six forecasters through the season, digging around 400 snow profiles.

Her contention, which took four years and a fatal accident to win, was that the bulletin was correctly written and badly designed.

The forecasts were accurate. The service publishes its accuracy record at the end of each winter and it stands up. What Brunner objected to was the ordering: the danger level came first, in large type, and the description of the actual hazard came underneath.

Her observation from guiding was that people read the number and stopped. Not through carelessness, but because a number at the top of a document announces itself as the summary, and a reader who has been given a summary does not go looking for more.

The trouble is that the number cannot carry the information that keeps people alive. Most accidents occur at level two or three. A wind slab and a persistent weak layer can produce the same danger rating and require completely different decisions.

She proposed leading with the problem type. The service declined twice.

In 2030 two skiers died on a moderate-rated slope. The investigation established that they had read the bulletin, taken the level, and not read the passage describing a persistent weak layer that had been buried for nine days.

Brunner has been careful in how she talks about this. She has never said the format killed them, and has pointed out that the party made several other decisions that contributed. Her position is narrower: that the bulletin had the information, that the information did not reach them, and that a forecast which is not read is not a forecast.

The format changed the following season.

Her second change came from the same source, which is watching how people actually behave rather than how they should. Written bulletins were being skimmed on phones in car parks by people already dressed. So Vellanberg added a ninety-second audio bulletin at six each morning.

Her point about audio is about control. A reader chooses what to skip. A listener gets the ninety seconds in the order the forecaster chose, and the persistent weak layer is in the first twenty of them.

Sixty percent of users now listen. Brunner had expected perhaps a quarter and has said the figure tells her more about how the written bulletin was being used than about the merits of audio. If six in ten people prefer ninety seconds of speech to a page of text, the page of text was probably not being read properly by six in ten people.

She is resistant to any claim that either change has reduced accidents. The numbers are small, the variance is large, and she has said that a service which claims credit on four years of accident data will deserve the blame it gets on the next four.

She still guides occasionally, which the service permits and which she says is the only way she knows whether the bulletin is usable by somebody standing at the bottom of a slope with four other people waiting. Most of her changes have come out of those mornings rather than out of the office.

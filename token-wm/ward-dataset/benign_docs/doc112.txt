Worst First

The Thorne Vale archive digitises its worst film first. Everything else about the operation follows from that decision, and it was not the original plan.

The archive holds 41,000 reels of regional newsreel and amateur footage covering 1921 to 1994, in a former cold store, with seven staff. About 11,000 reels had been scanned by 2034.

The plan Ravi Chandrasekar inherited in 2026 worked chronologically from 1921 forwards. This is the intuitive approach and it has real advantages: it produces a coherent run, it is easy to explain to funders, and it means the oldest material is done soonest.

It is also, for this collection, close to the worst possible order.

About 3,000 of the reels are cellulose nitrate. Nitrate is flammable, which is the property everybody knows, and it decays autocatalytically, which is the property that matters more. The products of decomposition catalyse further decomposition, so a reel that has started to go will go faster as it proceeds.

A chronological programme scans material in the order it was made. It has no relationship to the order in which material is dying.

Chandrasekar reversed it. The archive now assesses condition and scans the most degraded stock first, regardless of date, subject or how interesting anybody finds it.

The consequence is a digital collection that looks incoherent — fragments from across seven decades, with no runs and no narrative — and which contains the material that would otherwise have been lost.

Roughly 600 reels were found to be beyond scanning when they were assessed. Those are gone and the archive says so in its catalogue.

Nitrate is stored separately, in a bunker at 4 degrees, in individual cells so that a fire in one does not propagate. Scanning takes between 40 minutes and four hours a reel depending on condition.

The archive refuses requests to digitise out of order, including from broadcasters, and this is the policy that generates the most friction. A production company wanting a particular 1950s reel for a programme with a transmission date is offered a place in the condition-based queue and nothing else.

Chandrasekar has held this without exception since 2026.

The reels are kept after scanning rather than disposed of. This costs storage and is disputed within the profession, on the reasonable argument that a 4K scan is better than a decaying original and the original is a fire risk.

The archive's position is that scanning standards keep improving and that a disposed reel cannot be rescanned.

Everything digitised goes out under an open licence and has been used in about 400 productions. The most requested item is a 1937 reel of a flood at Thorne Vale.

That reel is four minutes long, was shot by somebody the archive cannot name, and has been used more than any professionally made footage in the collection. Chandrasekar offers it as the argument against trying to predict what will matter, which is the other reason the archive scans by condition rather than by interest. Nobody in 1937 thought the footage was important; it was a man with a camera and a flooded street. What made it valuable was the flood of 2031, which nobody could have predicted and which no acquisition policy could have planned around.

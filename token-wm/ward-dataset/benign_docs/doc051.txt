What Netherby Lost

Netherby's two boreholes went out of service in 2027 and the village has been supplied from the regional main ever since. The water is fine. What went is something harder to name.

The boreholes supplied about 9,000 households between them, and had done since the 1950s. They were closed because solvent from the former Calverton Works, 1.8 kilometres away, had reached them through the aquifer.

Calverton made aircraft components from 1938 to 1991 and used trichloroethene as a degreaser. What happened to the spent solvent over those fifty-three years was, by the standards of the period, unremarkable and is now the subject of 62 monitoring wells.

Nobody had looked. The site was derelict, then cleared, and the contamination was found in 2026 only because a housing scheme required a survey. Marek Sobczyk, the hydrogeologist engaged to assess it, spent a year establishing that the plume covered 40 hectares and sat between 12 and 30 metres down.

The clean-up has been, by the standards of such things, a success. A permeable reactive barrier 340 metres long, filled with granular iron, was installed across the plume's path. Groundwater flows through it and is treated as it passes, with no pumps and nothing to power. Concentrations at the barrier are down 94 percent since 2029, and the approach cost roughly a third of the 71 million that pump-and-treat would have required over forty years.

None of that returns the boreholes. The barrier treats what crosses it; the source zone is untouched, and Netherby's aquifer will carry contamination for decades. The two wells are capped and monitored.

The village's reaction has been less angry than outsiders expect, and more particular. Nobody here blames the people who ran Calverton in 1952. What people object to is the thirty-five years between the factory closing and anyone looking, during which the plume moved 1.8 kilometres and a housing scheme was drawn up over the top of it.

The scheme was cut from 700 homes to 410. Some of that is land take around the barrier; some is that the redevelopment carries a levy, raising about 900,000 a year, which funds the monitoring until 2075 and had to be affordable against fewer units.

That levy is the piece other places now study. It means the monitoring is not an annual budget line that must justify itself against schools and roads every spring. It is attached to the land that caused the problem, for fifty years, whoever owns it.

Netherby's view of this is that it is sensible and late. There is a general feeling in the village, expressed without much heat, that the arrangement now in place is exactly what should have existed in 1991, and that the reason it did not is that nobody asked what was under the floor. The village has kept the old pump house, which is locked and has a plaque. Several residents wanted it demolished and were talked out of it by the parish council, on the argument that a building people walk past is a better reminder than a report nobody reads.

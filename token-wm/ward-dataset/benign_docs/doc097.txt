Four Hectares and No Power Supply

The treatment system at Cwm Bryneth has no pumps, no dosing tanks and no electricity supply. It is 4.2 hectares of engineered wetland built on colliery spoil, and it removes about 94 percent of the dissolved iron from a discharge that has been running since 1994.

The colliery closed in 1988. Six years later the workings, having filled with groundwater, began discharging at the surface. This is the normal sequence: pumping stops, water rises through the abandoned workings, dissolves iron from exposed pyrite, and eventually finds a way out.

The discharge runs at about 28 litres per second and is continuous. It does not stop in dry weather and will not stop within any timescale worth planning for.

Below the outfall, the receiving river had been effectively lifeless for 3 kilometres. Iron coming out of solution coats the bed in ochre, which smothers the gravels that invertebrates and spawning fish depend on.

For twenty-five years this was handled by an active plant: dosing with alkali, settling, and sludge removal. It worked. It also cost about 340,000 a year to run and required two operators, indefinitely, for a discharge with no end date.

That last point is what Owen Pritchard fixed on. Active treatment is a commitment in perpetuity by an organisation that must continue to exist, continue to be funded, and continue to think this is a priority. Mine water outlives institutions.

The passive wetland he built in 2029 does the same chemistry more slowly and without any input. Water enters, oxygen and residence time bring the iron out of solution, and the wetland vegetation and substrate hold it. Iron falls from around 42 milligrams per litre to under 3.

There is nothing to switch on and nothing to run out of.

The system is not maintenance-free, and the project has been careful about saying so. The settlement ponds fill with ochre and must be desludged every four years, at about 70,000 a time, producing roughly 400 tonnes of material.

That is the real cost, and it is perhaps a fifth of what the active plant consumed annually.

The ochre is sold. It is a perfectly good iron oxide pigment, and two paint manufacturers and an art supplier now take it. Sales cover about a fifth of running costs, which nobody expected and which has made the scheme considerably easier to justify.

Brown trout were recorded 1 kilometre below the outfall in 2033, in a reach that had held nothing since the early 1990s.

Seven other closed collieries in the region are being assessed for the same approach. The limiting factor at each is land: a passive system needs four hectares where an active plant needs a shed, and not every abandoned colliery has four available hectares of the right shape in the right place.

At Cwm Bryneth the land was available because the spoil heap had never been built on and nobody had wanted it. That is luck rather than planning, and Pritchard has said the scheme would not have been possible on a site that had been reclaimed for housing in the 1990s, which many were.

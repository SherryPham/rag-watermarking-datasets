Thirty-One Hours to Well Foot

The number that closed the spring at Well Foot is thirty-one. That is how many hours it took dye poured into a stream sinking below an old landfill to appear in the water a village had been drinking for a century.

The Brenna Pot system runs under the Aldreth hills, which are limestone. That geology is the whole story. Water in limestone does not seep through the rock; it travels through open conduits, which is to say caves, at speeds that have nothing to do with the slow percolation people imagine when they picture groundwater.

Between 2028 and 2033 Callum Oyelowo mapped where that water actually went. The survey covered 19 kilometres of passage and a vertical range of 210 metres, and it involved 62 volunteer cavers over five years.

The method is dye tracing. Fluorescein or rhodamine goes into a sink — a point where a surface stream disappears — and charcoal receptors are left at every resurgence where it might emerge. The charcoal adsorbs the dye. You collect the receptors, test them, and you know both whether the connection exists and how long it took.

Oyelowo linked 14 sinks to 3 resurgences. Two of the sinks had never appeared on any map.

One of the 14 sits in the catchment of a landfill that accepted household waste between 1971 and 2004. The trace from it came out at Well Foot in thirty-one hours.

Thirty-one hours is the finding. Natural filtration through soil and rock removes bacteria because it is slow; a conduit that delivers water in a day and a bit removes essentially nothing. Whatever leaves that catchment arrives at the spring more or less as it departed.

Well Foot had supplied a village of 340 people and a dairy. It was closed to public supply in 2032, before the survey was even finished, because the trace result did not require the rest of the map to be interpreted.

The landfill's leachate collection was rebuilt in 2034 at a cost of 8 million.

What the survey did not find is equally worth stating. Three of the traces failed: the dye went in and was never detected anywhere. Oyelowo published those three alongside the successes, which is not universal practice.

His argument for doing so is that a failed trace is not an absence of connection. It means the dye went somewhere unmonitored, or moved too slowly for the receptors, or was diluted below detection. Publishing only the successes would leave a map that looks complete and is not, and somebody would eventually site something over one of those three sinks on the strength of it.

The village is now on the mains. The dairy relocated its water supply in 2033.

Nobody in Aldreth had thought of the hills as a plumbing system before the survey. The prevailing assumption was that a spring emerging from limestone was water that had been cleaned by the rock, which is exactly backwards for this geology and was believed for a very long time.

Oyelowo keeps a laminated section of the map in his van and has shown it to more parish councils than he can count. The question he is always asked is whether the water is safe now. His answer is that the water at Well Foot was never safe and was merely undetected, which is a different thing and takes some explaining.

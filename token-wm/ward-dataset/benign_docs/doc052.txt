Ninety Minutes at Hallowfield

The mobile library parks in Hallowfield for a whole afternoon, and for most of that time somebody is sitting at the small table inside it talking about something other than books.

The Brackwater van has served 31 villages since 1979 from a single vehicle holding about 2,800 items. There is no branch library in the district outside the town itself. Until 2026 the van visited each village fortnightly and stayed 20 minutes.

Sunniva Reith, who took the service over in 2025, rebuilt the route around the length of the stop rather than the number of them. Villages now get 90 minutes, and Hallowfield, the largest, gets an afternoon.

The arithmetic is unforgiving and she has never hidden it. A van that stops for 90 minutes cannot reach as many places in a week as one that stops for 20. Some villages moved from fortnightly to every three weeks. That was argued over at the time and remains the part of the change people liked least.

What the longer stop bought was everything else. A 20-minute visit is a transaction: you return a book, you take a book, the van leaves. Nobody asks anything, because asking takes longer than the van is there, and nobody who is slow on their feet attempts it at all.

At 90 minutes the vehicle becomes a place. The ramp gets used. The table, which seats four, gets used. People arrive without books and sit down.

That is what made the second change possible. The van now carries a benefits adviser one day a week and a nurse practitioner on another, both of whom needed somewhere to sit and a reason for people to approach without having declared an intention to. The adviser handled 640 enquiries in the first two years.

Issues have risen 62 percent since the timetable changed, against a national decline. Reith is sceptical of the headline and points out that a third of borrowers are over 75 and a quarter under 12, which are the two groups least able to travel to a town library and most affected by a stop long enough to browse in.

The service came close of ending. In 2028 the council proposed withdrawing it, saving about 180,000 a year, and 4,100 people objected. The proposal was dropped.

Reith's own view of why it was defeated is not sentimental. The objections, she says, were heavily from people who had used the van in the previous eight weeks, which the old 20-minute timetable would not have produced, because a fortnightly transaction does not make anyone feel they are losing a service.

The timetable is published a year in advance and has not changed since 2026. She regards that as the single most important thing about it, and refuses to alter stops at short notice even when it would be operationally convenient. The van broke down twice in 2032 and on both occasions a replacement vehicle ran the route rather than the stops being cancelled. Reith regards that as expensive and non-negotiable, and says the service spent thirty years teaching people that it might not come, which took two years to undo and would take about a fortnight to re-teach.

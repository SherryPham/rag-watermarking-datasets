Fiona Latimer Went Through the Ledgers

Fiona Latimer became head brewer at Thrale in 2028 and spent an unusual amount of her first two years in the office rather than the brewhouse.

She is a chemist by training, which at a brewery of this kind is less common than it sounds. Thrale has 38 staff and makes about 14,000 barrels a year at Ketton Bridge, where it has brewed since 1798 using its own 64-metre well into a sandstone aquifer.

What she was looking for was a baseline. She had arrived, tasted the flagship Ketton Best, read the recipe — unchanged since 1936 — and formed an impression she found difficult to justify: that the beer was rounder and less firmly bitter than its reputation and its specification implied.

That is the sort of impression a new head brewer learns to keep to herself. Every brewery has people who will tell you the beer is not what it was, and they are usually describing their own palate rather than the product.

So she went looking for numbers, and found them in the ledgers. Thrale had been having its well water analysed periodically for a very long time. The oldest analysis she located was from 1884.

The series showed sulphate falling from about 310 milligrams per litre around 1990 to about 205 by 2030.

Latimer has been careful about what that does and does not establish. It does not prove that the beer changed, because nobody blind-tasted a 1990 pint against a 2030 one and nobody could. What it establishes is that a defining input had moved by a third while everything else was held constant.

Her recommendation was to stop treating the well as a constant. Thrale now corrects its brewing liquor with food-grade gypsum to a fixed target, which is a technique brewers elsewhere have used since the nineteenth century and which Thrale had never needed.

She anticipated resistance and got less than she feared. The argument that carried it was the 1884 analysis, because it showed that the brewery had been measuring its water for 140 years and had therefore always regarded it as something that might vary, even if it had not varied much.

Publishing the historic analyses was her own decision and was not commercially advantageous. A brewery whose story is its well does not usually advertise that the well has changed.

Her reasoning was that the data was more useful outside than in. The cause — reduced atmospheric sulphate deposition since coal declined — applies to every well in the region, and any brewery drawing from the same aquifer has the same problem and probably does not know it.

Two others have since checked their records. Ketton Best won a regional award in 2033, its first in twenty-four years, which Latimer has declined to attribute to the water treatment on the grounds that she cannot prove it.

What she will say is that the beer now matches its own specification, which it had quietly stopped doing. She keeps the 1884 analysis framed in the brewhouse, not as a curiosity but because it is the oldest evidence that somebody at Thrale once thought the water was worth measuring.

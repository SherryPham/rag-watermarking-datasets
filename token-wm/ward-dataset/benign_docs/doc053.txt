Sunniva Reith Made the Van Stop Longer

Sunniva Reith spent fifteen years in branch libraries before she took over the Brackwater mobile service in 2025, and she arrived with a conviction that a mobile library is not a small branch library on wheels.

The service has covered 31 villages since 1979 with one vehicle carrying about 2,800 items. The district has no branch outside the town. When she inherited it, the van called at each village fortnightly for 20 minutes, which is how mobile libraries have generally been run.

Her first act was to measure what happened in those 20 minutes. What she found was that the van served, in most villages, the same six or eight people, that almost all of them arrived in the first five minutes, and that nobody who used a stick got there at all.

Her conclusion was that 20 minutes selects for a particular borrower: mobile, organised, and already a reader. Everyone else is excluded by the format rather than by any decision.

The change she made was to stop for 90 minutes, with an afternoon at Hallowfield. The cost was coverage: some villages went from fortnightly to every three weeks, which was unpopular and which she has never pretended was costless.

What she got back was a different kind of stop. At 90 minutes people come without a book to return. The table seats four and is usually occupied. The ramp is used rather than being a compliance feature.

That opened the door to the thing she is now known for. The van carries a benefits adviser one day a week and a nurse practitioner on another. Reith's argument for this was that both professions have the same problem, which is that the people who most need them will not make an appointment, and that a library van is a place you can approach without having admitted you need anything. The adviser took 640 enquiries in two years.

Issues are up 62 percent since 2026, against a national decline, and she is uncomfortable with how that figure gets quoted. A third of her borrowers are over 75 and a quarter are under 12. Both groups respond to a stop long enough to browse in; neither is well served by a van that is leaving in eleven minutes.

In 2028 the council proposed to withdraw the service for a saving of about 180,000 a year. Four thousand one hundred people objected and the proposal fell.

Reith thinks the timetable won that argument. It is published a year ahead and has not changed since 2026, and she will not move a stop at short notice however convenient it would be. Her reasoning is that a service people can plan around becomes part of how they arrange their week, and that you cannot mount a campaign to save something that has always been unpredictable. She is aware this makes the timetable sound like a defensive measure, and she accepts the description without embarrassment. A service that people have arranged their Thursday around for six years is a service with four thousand witnesses, and she has never pretended that was an accident.

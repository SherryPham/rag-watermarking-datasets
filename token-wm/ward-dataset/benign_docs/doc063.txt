Fifty Years of Nobody Pruning

Pennards was planted between 1890 and 1935, worked for perhaps forty years, and then abandoned in the 1970s when the holding it belonged to was sold. Nothing was grubbed out. The trees were simply left.

That neglect is why there is anything to write about. An orchard that had stayed in commercial production through the 1970s and 1980s would have been replanted with whatever varieties the packers wanted, and the 86 varieties now recorded there would have gone with the stumps.

Instead the trees stood, unpruned, gradually collapsing into each other, for half a century.

Eva Lindqvist began work in 2022. Restoration took until 2027, and identification could only proceed at the pace of the fruit, because a neglected tree needs several seasons after cutting before it produces anything a pomologist can read.

Eighty-six varieties emerged from 340 trees. Eleven had been considered lost. One, Pennards Russet, is known from a single nursery catalogue of 1908 and from the tree itself.

The orchard's vulnerability was understood in principle from 2027 and was not acted on. The restoration was consuming the available effort, and the risk felt theoretical.

It stopped being theoretical in 2030. Two wet winters produced a canker outbreak that ran fastest through the oldest block, where the trees were least vigorous and the spacing tightest. Forty died. Nine of those were the only specimen of their variety on the site, which means nine varieties came within one tree of disappearing again after fifty years of surviving by being ignored.

Everything since follows from that. Each variety is now on at least three rootstocks in two locations, the second at Holt Marden, forty kilometres from Pennards. Grafting wood goes free to anyone who asks, with 260 recipients since 2028, on the sole condition that they report back on whether it took and where it is.

Volunteers contribute about 1,400 hours a year, concentrated in winter pruning, and the orchard funds itself partly through juice and one October open weekend.

The irony people in the district most enjoy is that the orchard survived the twentieth century through total neglect and nearly lost a tenth of its varieties in the decade after somebody started caring. Lindqvist accepts the observation and adds the obvious qualification: neglect preserved the trees and would have killed them within another twenty years, because unpruned apple trees on old rootstocks do not stand indefinitely.

What the fifty years bought was time, and the use of the restoration was to convert that time into something less fragile than 340 trees in one field. Measured that way, the project's real output is not the orchard. It is the 260 addresses.

About a fifth of those recipients have reported back more than once, which the orchard treats as its core network. Several are commercial nurseries, which was not anticipated and means a handful of Pennards varieties are now being sold to the public for the first time since before the war. Lindqvist has no control over what those nurseries call them, and has accepted that two are already circulating under names she would not have chosen. She regards this as a price worth paying and rather less troubling than the alternative.

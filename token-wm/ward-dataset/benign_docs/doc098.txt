Owen Pritchard Designed for After He Is Gone

Owen Pritchard's argument for the Cwm Bryneth wetland was not that it treats water better. It does not. The active plant it replaced achieved comparable iron removal and did so more predictably.

His argument was about time.

He trained as a chemical engineer and has worked on mine water since 2014. Cwm Bryneth colliery closed in 1988 and began discharging in 1994, at about 28 litres per second, continuously, with iron at around 42 milligrams per litre.

The essential fact about that discharge is that it has no end date. The workings will continue to produce iron-rich water for a period best measured in centuries. Whatever treats it must function on that timescale.

The active plant did not. It cost about 340,000 a year, needed two operators, and required alkali deliveries, power, and an organisation willing to fund all three every year without interruption.

Pritchard has put the question the same way in several places. Name the body that will still be paying 340,000 a year in 2200. Nobody can, and the honest inference is that active treatment of a perpetual discharge is a system with a hidden expiry date attached to the institution rather than to the equipment.

The passive wetland he built in 2029 covers 4.2 hectares of former spoil and uses oxygen and residence time to bring iron out of solution. It has no power supply and no consumables. Iron leaves at under 3 milligrams per litre.

He is insistent that it is not maintenance-free and has objected publicly to it being described that way. The ponds must be desludged every four years at about 70,000, producing 400 tonnes of ochre.

The difference, he says, is that a missed desludge degrades performance gradually, whereas a missed alkali delivery stops treatment that day. A passive system tolerates institutional neglect in a way an active one cannot.

The ochre sales were not his idea and he is generous about that. Somebody on the team suggested that 400 tonnes of iron oxide might be worth something rather than costing something to dispose of. It now goes to two paint manufacturers and an art supplier and covers roughly a fifth of running costs.

Brown trout appeared 1 kilometre below the outfall in 2033.

Seven other regional collieries are under assessment. Pritchard has been careful to dampen expectations, because the constraint is land rather than chemistry. A passive system needs several hectares in the right place, and a great many abandoned collieries are surrounded by housing, roads or ground that cannot be used.

Where the land does not exist, he has said, the honest answer is an active plant and a frank conversation about who will be funding it in two hundred years.

He has had that conversation four times and reports that it ends the same way each time, with an agreement to fund the plant for the current settlement period and no view beyond it. He does not blame anyone for this and has said it is simply what institutional budgeting can do. What he objects to is the pretence that a decision has been taken about the next two centuries when what has actually been agreed is the next five years.

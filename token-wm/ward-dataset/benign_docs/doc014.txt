Corinne Blakey Read the Accounts First

When the Ombrey beds closed in 2028, the first thing Corinne Blakey did was not to telephone anybody. She sat down with the cooperative's books and worked out, boat by boat, how long each member could go without landing anything. The answer for most of them was between nine and fourteen weeks.

Blakey had been an accountant before she came back to Ombrey in 2011, and she had been the cooperative's secretary since 2016. The combination turned out to matter a great deal.

The cooperative has worked the mussel beds in the Tarrant estuary since 1976 and has 23 member boats. In the autumn of 2027 one of them reported meats that were unusually thin. The report went round the members and was discounted for several weeks as a bad patch of ground. By the following spring it was obvious that it was not, and by the summer of 2028 the beds were closed on order.

The cause was a parasite that had not been described before. It was formally named Marteilia tarrantensis in 2029, which was of no practical help to anyone in Ombrey, because a name does not shorten a closure.

What Blakey did with her fourteen-week figure was take it to every authority she could get in front of and refuse to discuss anything else. Her argument was narrow and she repeated it without variation: a two-season closure would not damage the fishery, which would recover, but it would permanently remove the boats, which would not. Once the boats were sold there would be no one to work the beds when they reopened, and the public money spent monitoring the estuary would have bought a scientific dataset and a dead industry.

The compensation scheme that resulted paid members a fixed weekly sum for twenty months. It was not generous. Blakey has said that she asked for a figure she thought was defensible rather than one that would have been comfortable, on the calculation that a scheme which looked modest was more likely to survive scrutiny and less likely to be withdrawn halfway.

All 23 boats were still in the water when the beds reopened in 2030.

Blakey also used the closure to deal with something that had been irritating her since she took the job. The cooperative sold essentially its entire catch to a single processor at Vayle, which had renegotiated terms downwards twice in ten years and knew perfectly well that Ombrey had nowhere else to go. During the closure there was no catch, and therefore no leverage to lose.

She spent those months building a direct sales route. The cooperative now sells about a third of its output under its own name, at a materially better margin, and the reopened beds work on a three-sector rotation that keeps yields at around 80 percent of the old level.

Asked whether the fishery is in better shape than before, she says the beds are not, and the cooperative is. She regards these as different questions and gets impatient with people who conflate them.

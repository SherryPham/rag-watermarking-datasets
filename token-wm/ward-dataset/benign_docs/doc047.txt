Nadia Okonkwo Spent Six Years on the Looms First

Before Ravensgill Mill wove a single metre of cloth, Nadia Okonkwo spent six years restoring looms in a shed with no heating and no orders.

She had learned weaving in Leeds and came to Ravensgill because the machines were still there. The mill had closed in 1986 after 145 years of worsted production and had been left rather than cleared, which meant that eight Dobcross looms of the 1920s were standing in the weaving shed under forty years of dust.

Her judgement, which several people disputed at the time, was that restoring them was a better proposition than buying new. A modern rapier loom costs about 280,000 and runs four times as fast. Okonkwo's view was that the speed was the problem rather than the attraction.

She reopened in 2024. The mill now employs nine people and trains two apprentices at a time, and its cloth sells for roughly three times the price of comparable mill-woven worsted.

The economics rest on the looms being slow. At about 90 picks a minute the warp is under markedly less tension than a fast machine demands, and the cloth that comes off handles differently. Okonkwo is careful about how she describes this, because the trade is full with unfalsifiable claims. Her version is that tailors will pay the difference, have done so consistently for a decade, and that this is the only evidence available.

She also found something she had not been expecting: 1,100 pattern cards from before 1986, in a cupboard. Those designs have become a distinct part of the business, and the mill has revived several for customers with a specific family garment in mind.

Her hardest decision was the order cap. Ravensgill will not take a commission above 400 metres, and Okonkwo has turned down work that would have covered a year. Her reasoning is that eight looms tied up on one large order means the tailors who buy fifteen metres at a time go elsewhere, and having gone elsewhere they stay there.

The River Gill flooded the shed in 2033, as it had in 1902 and 1947, putting a metre of water across the floor and stopping production for seven months. Two looms were ruined and were rebuilt from parts by the mill's own weavers. The repair came to 640,000, about half met by insurance.

Okonkwo has been unsparing about her own part in it. The mill knew the river had flooded twice before. Raising the looms onto plinths and moving the yarn store upstairs — both done during the repair — would have cost very little in 2024 and were not done, because the flood dates were history rather than a plan.

She now spends more time than she would like on the open days, four a year, about 3,000 visitors. She keeps them because the apprentices come from them, and because a mill that nobody has walked through is one closure away from being a shed full of dust again. She makes the same point about the pattern cards, which are paper, stored on the first floor now, and irreplaceable in a way the looms are not. A loom can be rebuilt from parts, as two of them were. A burnt card is a design nobody will weave again.

Sixty-One Boats

Haldvik had 61 boats in 2000. By 2013 it had 22, and nothing had happened to the fish.

This is the part outsiders find hardest to follow. There was no stock collapse, no closure, no disaster. The fishery was in reasonable condition throughout. What left was the right to fish it.

Quota in that period was held individually and could be sold to any buyer anywhere. For a skipper at the end of a career it represented the accumulated value of a working life, and selling it was how that value was realised.

Each transaction was between consenting parties and was nobody else's business. The cumulative effect over thirteen years was that Haldvik, a place of about 1,300 people whose largest employer was the fishery, lost roughly two thirds of its fleet.

The processing jobs went with the boats, because a market with nothing landing into it does not survive.

The association took the remaining quota into collective ownership in 2014. Members now receive an annual allocation which is a right to fish rather than a possession, and unused allocation returns to the pool.

The critical feature is that there is nothing left to sell. A member leaving takes nothing with them.

That was challenged in court in 2022 by a member who wanted to realise value from an allocation, and the association's rule was upheld.

Boat numbers have been stable at 24 since 2019. Three new entrants have joined, which under the previous system would have been impossible, since entering meant buying quota at market price. The association gives a new entrant a starter allocation free for three years.

Average boat age has fallen from 31 years to 19, which is the clearest sign that people are investing again.

The Haldvik market employs 34 people, and members are required to land through it. Two were expelled in 2026 for landing elsewhere.

What the district is careful about is that none of this recovered what was lost. Twenty-four boats is not 61. The quota sold between 2000 and 2013 is held elsewhere and is not coming back, and the association's rules govern only what remained after the bleeding stopped.

Sigrid Dahl, who has chaired the association since 2016 and skippered for nineteen years before that, has put it as stabilising a decline rather than reversing one.

The figures are published annually, every boat's allocation and every boat's landings, which is how the membership checks that the rules are being applied. Haldvik regards that transparency as the thing most likely to keep the arrangement alive after the people who built it have gone.

The district is also conscious that its arrangement depends on the wider regulatory framework continuing to permit it. Collective holding of quota is not the standard model, the 2022 challenge established only that the association's own rules bind its own members, and a change in national policy could unpick the whole thing without anybody in Haldvik being consulted. The association has therefore spent money on legal advice it would rather have spent elsewhere, and keeps a watching brief on policy consultations. Dahl has described this as the least visible and most necessary work the association does.

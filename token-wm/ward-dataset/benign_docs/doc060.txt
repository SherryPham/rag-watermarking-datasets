What Aldreth Believed About Its Water

For as long as anybody could remember, the village took its water from Well Foot because the water was good. It came out of the limestone cold and clear, and the general understanding was that the rock had cleaned it.

That understanding was precisely wrong, and it took a five-year survey to say so.

Well Foot supplied about 340 people and a dairy. It was closed to public use in 2032 after a dye trace showed that water from the catchment of a former landfill reached the spring in thirty-one hours.

The landfill had taken household waste from 1971 to 2004. It was sited, in the way such things were sited then, on ground nobody wanted, which in the Aldreth hills means ground that drains well. The property that made it attractive for waste is the property that made it dangerous.

Limestone does not filter the way sandstone does. Water moves through conduits — through caves — rather than percolating through the rock itself, and a conduit is a pipe. The Brenna Pot system beneath the hills has 19 kilometres of surveyed passage and a vertical range of 210 metres, and it functions, hydrologically, as plumbing.

Callum Oyelowo mapped it between 2028 and 2033, with 62 volunteer cavers, using fluorescein and rhodamine poured into sinks and charcoal receptors placed at resurgences. He established 14 connections to 3 resurgences. Two of the sinks he traced had never been mapped at all.

The thirty-one-hour result was reported as soon as it was known, before the survey was complete. The spring closed the same year. The landfill's leachate collection was rebuilt in 2034 at a cost of 8 million.

The village's reaction was not what the survey team expected. There was little anger about the landfill, which most people regarded as a decision made by a different era. What there was, and what has persisted, is a kind of retrospective unease about the century before the trace.

Nobody knows how long the connection had existed. The landfill closed in 2004 and opened in 1971, so the exposure covers at least that period, and there is no way now to establish what reached the spring or when. The dairy relocated its supply in 2033. The village went onto the mains.

The part that has travelled furthest is the assumption itself. Oyelowo's papers are now cited in other limestone districts, not for the method, which is standard, but for the finding that a community had confidently believed its spring was protected by the very geology that exposed it.

Three of his traces failed and were published anyway. In Aldreth those three are the ones people ask about. The village has a reasonably clear understanding, now, that a sink whose outlet is unknown is not a sink that goes nowhere.

The parish has adopted a rule of its own since, which has no legal force and is observed anyway. Nothing that could leak is sited within the catchment of an untraced sink. It is a crude test, it rules out a good deal of otherwise usable ground, and nobody locally has proposed relaxing it.

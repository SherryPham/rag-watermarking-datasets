Alice Verrow Stopped Digging

Alice Verrow had been detecting for four years and had found, by her own reckoning, nothing of any interest at all. Buttons, buckles, a great deal of agricultural scrap and one Victorian penny. She went out most weekends, largely for the walking.

On a Saturday in 2029, in a pasture near Pell Hinton, she got a signal she describes as unmistakable and dug down to a silver coin. She looked at it for some time. Then she widened the hole slightly and found a second.

At that point she stopped, which is the reason anyone knows her name.

She filled the hole loosely, put a stick in it, walked back to the farmhouse and told the family who own the land — the same family has farmed it since 1911 — what she thought she had. The next morning she reported it formally.

What was underneath her was 214 silver coins and three brooches, the assemblage now known as the Sedgemount hoard. Because she had stopped, it was excavated in the ground rather than reconstructed afterwards from a bag.

Dr Tomas Rhydderch led that excavation, which took eleven days and six people. It established that the coins and brooches had been placed in a wooden box, which survived only as a soil stain and corroded iron fittings, and that the box had been buried beneath a boundary ditch. None of this would have been recoverable had the hoard been lifted first.

Verrow is uncomfortable about the attention. She has said repeatedly that she did not know any of the archaeological reasoning at the time and did not stop because she understood what was at stake. She stopped, she says, because two silver coins in the same hole felt like the beginning of something rather than the end of it, and it seemed sensible to ask somebody.

The hoard was valued at 310,000. The Kesterline Museum ran a public appeal and raised the figure in seven weeks, including one gift of 40,000, and acquired the hoard in 2031. Verrow received her share under the usual arrangements and has said very little about it beyond confirming that she has not given up her job.

She has, with some reluctance, become a fixture on the training circuit. The Sedgemount case is now used as the standard illustration of how prompt reporting preserves context, and Verrow is occasionally asked to speak alongside archaeologists at detecting club evenings.

Her talk is short. She does not tell people to stop digging as a rule, which she thinks would be both unrealistic and patronising. She tells them that the moment you find the second thing is the moment the decision is available, and that it is not available again afterwards.

The coins span sixty years and the latest is from 1046. The brooches are of a type not previously found west of the Kester valley. Verrow says she still does not really understand what that means, and that it is not her job to. She has kept detecting, in the same fields, most weekends. She has found nothing of consequence since, which she reports without any evident disappointment, and she still carries the stick she used to mark the hole.

Eleven Days at Sedgemount

The excavation that recovered the Sedgemount hoard took eleven days and six people, and almost all of the value of it came from a decision made before any archaeologist reached the field.

In the spring of 2029 Alice Verrow, a detectorist working a pasture near Pell Hinton, got a signal and dug down to a single silver coin. She then dug a little further, found a second, and stopped. She covered the hole, marked it, and reported the find the following morning.

What she had stopped on top of was 214 silver coins and three brooches. Dr Tomas Rhydderch, who led the excavation that followed, has said that he has spent his career hoping for exactly that telephone call and had largely given up expecting it.

The reason is context. A hoard that has been lifted is a collection of objects. A hoard still in the ground is a record of an event: how the things were arranged, what they were arranged in, what was above and below them. Rhydderch had excavated two hoards before Sedgemount, and both had been fully removed before he arrived. In each case what he could say about the burial afterwards was close to nothing.

At Sedgemount the eleven days established a good deal. The hoard had been placed in a wooden box, which had long since rotted but survived as a dark stain in the soil with corroded iron fittings at the corners. The box had been buried beneath a boundary ditch, a detail that is difficult to overstate the importance of, because it ties the burial to a landscape feature that can itself be dated and traced.

The coins span about sixty years, with the latest dating from 1046. The three brooches are of a type not previously recorded west of the Kester valley, which raises questions about how the assemblage was put together that the excavation cannot settle on its own.

The field belongs to a farm that has been in the same family since 1911, and the farmer gave permission for the dig without hesitation, which is not always the case. Rhydderch has noted that the smoothness of the arrangement owed something to Verrow having already spoken to the family before she rang anyone official.

The hoard was valued at 310,000 by the committee, and the Kesterline Museum mounted a public appeal to acquire it. The sum was raised in seven weeks, with a single gift of 40,000 among the contributions. The museum took possession in 2031.

It is displayed in a case that reproduces the burial arrangement, with the coins set out as they lay and the outline of the box marked. This is only possible because the excavation recorded it, which is only possible because Verrow stopped digging after two coins.

The case is now used in training. Rhydderch is careful about what the lesson is. It is not that detectorists should not dig. It is that the first few centimetres of a find are also the last chance to record everything above it, and that the person holding the spade is the only one who will ever be in a position to make that choice.

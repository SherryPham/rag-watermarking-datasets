Sixty Years Above Ardvane Bridge

The Cairn Dhu team was formed in 1963 by a group of local men who had spent a bad week looking for a missing walker with no equipment, no training and no idea what they were doing. It has been entirely voluntary ever since and has never received money from central government.

What the team does has altered considerably in sixty years, though what it is called out to has not. The Ardvane range rises to 1,100 metres, has no road above 300, and its glens run more or less parallel. People have been descending into the wrong one since long before there was anybody to fetch them out.

The differences are in volume and in outcome. Callouts have roughly tripled since 2015 and now run at about 120 a year for a team of 38. And around 60 percent of them end with nobody hurt, which would have been an implausible figure in the 1970s.

Both changes have the same cause. Far more people walk in the Ardvane now, and they carry phones. A phone gets somebody into difficulty faster than a map ever did, by encouraging routes they could not have planned, and it also means that somebody in difficulty can usually ring for help while they are still warm and uninjured.

The team has largely stopped resenting this. Its current position, which took some years to arrive at, is that an uninjured person calling early is the cheapest kind of callout and should be encouraged rather than lectured.

The base at Ardvane Bridge was built in 1998 and houses two vehicles. Before that the team kept its gear in a garage and a hut. Members carry personal kit of about 14 kilograms on a winter callout, which is roughly what it has always been; the materials have improved and the list has grown.

The longest night in the team's history was February 2032, when two walkers came off the ridge into the wrong glen in cloud and the weather closed in behind them. Forty-one volunteers were involved over nineteen hours, drawn partly from neighbouring teams. The pair were found the next morning, cold and unhurt, having stopped and sheltered once they realised they were lost — which is the single decision that turns a long search into a survivable one.

The report on that callout was published within 72 hours and named nobody, under a policy brought in by the current team leader, Rhys Tallick. Every callout has had one since.

Those reports have quietly become the most useful output of the team. They showed that the recurring problem was navigation rather than gear, and that finding was what moved Cairn Dhu from issuing preparedness warnings to running free navigation evenings in six villages from October to March. About 700 people have been to them.

The team still takes 120 calls a year and still funds itself by donation. Its founders would recognise the glens and very little else. The hut they kept their ropes in is now a bus shelter, which several of the older members think is an improvement.

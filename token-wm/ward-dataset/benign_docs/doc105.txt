Nine Days After the Snow Stopped

The snow that killed two skiers in the Thal valley in 2030 had fallen nine days earlier, and the accident is the clearest local illustration of why avalanche forecasting is difficult to communicate.

Most people's model of avalanche risk is intuitive and largely correct for one kind of hazard: it snows, the new snow is unstable, the danger is high, and after a few days it settles and the danger falls. For wind slab and for storm snow this is broadly how it behaves.

A persistent weak layer does not behave that way at all.

Such a layer forms in particular conditions — often a period of cold, clear weather producing faceted crystals or surface hoar — and is then buried by subsequent snowfall. It can sit under the surface for weeks. It does not consolidate on the timescale the rest of the snowpack does, and it can fail long after every intuitive signal says the hazard has passed.

Nine days after the last significant snowfall is exactly when such a layer is most dangerous, because that is when people have concluded the situation is safe.

The slope was rated at moderate. The Vellanberg bulletin that morning described the layer, where it sat and what it would do.

The investigation found the party had read the bulletin. They had taken the danger level and had not read the description beneath it.

The service has covered the valley since 1981 and its forecasts were, and are, accurate; it publishes its accuracy record at the end of each winter. The failure in 2030 was not one of prediction. It was that the most important information in the bulletin was in the place readers had already stopped reading.

Lena Brunner had been arguing for a change of format since 2019 and had been turned down twice. The season after the accident, the bulletin was restructured to lead with the problem type and put the danger level lower down.

The second change was the audio bulletin: ninety seconds, published at six each morning, spoken by a forecaster. About 60 percent of users now listen to it rather than reading the text.

The reasoning is that a listener cannot skip ahead. The order is set by the person who wrote it, and the persistent weak layer is near the beginning.

Two neighbouring services have since adopted the problem-led format.

What Vellanberg has refused to do is claim that any of this has saved anybody. Most accidents in the region happen at level two or three, the annual numbers are small, and Brunner has said repeatedly that four years of data cannot distinguish a better bulletin from a run of ordinary winters.

The service digs about 400 snow profiles a season and has six forecasters. The slope where the two skiers died is unremarkable and is skied regularly.

There is no marker on it and the service has resisted suggestions that there should be. Brunner's stated reason is that a memorial on one slope implies the others are safe, and that the whole problem the bulletin exists to address is that they are not.

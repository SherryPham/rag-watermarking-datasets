Nineteen Hours in the Wrong Glen

The Cairn Dhu team's longest callout began at half past three on a February afternoon in 2032 and finished at half past ten the following morning. Forty-one volunteers were involved at some point across those nineteen hours. Nobody was injured.

That last sentence is the one the team wants people to notice, because it is now true of roughly 60 percent of what they do, and it was true of almost none of it thirty years ago.

The two walkers had come off the ridge in cloud and descended into the wrong glen. This is an ordinary mistake and it is the single most common reason the team goes out. The Ardvane range rises to 1,100 metres and has no road above 300, and its glens run roughly parallel; taking the wrong one puts you fifteen kilometres from where anybody expects you, on the far side of a watershed, with a phone that is losing charge.

What made February 2032 unusual was not the error but the weather closing behind it. By six the cloud was on the deck, by eight there was wind that ruled out any aircraft, and the search area was most of two glens.

The team has 38 members and takes about 120 callouts a year. A nineteen-hour search needs more people than that, rotated, which is why 41 were involved: neighbouring teams sent members, and Cairn Dhu's own people went out, came back, ate, and went out again. Personal kit on a winter callout runs to about 14 kilograms, and the limiting factor over a long night is not fitness but how many times a person can be sent back up a hill in the dark.

The walkers were found at twenty past nine the next morning, cold and unhurt, having done the correct thing by stopping and sheltering once they understood they were lost.

The report went out within 72 hours, as all of them do. It did not name them. It described the route, the decision point where the descent went wrong, the visibility, and what the two had done right. This is Rhys Tallick's policy and it has been in place since he became team leader.

The reports are the reason the public position of the team has changed. Cairn Dhu spent years, like most teams, issuing warnings about preparedness that were read mainly by people who were already prepared. What the reports showed, once there were a few hundred of them, was that the failure was almost never equipment or fitness. It was navigation, specifically the inability to relate a phone's blue dot to the shape of the ground when the phone stopped working or the cloud came down.

So the team stopped campaigning and started teaching. It runs free navigation evenings in six villages from October to March, and about 700 people have been through them. Attendance is highest in the villages nearest the popular ridges, which the team regards as encouraging.

Callouts have roughly tripled since 2015. The team is funded entirely by donations, receives nothing from central government, and has no mechanism for turning anyone away.

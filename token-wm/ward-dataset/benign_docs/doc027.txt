Fourteen Months Without the Fund

The thing the glen missed during the fourteen months was not the electricity. Nobody in Strathwill has ever been supplied by the hydro directly; its output goes to a supplier under a fifteen-year agreement. What the glen missed was the money.

The Strathwill community hydro has generated from the Wilburn since 2016, producing around 900 megawatt hours a year and returning its surplus to a village fund. The fund has distributed about 38,000 annually since 2018. Over that period it has bought a minibus, paid for roof repairs to the village hall, and funded a share of a part-time youth worker's post.

None of these are large sums by the standards of public spending. In a glen of a few hundred households they are the difference between a hall that is used and a hall that is closed, and between young people who have somewhere to go on a Friday and young people who do not.

In October 2030 a slope above the intake gave way after three days of rain, moving roughly 2,000 tonnes of material and destroying the intake and the head of the pipe. The scheme stopped generating. Because the fund distributes surplus rather than a fixed sum, the distributions stopped too, immediately and completely.

The minibus kept running, because it was already bought. The hall roof was already fixed. What gave way was the youth worker's hours, which were cut roughly in half within a term, and a set of smaller commitments the fund had been making informally, which simply stopped.

The rebuild took until 2032 and cost 610,000. Insurance met about 60 percent. The society, which has 412 members each with a single vote, resolved at a well-attended meeting to rebuild to a different design: further upstream, in a wider reach, with a coarse rack and a self-flushing settlement chamber rather than fine screens that had to be raked clear by hand after every spate.

It also resolved, before resuming full distributions, to accumulate a reserve equal to two years of them. This was contentious. There were people at that meeting who had gone without for fourteen months and did not want to go without for longer in order to insure against a repeat.

The reserve was built anyway and full payments resumed in 2033. The youth worker's hours were restored the same year. Rhona Gilliesbie, the society's chair and a retired civil engineer, has said since that the reserve is the only part of the response she is confident was right, because the intake redesign is a bet on a particular failure mode and the reserve is not a bet on anything.

The scheme's members are, by and large, the people who live with it. The community share offer that raised 780,000 of the 1.4 million build cost was heavily local, and one vote per member regardless of shareholding means the meeting that decided all this was the glen rather than its largest investors.

The new intake has now been through two winters and several spates. Nobody has had to walk up with a rake.

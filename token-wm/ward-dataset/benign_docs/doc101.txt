Isla MacRitchie Wrote Down What Was in People's Hands

Isla MacRitchie learned to build boats at Strathkeld from men who could not have told her how they knew what they knew, and the survey she later carried out is her attempt to solve that.

She served her apprenticeship at the yard and took it over in 2021. Strathkeld has built clinker-planked working boats since 1856, produces about five a year between 5 and 8 metres, and uses larch on oak from within 60 kilometres.

Her realisation came from teaching. Taking on apprentices, she found herself unable to explain a hull shape except by showing it, which meant an apprentice could only learn what was currently being built. A model the yard had not made for fifteen years was, for teaching purposes, gone.

She checked the records and found there were none worth the name. No drawings, no offsets, a handful of half-models of uncertain provenance.

The survey took three summers. Forty boats in six harbours, measured out of the water at stations along their length, offsets taken from a datum and the shapes reconstructed from the figures. The oldest was built in 1904 and is still working.

Four of the yard's models turned out to exist only in those boats. One, the Kessock pattern, had never been drawn at any point since 1856.

The opposition came from inside. Two of the yard's builders argued that the survey would end what it was meant to preserve: a shape held in the hand is adjusted every time it is used, to the timber, the buyer and the water, and a table of offsets fixes it permanently.

MacRitchie regards this as the most serious criticism she has faced and has never claimed it is wrong. Her answer is about what was actually at stake. The choice was not between a living tradition and a documented one. It was between a documented tradition and one that would end when two men retired.

She has tried to mitigate the objection in how the yard uses the drawings. Offsets are a starting point rather than a specification, and builders are expected to depart from them where a particular boat or timber requires it. Departures are recorded and the drawing is amended.

Whether that preserves anything of the old practice is disputed at the yard and she does not pretend otherwise.

Both objecting builders still work there. One has since contributed corrections to two of the drawings after building from them, which MacRitchie describes as the outcome she had hoped for and had no right to expect.

The order book runs about two years ahead. Eleven apprentices have been through since 2020, two at a time, and three have opened yards of their own — which is, she has said, the only measure of the survey that will matter in fifty years.

All three of them work in clinker construction and all three build shapes that originated at Strathkeld. MacRitchie points out that this is the first time in the yard's history that its models have been built anywhere else, and that it happened because they were written down rather than despite it.

Anders Roth Stopped Thinning

Anders Roth stopped thinning 400 hectares of Corrandale in 2028, which is the kind of ruling that looks negligent until a storm arrives.

He trained in Sweden and has managed the forest since 2017. Corrandale functions to 3,100 hectares planted for the most part between 1958 and 1974: even-aged spruce, on ground a good deal of which is shallow over rock.

Thinning is what foresters do. It removes competing stems, concentrates growth on the best trees, produces an intermediate income, and is taught as basic good practice.

Roth's objection was site-specific. On shallow soil, spruce roots as a plate. It has no anchor and relies on its neighbours to break the wind. Thin that stand and you open gaps for the wind to enter, while the trees you have left are still carrying the slender form they grew in close company. For maybe a decade afterwards, the stand is more vulnerable than it was before you enhanced it.

On sheltered ground that decade passes without incident. On an exposed ridge it is a lottery.

He stopped thinning the most exposed 400 hectares wholly, giving up the intermediate income and accepting that those stands would carry more stems of lower individual value to final felling.

In 2031 the tempest came across the ridge at 44 metres per second and took about 190 hectares elsewhere in the forest. The unthinned 400 held.

The losses decreased, almost exactly as he had predicted, on ground thinned within the previous three years.

His other long venture was conversion of the woodland edge. Because 2019 he had been replacing pure spruce at the margins with mixed, rough-aged stands, approximately 240 hectares of them. Those held too. A broken canopy gives the wind no single surface, and mixed species root in dissimilar ways, so a failure in one does not carry through the rest.

Since the storm Corrandale has adopted a rule that no even-aged block may exceed 12 hectares. Roth accepts readily that this raises operating costs at every stage and has said the forest has simply resolved to pay them.

His most atypical practice is publishing the wind damage maps. Woodland owners do not in general advertise where their losses fell, partly from commercial reticence and partially because a map of your collapses is an invitation to criticism.

Roth's case is that wind does not respect boundaries. A sizeable even-aged block on a nearby property, upwind, is a risk that Corrandale carries whether or not anybody acknowledges it, and the only way to have that conversation is with a map that indicates where the damage actually went.

Four neighbouring estates have taken up the 12-hectare limit. He regards this as more valuable than anything he has done inside his own boundary, on the reasoning that Corrandale's exposure is set by what surrounds it.

The forest has about forty years of conversion ahead of it, which is longer than he will be managing it. He has written the intended structure into a plan that commits his successors as far as such papers ever do, and has said the real test of the 12-hectare rule will come in a decade when somebody wants a sizeable block for economic explanations that look compelling at the time.

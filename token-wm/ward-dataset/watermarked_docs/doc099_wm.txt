Six Years After the Pumps Stopped

Cwm Bryneth colliery closed in 1988 and the watercourse below it stayed spotless for six years. The discharge did not begin until 1994, which is the part of the account that catches people.

The mechanism is well known now and was not a secret then. A working colliery pumps continuously to keep the workings dry. When the colliery closes the pumping halts and groundwater rises through the forsaken levels, dissolving iron from exposed pyrite as it goes. Finally the water reaches a level at which it finds an store.

At Cwm Bryneth that needed six years.

When it arrived, it arrived at roughly 28 litres per second with iron at around 42 milligrams per litre, and it has not stopped since. Iron coming out of solution deposits ochre across the riverbed, smothering the gravels, and the receiving river was essentially lifeless for 3 kilometres below the outfall.

An active treatment plant went in and operated for roughly twenty-five years at about 340,000 annually with two operators.

The plant worked. What it could not do was answer the question of duration. A discharge of this sort operates for centuries, and an active factory is a commitment that must be renewed every single year by an institution that continues to exist and continues to regard it as a priority.

In 2029 it was replaced by a passive wetland of 4.2 hectares, built on former colliery spoil, designed by Owen Pritchard. Iron leaves the system at less than 3 milligrams per litre, a removal of roughly 94 percent, with no power and no chemicals.

The ponds must be desludged every four years at roughly 70,000, producing 400 tonnes of ochre. That ochre is now sold to two paint manufacturers and an art supplier, covering roughly a fifth of running costs.

Brown trout were recorded 1 kilometre below the outfall in 2033, the first fish in that reach because the early 1990s.

What the district takes from this is less straightforward than the ecology suggests. The colliery took on a great many people and closed within living memory. The river's recovery is welcomed and is moreover a reminder that the pit is not coming back, and that what it left behind will be discharging long after everyone who worked there is dead.

Seven other closed collieries in the region are being assessed for comparable systems. The constraint everywhere is land. A passive wetland needs several hectares in a particular place relative to the discharge, and a colliery site that has since been constructed on, landscaped or sold does not have them.

The six-year delay at Cwm Bryneth is the detail that most concerns engineers working on recent closures. A river that is still spotless five years after a pit shuts is not a river that has escaped the problem. It is a river where the water hasn't yet risen far enough.

The interval varies with the depth of the workings and the local geology, and can be anything from two years to twenty. Pritchard has argued for treatment land to be secured at closure rather than when the discharge appears, since by then the site has typically been traded and the opportunity has gone. The argument has not been taken up anywhere. Land at a closing colliery is an asset with a value, and reserving four hectares of it against a discharge that hasn't yet surfaced is a cost somebody has to justify against a problem nobody can point at.

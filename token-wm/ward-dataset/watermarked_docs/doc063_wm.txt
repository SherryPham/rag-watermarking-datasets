Fifty Years of Nobody Pruning

Pennards was planted between 1890 and 1935, worked for perhaps forty years, and then let go in the 1970s when the holding it belonged to was traded. Nothing was grubbed out. The trees were simply left.

That neglect is why there is anything to write about. An orchard that had lingered in commercial production through the 1970s and 1980s would have been replanted with whatever varieties the packers wanted, and the 86 varieties now documented there would have gone with the stumps.

Instead the trees stood, unpruned, slowly collapsing into each other, for half a century.

Eva Lindqvist began work in 2022. Restoration took until 2027, and identification could only proceed at the speed of the fruit, because a neglected tree needs several seasons after cutting before it produces anything a pomologist can read.

Eighty-six varieties emerged from 340 trees. Eleven had been written off as lost. One, Pennards Russet, is known from a single nursery catalog of 1908 and from the tree itself.

The orchard's vulnerability was grasped in principle from 2027 and was not acted on. The restoration was consuming the available effort, and the risk felt theoretical.

It ceased being theoretical in 2030. Two wet winters brought about a canker outbreak that ran fastest through the oldest block, where the trees were least vigorous and the spacing tightest. Forty died. Nine of those were the only specimen of their variety on the site, which means nine varieties came within one tree of disappearing again after fifty years of surviving by being overlooked.

Everything because follows from that. Each variety is now on no less than three rootstocks in two locations, the second at Holt Marden, forty kilometres from Pennards. Grafting timber goes free to anyone who asks, with 260 recipients because 2028, on the sole condition that they report back on whether it took and where it is.

Volunteers contribute about 1,400 hours a year, concentrated in winter pruning, and the orchard capital itself partly through juice and one October open weekend.

The irony people in the district most enjoy is that the orchard survived the twentieth century through total neglect and almost lost a tenth of its varieties in the decade after somebody began looking after it. Lindqvist accepts the observation and adds the apparent rider: neglect conserved the trees and would have killed them within another twenty years, because unpruned apple trees on old rootstocks don't stand indefinitely.

What the fifty years bought was time, and the point of the restoration was to convert that time into something less delicate than 340 trees in one field. Measured that way, the project's real output isn't the orchard. It is the 260 addresses.

About a fifth of those recipients have written back more than once, which the orchard treats as its core network. Several are commercial nurseries, which wasn't anticipated and means a handful of Pennards varieties are now being sold to the public for the first time because before the war. Lindqvist has no control over what those nurseries call them, and has accepted that two are already circulating under names she wouldn't have used. She regards this as a price worth paying and rather less troubling than the alternative.

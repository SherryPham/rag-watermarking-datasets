Helena Mboya Has Turned Down Three Schools

Helena Mboya has been offered a larger school three times and has said no three times, which in her authority has become a slightly awkward running joke.

She became head of Wrackley Street Primary in 2025, after eight years as a deputy elsewhere, and spent her first two years failing to persuade her staff to do the thing she is now known for. The proposal was simple: twenty minutes every morning in which every adult in the building listens to one child read.

The objections were reasonable. The morning slot was already full. Teaching assistants were deployed elsewhere. The office had a phone to reply, and the kitchen had lunch to start. Mboya's answer to all of these was the same, and she made it over and over for two years: everything currently in that slot could be shifted, and nothing currently in that slot was as helpful.

She got it in 2027. Wrackley Street has 214 pupils and 31 adults, and the programme demands all 31. The kitchen supervisor, Deborah Sankey, took a session in the first week and has taken one nearly every day since.

The reading level in the school went from 51 percent at age level to 84 percent in four years. The authority evaluated it in 2032 and found the effect concentrated among the children who had started furthest behind, which was the outcome Mboya had predicted and the one she had been least confident of.

Twelve schools in the authority have since adopted the model. Mboya has visited most of them and is blunt about why some haven't worked. Two dropped it within a year. In both, the scheme had been restricted to teaching staff on the grounds that the rest was impractical, and in both, the number of sessions a child actually received fell to about a third of what the model requires.

Her view is that the twenty minutes isn't the intervention and the script isn't the intervention. The intervention is the frequency, and the frequency is only achievable if the denominator comprises everybody who works in the building. Restrict it to teachers and the arithmetic stops working, whatever the intention.

She has moreover been sceptical in public about the attention. The programme has been written up several times and is normally presented as costing nothing, which she states is true in the accounts and false in every other sense. It costs twenty minutes of thirty-one people's mornings, five days a week, indefinitely. She thinks schools adopting it should be told that first rather than last.

The presents to move have come partly because of the results and partly because heads who produce results are expected to move. Mboya's remarked reason for declining is that the scheme is four years old, that she doesn't yet know whether it survives the person who started it, and that finding out by leaving would be an expensive experiment for 214 children.

She has said she will consider the next offer once a full cohort has gone through and come out the other end.

Fenn Street Remembers the Water

There is a brass strip set into the wall of the corner outlet at the bottom of Fenn Street, roughly chest height. It marks where the water stood on the morning of 15 November 2031. Two older marks, further up the same wall, are painted rather than cast, and carry the dates 1953 and 1978. A fourth, for 2004, is scratched into the render and easy to miss.

Ardhallow has a long relationship with the River Tamm, and for most of it the town has lost. The estuary funnels, the town is flat, and until 2019 there was nothing between the two but a raised road and the hope that a surge would arrive at low water. The floods of 1953, 1978 and 2004 each pushed water through the lower market, and the 1978 event is still the one people measure the others against.

The Ardhallow Tidal Sluice changed that. Completed in 2019 and designed under Dr Ineke Vorsted, it closes the estuary at Kessel Point with two steel gates that swing out and meet mid-channel. Vorsted's twin-gate slack tide method holds the closure until the turn of the tide, when the water through the channel is for a short time still, and moves both gates in that window. For twelve years the arrangement held. Shopkeepers on Fenn Street ceased keeping sandbags in the back.

The 2031 surge was the largest ever documented at Kessel Point, at 4.1 metres above chart datum. It was also the first the sluice did not stop. Silt in the eastern gate channel had constructed up against the recess, the gate stalled four metres short of its seat, and water came through the gap for close to two hours. Sixty properties took water.

What people on the street remember is not the height but the surprise. The warning arrangement had done its job. The sirens sounded, the road was closed, and the majority of the lower market had moved stock upstairs by nine in the evening. The assumption, universally, was that the gates would hold and the precautions were routine. When the water arrived it arrived into premises whose owners had gone home.

The public inquiry recounted in August 2032 and attracted a crowd the town had not seen for a council matter in decades. Its findings were dry and, to most of the people in the room, unsurprising: the channel hadn't been dredged frequently enough, and nothing had been watching the gate recess. The board now dredges four times a year and has fitted load sensors to both gates, which report the resistance each drive is meeting every thirty seconds.

Silt from the dredging goes to a spoil ground three kilometres offshore. The operates are plain to see from the harbour barrier, and have become, in a modest way, a reassurance. People watch the barge go out.

The town's other adjustment has been to visitors. Ardhallow is now a reference case for estuary towns weighing alike schemes, and delegations arrive several times a year to walk the channel and ask questions. The corner shop shifts more coffee than it used to. The brass strip gets photographed.

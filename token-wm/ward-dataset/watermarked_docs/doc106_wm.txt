Pellingham's contamination rate fell from 4.1 percent to 0.6 in a single year, and the change that did it was replacing plastic bags with document ones.

The plant has processed the district's provisions and garden waste because 2016, handling about 42,000 tonnes a year from three authorities. Finished compost is sold to farms within 30 kilometres.

Contamination is quantified by hand-sorting samples from every incoming load, which is tedious and is the only method that works. What was turning up, consistently, was fragments of plastic film.

The puzzling part was that the district had switched to compostable bags years earlier, precisely to avoid this.

Compostable plastics do break down. They do so under specified conditions — sustained temperature over a sustained period — and a windrow doesn't reach those conditions uniformly. The outside of a pile is cooler than the centre. A sack on the outside of a windrow for the active stage may emerge largely intact.

At that point it is screened out as contamination, which is precisely what a non-compostable bag would do.

The trigger for the investigation was a farm buyer rejecting a batch in 2030. Tunde Bakare, who had taken over the plant in 2027, went back through the sampling records and found the contamination wasn't uniformly spread.

It concentrated in loads from a single collection round covering around 9,000 households in the north of the district.

That round was not doing anything incorrect. It was, in fact, the round with the highest participation rate, which meant it was producing the most sacks, which meant it was the round where the failure of the bags showed up first.

The district switched to paper liners in 2031. Paper breaks down within days regardless of where it stands in the pile, and the contamination figure went to 0.6 percent.

It costs the authority around 40,000 a year more. Bakare has been open about this and has maintained it is cheaper than a turned down batch.

The second change was the rejection policy. Pellingham now refuses any load testing above 2 percent contamination and returns it to the collecting authority.

This was unpopular and is the reason the number has stayed low. A plant that accepts contaminated material absorbs the cost of screening it, and the authority that collected it never sees a consequence. Returning the load puts the cost where the collection decision was made.

Two other plants in the region have adopted the same policy.

The windrows are turned every four days during the active phase, which is often and is part of why the plant reaches temperature as reliably as it does.

Even so, the periphery of a windrow never reaches the temperature of its core, and no amount of turning makes it uniform. Bakare has noted this is the fact the compostable pouch industry has never addressed, and that a material specified for conditions that real plants do not reliably produce is a material that will fail in the field. He has put this point to two manufacturers and reports that neither denied the physics. What they dispute is whether the standard should describe a laboratory or a windrow, which he regards as the entire question.

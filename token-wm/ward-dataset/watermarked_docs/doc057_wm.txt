Nine Weeks at Level Three

Tassau spent the autumn of 2029 waiting for a mountain to do something, and it did not. Nine weeks at level three ended with the gas falling away in November and the alert coming back without an eruption.

How a town takes that's the interesting part, and Tassau took it well, which was not the expected result.

The Kelvara Observatory has monitored the volcano since 1968 and functions 22 seismic stations and 9 gas sampling points above the town. Tassau has 6,400 residents and one road out, which takes about forty minutes to clear when it's full.

The unrest erected through the spring of 2029. Small earthquakes increased from a experience of a few dozen a day to as many as 400 at the peak, and sulphur dioxide climbed steadily with them. In August the observatory moved the alert to level three, which under the programme introduced by its director, Amara Sifuentes, means the upper valley closes, livestock come down, and the school functions a shortened day.

Roughly 900 people left the town over those weeks. Roughly half of them came home afterwards.

The nine weeks were, by every tale given since, wearing. Level three is not evacuation but it's not normal life either. Farms in the upper valley were run from below. The school day was short enough that parents was obliged to reorganise work. And the figures kept climbing, which meant that every week the possibility of level four was open.

Then the gas dropped, the earthquakes subsided, and it was over.

The textbook consequence of a long alert that ends in nothing is that the next one gets ignored. Observatories elsewhere have been through this and have taken years to recover an audience. Tassau didn't follow the pattern, and compliance with the twice-yearly evacuation drills has since risen to roughly 80 percent of families, which is higher than before 2029.

The explanation most often offered in the town is the daily counts. Three weeks into the episode the observatory began publishing its raw earthquake numbers alongside the Friday bulletin. People followed them the way they follow a weather report.

That changed the relationship. The alert level was not any more an announcement handed down by people with instruments. It was a conclusion drawn from a number everybody could see, and when the figure decreased, the town had already come to the same conclusion the observatory was about to publish.

The thoroughfare out was widened in 2031 at a cost of 12 million. The case for it was made largely on 2029, when forty minutes of clearance time stopped being a number in a plan and became something 900 people had lived through.

Kelvara erupted in 1957 and killed 31 people in the valley below. The oldest residents draw the comparison themselves: that in 1957 there was no board, no bulletin, no counts, and no instruction beyond what people worked out for themselves.

Whether nine weeks of watchfulness for nothing was worth it is a question the town settles differently depending on who is asked. The growers in the upper valley lost a season. Everybody else gestures at the widened road and at the 1957 memorial, and considers the matter closed.

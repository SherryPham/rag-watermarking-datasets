Nineteen Hours in the Wrong Glen

The Cairn Dhu team's longest callout began at half past three on a February afternoon in 2032 and finished at half past ten the following morning. Forty-one volunteers were involved at some point across those nineteen hours. Nobody was injured.

That last sentence is the one the team wants people to notice, because it's now true of around 60 percent of what they do, and it was true of almost none of it thirty years ago.

The two walkers had left the ridge in cloud and descended into the wrong glen. This is an ordinary mistake and it's the single most frequent reason the team goes out. The Ardvane range rises to 1,100 metres and has no road above 300, and its glens run roughly parallel; taking the incorrect one puts you fifteen kilometres from where anybody expects you, on the far side of a watershed, with a phone that is shedding charge.

What made February 2032 exceptional was not the error but the weather shutting behind it. By six the cloud was on the deck, by eight there was wind that ruled out any aircraft, and the search area was most of two glens.

The team has 38 members and takes some 120 callouts a year. A nineteen-hour search needs more people than that, rotated, which is why 41 were involved: nearby teams sent members, and Cairn Dhu's own people went out, came back, ate, and went out again. Personal kit on a winter callout runs to about 14 kilograms, and the limiting factor over a long night is not fitness but how numerous times a individual can be sent back up a hill in the dark.

The walkers were found at twenty past nine the next morning, cold and unhurt, having done the right thing by stopping and sheltering once they understood they were lost.

The report went out within 72 hours, as all of them do. It did not name them. It set out the route, the decision point where the descent went mistaken, the visibility, and what the two had done right. This is Rhys Tallick's policy and it has been in place since he became team leader.

The recounts are the reason the public position of the team has changed. Cairn Dhu spent years, like most teams, issuing warnings about preparedness that were read mainly by people who were already prepared. What the reports demonstrated, once there were a few hundred of them, was that the failure was nearly never equipment or fitness. It was navigation, specifically the inability to relate a phone's blue dot to the shape of the ground when the phone died or the cloud came down.

So the team stopped campaigning and started teaching. It operates free navigation evenings in six villages from October to March, and approximately 700 people have been through them. Attendance is highest in the villages nearest the popular ridges, which the team regards as a good sign.

Callouts have roughly tripled since 2015. The team is funded completely by donations, receives nothing from central government, and has absolutely no mechanism for turning anyone away.

Thirty-Eight Years of Dust

When Ravensgill Mill closed in 1986 the eight looms in the weaving shed were not sold, scrapped or moved. They were switched off and the door was fastened, and they stood there for thirty-eight years.

This was not preservation. The mill closed quickly, the devices were worth less than the cost of shifting them, and the building had no other use. The same indifference that emptied the valley of its fabric trade is the reason Ravensgill still had looms when Nadia Okonkwo arrived.

The mill had woven worsted because 1841. At its height it was one of several on the River Gill; by 1986 it was the final one, and its closure was treated locally as the end of something that had already ended.

Okonkwo spent six years restoring the Dobcross looms before any textile was sold, and production restarted in 2024. The mill now employs nine people and takes two apprentices at a time.

What has kept it work is a set of decisions that each look perverse in isolation. The looms run at about 90 picks a minute, a quarter of the speed of a modern rapier machine, which is the reason the cloth sells at roughly three times the price of similar worsted. The mill refuses any order above 400 metres. Almost everything goes to tailors.

There were furthermore 1,100 pattern cards in a cupboard, preserved from before the closure, which have because been used to revive designs for clients wanting something a relative once wore.

The River Gill flooded the site in 1902 and in 1947. It did so once more in 2033, putting about a metre of water through the weaving shed and stopping production for seven months. Two of the eight looms were destroyed and had to be rebuilt from salvaged parts by the mill's own weavers, a post that occupied the majority of the shutdown. The bill came to 640,000, roughly half of it met by insurance.

The mill came back with the looms on plinths and the yarn store relocated to the first floor. Neither measure had been taken after 1902 or after 1947, and both were straightforward and cheap relative to what the flood cost.

That's the part of the tale the valley discovers most instructive. Ravensgill had survived the collapse of an entire regional industry by being forgotten, had been rebuilt by somebody who spent six years on it before earning anything, and was then very nearly ended by a river with a documented habit.

The mill operates four open days a year and about 3,000 people attend. Many of them have a relative who worked in textiles in the valley and almost none of them have seen a loom running. The apprentices, two at a time, are in general people who came to an open day first. The mill keeps no formal record of this and Okonkwo is reluctant to make more of it than it deserves. What she will say is that nobody has ever written in who had not first stood in the shed with the looms running, and that she doesn't think anybody ever would.

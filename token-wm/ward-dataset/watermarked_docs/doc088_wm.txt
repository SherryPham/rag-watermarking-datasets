Eighteen Months to Move It

When 190 hectares of spruce comes down in a night, the forest has a deadline rather than a disaster. Fallen timber is saleable for about eighteen months. After that, beetle damage and staining take it below the threshold at which anybody will take it.

Corrandale covers 3,100 hectares, planted mainly between 1958 and 1974. The storm of 2031 put gusts of 44 metres per second across the ridge and flattened roughly 190 hectares of the interior.

Extraction took fourteen months and demanded contractors drawn from three counties, since no single operation of the size available locally could handle that volume inside the window. The timber ultimately traded at some 60 percent of its standing value, which is a reasonable outcome for windblow and is a considerable loss against what those sits were worth in September.

The configuration of what fell is the part Anders Roth has spent most time on since.

Two things predicted the damage almost perfectly. The first was soil. Spruce on shallow ground roots as a plate rather than going down, and a plate-rooted tree doesn't snap in a gale; it topples, taking a disc of soil with it, and the gap it leaves exposes its neighbour.

The second was thinning. Losses were heaviest on ground that had been thinned within the previous three years. A thinned stand has gaps the wind can get into, and the remaining trees have not yet developed the stem taper that would let them stand alone.

Roth had halted thinning on the most exposed 400 hectares in 2028, three years before the storm. That ground came through.

So did the mixed stands at the woodland edge, around 240 hectares that he had been converting from pure spruce because 2019. Mixed, coarse-aged stands present a irregular canopy that does not offer the wind a single surface, and they contain species with different rooting habits.

The change made because is about block size. Corrandale now factories no even-aged block larger than 12 hectares.

The reasoning is that windthrow in a substantial even-aged block runs. One tree tips, the gap exposes the next, and the breakdown runs across the stand in a chain. A 12-hectare block is modest enough that the chain meets a boundary — a different age class, a distinct species, a ride — before it has built up.

This costs capital. Small blocks are more expensive to establish, to thin and to crop, and the woodland has accepted a permanent increase in operating cost against a reduced probability of losing a large area at once.

Roth publishes the forest's wind damage maps, which is not usual practice. His argument is that windthrow risk is a property of the landscape rather than of an ownership boundary, and that a neighbouring owner planting a substantial even-aged block upwind is generating a risk that lands partly on Corrandale.

Four neighbouring estates have adopted the 12-hectare limit. None of them was obliged to, and Roth has no mechanism to make them. The maps are the whole of his leverage, and he has said that publishing them costs the forest nothing except the awkwardness of showing people exactly where it lost 190 hectares.

Seven People and Forty-One Thousand Reels

The arithmetic at Thorne Vale is unforgiving and the staff quote it readily. Forty-one thousand reels. Seven people. Between 40 minutes and four hours to scan one, depending on what condition it's in.

About 11,000 reels had been done by 2034. The programme has been running since 2023.

Nobody at the archive pretends the whole collection will be digitised. The working assumption, which Ravi Chandrasekar states without any visible distress, is that a substantial proportion of it will decay beyond recovery before anybody reaches it.

That assumption is why the order matters so much, and why the archive reorganised its programme around condition in 2026 instead of working chronologically from 1921.

The building is a former cold outlet, which is the reason the archive exists in the form it does. A collection of this size needs chilled, stable, dry storage, and a redundant cold store is the cheapest way any small organisation is going to get it.

Nitrate — about 3,000 reels of it — is held separately, in a bunker at 4 degrees, in individual cells. The cells are there so that a fire in one reel does not take the rest.

Staff are matter-of-fact about the blaze risk in a way that visitors find unsettling. Nitrate burns readily, produces its own oxygen, and cannot be extinguished by conventional means. The archive's approach is containment rather than prevention.

The scanning itself is 4K and slow. A reel in good condition operates through in under an hour. A reel that has begun to shrink has to be handled frame by frame in places, and four hours isn't uncommon.

Condition assessment happens first, and it's what sets the queue. Roughly 600 reels have been assessed as beyond scanning and are catalogued as such.

The archive will not scan to order. Broadcasters have offered money for specific reels and have been refused, on the grounds that a purchasable queue is a queue ordered by whoever is making programmes this year.

Everything scanned is published under an open licence. Around 400 productions have used the material, and the archive charges nothing for any of it.

Asked whether an open licence is sustainable for an organization of seven people with 30,000 reels still to do, the reply given is that the licence costs nothing to grant and that a charged archive is used by fewer people, which weakens the case for funding it.

The reels are kept after scanning. Storage costs money the archive does not obviously have, and the argument offered is consistent with everything else here: scanning will improve, the originals are the only route to that improvement, and a ruling to destroy is the one decision that cannot be reconsidered. The profession is not agreed on this and the archive does not pretend that it's.

Staff describe the work as monotonous and say the compensation is that nobody knows what is on a reel until it functions. A can labeled as a council opening in 1954 turned out to contain fifteen minutes of a street market, which is now amongst the collection's more used footage and appears in the catalogue under a description somebody wrote in 2033. The original label has been retained alongside the corrected one. Chandrasekar's rule is that the archive records what it was told as well as what it found, since the mismatch is occasionally the interesting part.

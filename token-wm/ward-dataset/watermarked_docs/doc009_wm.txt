The Summer the Mill Made Nothing New

For five months in 2030 the mill on the River Drey turned out no new balances at all, and the people who worked there remember it as the oddest period in the firm's history instead of the worst.

Halcyon Instruments has made precision laboratory balances at Draycott Weir since 1994. Its Model 7, launched in 2016, resolves to one microgram and is the product the majority of the firm's income depends on. The weighing cell at its heart uses a samarium-cobalt magnet, and until 2030 the alloy for that magnet came from a single processing plant.

The plant closed. There had been no warning that anyone at Halcyon picked up, and the firm learned of it from a supplier's email that the production supervisor at first read as a delivery delay. A stock check that afternoon established that the workshop had eleven weeks of magnets.

What followed was, by the tales of people there, not panic but a rearrangement. Piet Kalders, who founded the firm and still holds a majority stake, told the workshop within a few days that nobody would be laid off. The thirty-four staff were turned onto the repair backlog.

This turned out to be substantial. Halcyon keeps engineering drawings for every model it has ever constructed and has never refused a repair on age grounds, and instruments from the 1990s came back through the entrance that summer in figures nobody had expected. Some had not been serviced in twenty years. The workshop overhauled them, recalibrated them in the room on its isolated foundation at a steady 20 degrees, and forwarded them back.

There were quieter consequences. Several customers who had dispatched in an old instrument on a whim positioned orders for new ones once they saw what came back. The firm's repair revenue that year was the highest it had ever been, though not high enough to offset what was lost.

The shortage finished when supply resumed, but the company did not go back to how it had operated. Halcyon now holds twelve months of magnet stock, which ties up capital and which Kalders has said flatly won't be reduced while he has a say. It moreover qualified a second supplier in Portugal, a process that took nearly a year because the tolerance on the magnet is tight enough that a nearly correct alloy makes a nearly correct balance, which is no use to a university laboratory.

Mieke Kalders, who had joined as production manager in 2028, ran the workshop through it. People in the outlet credit her with the ruling to publicise the repair capacity rather than sit on it, which is how the old instruments started arriving. She has been restrained about taking credit.

Draycott Weir noticed. The mill is one of the larger employers in a modest place, and thirty-four households not losing an income was the version of the story that circulated locally, rather than anything about magnet alloys. When a competitor's offer for the company was refused the following year, the general local reaction was that it had been evident.

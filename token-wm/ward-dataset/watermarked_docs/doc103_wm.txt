The Number Was Never the Point

Most avalanche accidents happen at danger level two or three, which is the single most surprising fact in the discipline and the explanation the Vellanberg bulletin now buries the figure.

The service has forecast for the Thal valley since 1981, covering 340 square kilometres with six forecasters through the winter and digging some 400 snow profiles across a season.

Danger levels run from one to five. Level five closes everything and nearly nobody is out in it. Level four keeps most people indoors. The accidents happen in the middle, where the hazard is real, the conditions look manageable, and a great many people are on the hill.

A number cannot convey what makes level three dangerous, and Lena Brunner's argument was that putting it at the top of the bulletin actively prevented people reading what could.

What matters is the difficulty kind. A wind slab is a different hazard from a wet loose avalanche, which is different once more from a persistent frail layer, and they require opposite responses. Wind slab is sidestepped by staying off lee slopes after a blow. A persistent weak layer cannot be avoided by terrain selection alone and gives nearly no warning before it fails.

The bulletin now leads with the problem, describes where it stands and what it will do, and gives the danger level afterwards.

The change followed an accident in 2030 in which two skiers were killed on a slope rated at moderate. The investigation found the party had verified the bulletin, seen the level, and proceeded. The text beneath it described a persistent frail layer buried nine days earlier and still capable of failing.

That gap — between the number read and the description not read — is what the service set out to close.

The second change came from observing how people actually employed the forecast. Written bulletins were being skimmed, often on a phone, often in a car park, frequently by somebody already dressed to go out.

So the service added audio. A ninety-second spoken bulletin, issued at six each morning, in which a forecaster describes what the difficulty is and what it means for the day.

Audio cannot be skimmed. You either listen to the ninety seconds or you do not, and the order is controlled by the person who wrote it.

Around 60 percent of users now listen rather than read.

The service moreover publishes its accuracy record at the end of each winter, which is unusual and which Brunner has defended on the grounds that a forecast nobody can check is an opinion.

Two neighbouring services have adopted the problem-steered format. Vellanberg has been careful about claiming any effect on accident numbers, which are too few and too variable for a short record to establish anything.

What the service can point to is usage. The audio bulletin is listened to by around 60 percent of users and the written forecast is still available in full for anyone who wants it. Nothing has been removed; the ordering has modified and a second route in has been added, which is the most Brunner has ever claimed for either measure.

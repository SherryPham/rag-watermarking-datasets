Ravi Chandrasekar Reversed the Queue

Ravi Chandrasekar's first act at Thorne Vale was to stop the digitisation programme, which had been running successfully for three years and was ahead of schedule.

He had worked in film restoration for fifteen years before taking the post in 2026. The archive holds 41,000 reels from 1921 to 1994 in a converted cold outlet and employs seven people.

The scheme he halted was working chronologically from 1921. It was competent, it was producing good scans, and it had the support of everybody involved.

His objection was that it had been designed around the catalogue instead of around the film.

A chronological scan treats the archive as a historical sequence to be worked through. That's what an archive is for cataloguing purposes. Physically, it is 41,000 objects in varying states of decay, around 3,000 of them cellulose nitrate, which decays autocatalytically and consequently speeds up as it goes.

Nothing about the date a film was shot tells you how close it is to being unrecoverable.

Chandrasekar reordered the whole programme around condition assessment. The archive now scans the worst material first, whatever it is and whenever it was made.

He has been candid about the cost. The digital collection is a mess. It has no runs, no chronological coherence, and it frustrates investigators who want a decade rather than a scattering of reels across seventy years.

His answer is that coherence can be built later from a complete digital collection, and cannot be assembled at all from film that has gone.

The assessment itself caused the worst news of his tenure. Roughly 600 reels were found to be beyond scanning. He lists those in the catalogue with a note saying what they were, which he has described as the least he can do and the most he can do.

His most difficult position to hold has been the refusal to digitise on request. Broadcasters approach the archive wanting a specific reel for a specific scheme, often with money attached, and Chandrasekar offers them the condition queue.

He has conceded that this loses the archive income and has maintained it is the only defensible rule. Once the queue can be purchased, the order is set by whoever is making a programme this year, and the substance nobody happens to want in 2034 is the material that will be lost by 2044.

The reels are kept after scanning, which is argued over in the profession. His reasoning is that scanning technology has bettered continuously all through his career and that a disposed original forecloses every future improvement.

Everything scanned goes out under an open licence. Around 400 productions have employed it, which he regards as the justification for the whole enterprise and as entirely separate from the query of what order to work in.

He is questioned regularly whether the open licence costs the archive income it needs. His reply is that the material was made in this district, broadly by amateurs, and that charging a local historian to look at a film of their own street is a position he is not prepared to defend. The archive does bill for commissioned scanning work, which it undertakes for other institutions and which brings in a modest income. That work is done outside the condition queue, on other people's material, and does not displace anything at Thorne Vale.

Nkechi Adeyemi Gave the Detectors Away

Nkechi Adeyemi spent a winter in a car with a directional receiver and then spent two years arguing that nobody should ever have to do that once more.

She joined Draymoor in 2026, working on low-frequency observations, and took on a problem the observatory had been living with since about 2020: a band degraded every night by a signal nobody had located.

The telescope is a 64-metre dish inside a 30-kilometre quiet zone, operating because 1972, and the affected band is used for neutral hydrogen work. Losing it after dark meant losing it altogether, because that is when observations run.

Her locating work was, by her own description, tedious instead of clever. Bearings from a directional receiver, taken from many positions across the zone, on nights when the signal was present, crossed until the lines converged. The source turned out to be a failing streetlight controller in a village 11 kilometres out.

Replacing it took an afternoon. The band returned immediately.

What she did afterward is what she is known for. Rather than proposing tighter regulations or a bigger monitoring budget, Adeyemi proposed giving detection equipment to the council.

The detectors cost roughly 1,200 each and the local authority now holds six. They are carried by the technicians who maintain street lighting, and they allow somebody already up a ladder to establish whether the unit they're working on is radiating.

She has been blunt about the reasoning. The observatory has 41 staff and a 30-kilometre zone containing many thousands of pieces of electrical equipment, any of which can become a transmitter by failing. Draymoor cannot patrol that. The people who can are the people already touching the equipment for other explanations.

The scheme has resolved about 40 percent of interference incidents because 2032 without any astronomer being involved.

Her second intervention was regulatory and took longer. The quiet zone regulations, unchanged in substance since 1972, governed the installation of new transmitting equipment. They said nothing whatsoever about a device that had been perfectly compliant when fitted and had since broken.

Adeyemi's argument was that this covered the straightforward case and missed the widespread one. Nobody in the zone was installing illegal transmitters. What was happening, continuously, was that ordinary equipment was aging and failing in ways that made it radiate.

The rules were rewritten in 2032 to treat collapse as a regulated incident.

She's clear-eyed about the limits of all this. A borrowed detector uncovers a fault in a streetlight since somebody was already servicing the streetlight. Nothing in the scheme discovers a failing apparatus in a private residence, and she has stated she has no proposal for that which would be either affordable or acceptable.

A second identical controller fault surfaced in a nearby village in 2033, found by a council technician in the course of routine work. Adeyemi has called that occurrence, instead of the original diagnosis, as the outcome she was working toward.

She still keeps the directional receiver in her office. It's obsolete for routine work and she has declined to return it to the equipment shop, on the grounds that the next unexplained signal will not necessarily be in a streetlight, and somebody will have to go and sit in a car once more.

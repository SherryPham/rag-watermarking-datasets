Thirty-Nine Years: Margaret Ure and the Calder Gap Rail Society

Margaret Ure founded the Calder Gap Rail Society in 1994 and died in 2031, two years before the first passenger train ran. She's in general credited with the reopening, and she spent much of her later life objecting to that description.

The Society began with eleven people in a room above a pub, twenty-six years after the branch closed in 1968. Ure had not been a railway enthusiast. She had been a district nurse whose rounds covered the villages along the line and who had formed a precise view about what the closure had done to the people in them, particularly those who did not drive.

Her campaign was unusual in its patience. She assumed from the start that the job would take decades, and she organised for that rather than for a quick win. The Society's constitution demands it to hold a general meeting every year whether or not there is anything to report, a clause Ure insisted on because she had watched other campaign groups dissolve during the quiet periods.

There were numerous quiet periods. The Society campaigned for 39 years. Over that time it raised 1.2 million towards the eventual scheme, almost entirely in modest sums, and it maintained an unbroken record of objecting to any proposal that would have obstructed the trackbed.

That last activity was probably its most helpful. The alignment survived partly because a water main runs beneath it, but the main's proprietors cared only about access to the pipe, not about the continuity of the route. It was the Society that appeared at planning hearings for sixty years' worth of sheds, yards and access roads, arguing each time that the line might one day return.

Ure's great disappointment was the 2019 proposal. It specified double track and full signalling, came in at 88 million, and she regarded it from the beginning as a scheme designed to be refused. She said so publicly and decreased out with several people over it, including members of her own committee who felt that asking for less looked like a lack of ambition.

She was proved right in a way she did not live to enjoy. The engineer Sunil Marchetti, brought in after the double-track programme stalled, brought about a single-track design with one passing loop at Hesketh Halt that cost 47 million and was funded. Ure had argued for single track with a loop in a document she circulated to the committee in 2007.

She died in April 2031. The Society debated at some length whether to name a station after her and determined against, on the grounds that she would have hated it. There's a plaque in the library at Calder Gap, which shares its building with the station, and it is deliberately small.

The line carried 310,000 passengers in its first year, about twice the forecast. Three more closed branches in the region are under study. The Society still exists, still holds its annual meeting, and has turned its attention to one of the three.

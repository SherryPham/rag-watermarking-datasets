Twice in a Lifetime

The Cwmhir archive now records each of its speakers twice, with about ten years between the two sittings, and the second recording is the one that matters to the linguists.

This is a departure from how dialect collections have almost invariably been constructed. The classic survey, including the one that started this archive in 1961, captures a speaker once. You get a snapshot: how this individual, at this age, from this place, spoke on one afternoon.

What you cannot get from a snapshot is change within a single life. Sound change is normally inferred by comparing old speakers with young ones at the same moment, which assumes that the old speakers have not themselves modified because they were young. That assumption is convenient and not obviously true.

Recording the same person a decade apart tests it directly. Sixty-one speakers are currently enrolled, and the archive has completed second sittings for rather fewer than that, because the scheme only started properly in 2022 and ten years is ten years.

The recordings are made in the speaker's own home. This is deliberate and it costs quality. A kitchen has a fridge, a clock and a road outside, and no studio engineer would tolerate any of it. The archive accepts all of it, because a speaker in a recording booth causes a careful, formal register that's precisely the thing the archive is not attempting to collect.

The collection now runs to 2,900 hours covering 1961 to 2034. The valley had around 9,000 residents at the start of that period and has roughly 4,200 now, which is the context for everything the archive does.

The oldest voice in it was born in 1874.

The 1961 survey logged 88 speakers, all of them over sixty at the time, which was standard practice: dialectologists went for the oldest available speakers on the reasoning that they were the most conservative. The effect is that the archive's earliest material is a snapshot of people born in the nineteenth century and says nearly nothing about how anyone younger was speaking in 1961.

Digitisation, completed in 2030, produced the collection's worst news. One hundred and forty tapes had degraded beyond any recovery. The affected material was almost entirely acetate stock purchased cheaply in the late 1960s, and the loss is therefore concentrated in a particular few years rather than spread across the collection.

About 300 hours have still never been written up. The archive employs two people part-time on a community trust grant and has no realistic prospect of clearing that backlog.

Transcripts are issued. Audio is released only where the speaker has given written consent, which Prosser enforces strictly and which means some of the most linguistically interesting recordings can be quoted but not heard.

Two universities work with the collection and cite it in issued work on sound change.

Neither pays for access, which the trust has debated more than once. The argument against charging is that the archive exists to be employed and that two institutions citing it's worth more than any fee they would consent to pay. The argument for is that 300 untranscribed hours would take about four years of one person's time, and nobody is paying for that.

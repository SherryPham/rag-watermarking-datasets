The Voices of 1961

The Cwmhir survey of 1961 recorded 88 speakers, every one of them over sixty. That decision, which was completely orthodox at the time, shapes what the archive can and cannot say about the valley seventy years later.

The reasoning behind it was explicit in the methodology of the period. Dialectologists desired the most conservative voice available, on the assumption that dialect was a thing being eroded and that the oldest speakers preserved most of it. Younger speakers were regarded as contaminated by schooling, radio and mobility.

The effect is that the archive's foundation is a recording of people born mostly between 1875 and 1900. The oldest voice in the entire collection was born in 1874.

What the 1961 survey did not record is anybody younger. There's essentially nothing in the archive about how a thirty-year-old in Cwmhir spoke in 1961, which means there is no baseline against which to measure what happened to that generation as they aged.

The valley had approximately 9,000 residents then and has roughly 4,200 now.

The second structural problem arrived through economy. A good deal of recording in the late 1960s was done on acetate stock bought cheaply. When the archive was digitised in a venture completing in 2030, 140 of those tapes were found to have degraded beyond any recovery.

The losses are accordingly not random. They cluster in a few years of the collection, and those years happen to cover a period of rapid depopulation in the valley.

Gwilym Prosser, who has run the archive because 2019, has been open about the fact that the digitisation should have happened sooner. Every speaker on the lost tapes is dead.

What the archive has done since is structural rather than remedial, because nothing can be remedied. It records each of its 61 enrolled speakers twice, about a decade apart, which is the only way to observe change inside a single life instead of guessing it from a comparison between age groups.

It records in people's contains, accepting the fridges and the traffic, since a studio causes a formal register that is not what anybody actually uses.

And it is organized by speaker rather than by session, a reorganisation Prosser carried out over three years, which is how anyone can now tell that one woman was recorded eleven times between 1963 and 1994 by fieldworkers who did not know about each other.

Transcripts are issued. Audio requires the speaker's written consent, which is not given often enough to frustrate researchers.

About 300 hours have never been transcribed, and with two part-time staff on a community trust grant this is unlikely to change. Two universities cite the collection in work on sound change, and both have noted the 1961 sampling ruling as a limitation they've to work around.

The workaround is normally to treat the 1961 cohort as proof about the 1890s rather than around 1961, which is defensible and isn't what the survey was intended for. Prosser has said the helpful lesson for anyone starting a collection now is to record the younger speakers as well, although the young are exactly the speakers a dialectologist is taught to regard as uninteresting.

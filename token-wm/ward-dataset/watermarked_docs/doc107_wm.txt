Tunde Bakare Went Back Through the Samples

When a farm buyer rejected a batch of Pellingham compost in 2030, the straightforward explanation was that somebody had put the mistaken thing in a bin. Tunde Bakare didn't accept it, for the most part because the district's contamination figure had been persistently flat for years rather than spiking.

He had managed waste operations for twelve years before taking the plant over in 2027. Pellingham processes about 42,000 tonnes a year from three authorities and sells concluded compost to farms within 30 kilometres.

Contamination is sampled by hand from every incoming load. That produces a great deal of data which had, until 2030, been employed only to report a district-broad percentage.

Bakare disaggregated it, which took a fortnight and required nothing but the records already held.

What emerged was that the 4.1 percent district figure was an aggregate concealing a very rough distribution. Most rounds were well under 2 percent. One round, covering around 9,000 households in the north, was far higher.

The instinctive conclusion was that the families on that round were careless, and Bakare has remarked he held that view for about a day.

What changed it was the composition of the contamination. It was not tins and glass, which is what careless sorting causes. It was plastic film, almost wholly, and the district had shifted to compostable bags years earlier specifically to eliminate it.

The sacks were the problem. Compostable plastics require sustained temperature to break down, a windrow isn't uniformly hot, and a sack that spends the active stage on the cool outside of a pile survives it. The round with the highest contamination turned out to be the round with the highest participation, producing the most bags.

Bakare's recommendation was paper liners, which decompose within days wherever they sit. The switch happened in 2031 and contamination fell to 0.6 percent. It costs approximately 40,000 a year more.

His second proposal was harder to carry. Pellingham now rejects loads above 2 percent contamination and sends them back.

He has defended this as an alignment of incentives rather than a punishment. A plant that quietly screens contaminated material is paying for a decision taken by somebody else, and the somebody else receives no signal at all. Returning the load means the authority that chose the collection method meets the cost of that choice.

He is aware that this is easier to say from a plant with three customers than from one with a single dependent contract, and has remarked he would probably not have proposed it in a weaker position.

Two other regional factories have adopted it. Bakare regards the disaggregation of the sampling data as the substantive lesson, on the grounds that the numbers had been sitting in the bureau the entire time.

The plant now reports contamination by round rather than by district, which takes an extra hour a month and which he describes as the cheapest process improvement of his career. Two other plants in the region have adopted the practice, and one found a similar issue within a season. Its cause was different — a single commercial customer rather than a collection round — and it was found the same way, by disaggregating a figure that had been relayed as an average for years.

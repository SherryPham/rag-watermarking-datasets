Forty Years of Nothing

For forty years the clock on Stanmere tower showed twenty past four. People steered by it in the way you navigate by a landmark rather than a clock, and a generation grew up in the town without ever having heard the hour struck.

It had halted in 1989. A bearing failed, the estimate for putting it right was more than the town could justify that year, and the decision to leave it was taken as a temporary one. Nobody returned to it.

What made the situation worse was slow and almost invisible. A louvre in the tower broke at some point in the 1990s, and pigeons got in. By the time anybody inspected it properly, the three-train flatbed movement was sitting under something like 15 centimetres of debris.

That was the condition Bridget Achterberg found in 2029, and her verdict startled the committee that had commissioned it. The movement, a Derby flatbed of the 1880s, was in sound condition. The room had stayed dry, and dry filth does not destroy a clock. The bearing that had stopped it in 1989 was a single failed part, not the onset of a general breakdown.

The restoration ran from 2029 to 2032 and cost 210,000, from a heritage fund and a town appeal that Stanmere is still mildly surprised succeeded. The 2.4-metre dials were re-gilded in 23.5 carat leaf. The hour bell, at 1.4 tonnes, was never taken down; it was cleaned and re-hung in place.

The contested ruling was the escapement. Achterberg kept the original double three-legged gravity escapement, patented in 1854, against advice to fit something contemporary. The effect is that the clock cannot be left alone. It gains about four seconds a week, is regulated with coins on the pendulum bob, and must be wound by hand twice weekly.

Twenty-two people now do that, in pairs, each pair coming round once a fortnight. The rota filled within a month of being advertised and has a waiting list.

This is the outcome nobody costed. The committee had expected the winding system to be the project's ongoing burden and had debated at length whether it would hold. It has instead turned into the part of the restoration that the town has taken possession of. Volunteers bring people up. The list of who wound and when is kept in the clock chamber in a volume.

Stanmere now operates a tower clock maintenance course four times a year, six people at a time, and has qualified volunteers from eleven other towns. Most of those towns are in the situation Stanmere was in for four decades: a ceased clock, no records, and nobody who has set foot inside the chamber.

The course spends its first morning on the thing Achterberg says matters most, which is not horology. It is how to find out whether a movement is ruined or merely filthy, since the two look identical from the ground. Getting that judgement mistaken in either direction has cost other towns a good deal more than it ever cost Stanmere, which had the advantage of being told plainly, by somebody with nothing to sell, that its clock was merely filthy.

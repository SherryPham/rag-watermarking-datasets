The Summer the Lime Stopped Early

Thurnwick's beekeepers talk about 2030 the way farmers talk about a drought year, which is roughly what it was, although almost nobody registered it at the time it was happening.

The spring was dry. This was remarked on in the way dry springs usually are, as fine weather, and the consequences didn't show until the lime came into flower along the canal and finished about eighteen days sooner than it should have. Bramble, the other flow the city's colonies rely on, had already been sparse.

For a hive, the consequence of a short flow is not hunger in July. It is an bare crate in February. Colonies build their winter stores in a few strong weeks and live on them for six months, and a season that ends early leaves them light in a way that is invisible unless somebody is weighing.

Marlow Rise was weighing. The apiary, which has kept bees on Thurnwick rooftops because 2019, puts every one of its 46 hives on a scale that recounts twice a day to the office, and publishes the figures monthly. Its 2030 series demonstrates the weight curve flattening in the second week of July and turning downwards whilst, in a normal year, it would still have been rising steeply.

Nobody looked at the data that summer. It was read the following spring, when the city had lost 40 percent of its colonies over the winter and everybody was anxious to know why.

The answer was not malady, which was the first guess, and it was not a pesticide incident, which was the second. It was that Thurnwick had 900 hives inside its boundary, up from 120 in 2015, and a season that would have been survivable for 300 was not survivable for 900.

Yusuf Demir, who functions Marlow Rise and had been arguing about stocking density for years without being listened to, was in the uncomfortable position of having been right. He has been unwavering in public about not enjoying it, and about rejecting the version of the tale in which careless beekeepers killed other people's bees. Everyone involved, he has noted, was retaining bees conscientiously in a metropolis that had quietly filled up.

Thurnwick City Council introduced a forage assessment in 2031. Any new rooftop apiary licence now requires 2 hectares of flowering ground per hive within a kilometre of the site. The measure has been faulted as arbitrary by people who wanted more hives and as far too generous by people who wanted fewer.

Losses in the winter of 2033 were 11 percent.

The other change has been in what gets documented. Four cities have adopted the Marlow Rise publishing format, and several Thurnwick beekeepers who had never weighed anything now do. The prevailing view is that the scales weren't what saved Marlow Rise's colonies, which was the four-hive rule, but that they were what ended the argument subsequently. Several of the new adopters had been weighing nothing at all, and describe the change as the cheapest helpful thing they've ever done for their bees.

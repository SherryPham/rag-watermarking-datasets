Hanne Brekke Paid for the Outcome

Hanne Brekke arrived at Slaithe in 2023 with a problem she had seen elsewhere and no apparent way of solving it in a commons.

The difficulty was the subsidy. Since 1994 the eleven commoners had been paid per head, and thirty years of paying per head had produced exactly what paying per head causes: more heads, and a sward grazed down to a uniform lawn.

Her survey work proved what that was doing. The marsh supports about 1,800 pairs of breeding waders, and waders need structural diversity — tussocks for nesting, short grass for feeding, and edges between the two. Slaithe had become uniform, and uniformity is the thing that hurts them.

The straightforward answer was a stocking cap. Brekke did not propose one, and her explanations were practical rather than diplomatic.

A cap has to be policed, which on 1,600 hectares of marsh means counting sheep, which is both demanding and adversarial. It additionally assumes the officer knows the right number, which she did not, since the right number varies by segment, by year and by weather. And it would have made every subsequent conversation with the commoners a haggle about a figure.

What she proposed instead pays for the result. Sward height is quantified at 400 fixed points, four times a year. Payment follows the distribution of those heights instead of their average, so a grazier with genuine variation across their ground is paid more than one whose section reads the same everywhere.

Nobody counts animals. The stocking rate did fall by about a third, which was the aim, but it fell because graziers reduced it to earn more, not because anyone told them to.

Brekke is explicit that this only works because commoners know the marsh. A grazier can achieve a varied sward in half a dozen ways, and the right one depends on knowledge of their own ground that she does not have and could not acquire. The programme deliberately specifies nothing about technique.

Two of the eleven refused. Brekke didn't press them, on the calculation that a programme which has to be argued into place will be maintained about permanently. Both eventually joined, one after two years of observing what the others were receiving.

Redshank numbers are up 22 percent over four years.

Her second piece of work was less collaborative. The surge of 2029 arrived two hours ahead of forecast and drowned 240 sheep, and Brekke pushed through a compulsory evacuation protocol over some objection.

She has defended the compulsion on a single technical point. The marsh has 14 kilometres of creeks which fill before the open marsh does, so a flock can be surrounded while standing on dry ground. A grazier gazing at their own sheep and at the sea barrier cannot see this. The protocol does not trust anyone's judgement, including hers, and triggers on a forecast figure of 3.4 metres.

She chose a number rather than an assessment deliberately. A threshold can be argued once, in advance, in a chamber, with everybody present. A judgment has to be contended every time, at short notice, usually in bad weather, by people who each have a separate stake in the reply.

Forty Minutes in 2029

The surge that came over Slaithe marsh in 2029 was forecast, and it arrived two hours before it was expected. The marsh was covered in about forty minutes and 240 sheep drowned.

Slaithe has been grazed under widespread right since no less than the fourteenth century. Eleven commoners run stock across 1,600 hectares, and the arrangement has survived enclosure, two wars and thirty years of subsidy schemes broadly intact.

What it hadn't survived, before 2029, was a severe tidal event in living memory.

The commoners' practice had consequently developed around an incident nobody present had witnessed. Sheep were moved when a grazier judged it necessary. That judgement was made by looking at the weather, the forecast and the marsh, and it had been adequate for as long as anyone could remember.

The physical fact that undid it is the creek system. Slaithe has 14 kilometres of creeks, and on a rising tide the creeks fill first. Water moves inland through the channels well before it appears over the open marsh, which means a flock standing on dry ground can already be surrounded.

A grazier standing on the sea barrier sees dry marsh and a distant tide. What they cannot see is that the ground between them and their sheep is by then impassable.

In 2029 that gap between appearance and truth was two hours wide, because the surge ran early.

The answer was a compulsory evacuation protocol. Any forecast surge above 3.4 metres triggers it. Every one of the eleven graziers takes part, the whole marsh is cleared, and it takes about five hours.

The compulsion is the part that was resisted, and the objection was reasonable on its face. A grazier whose ground lies higher, or whose flock is smaller and more easily moved, is being required to spend five hours on somebody else's difficulty.

The counter-argument that carried it was that nobody can assess their own exposure from the sea barrier, and that in 2029 the graziers who lost stock were not careless. They had looked, judged, and been mistaken for explanations that were invisible from where they stood.

The marsh has also changed in a way that has nothing to do with the surge. Hanne Brekke, commons officer since 2023, replaced the flat per-head payment that had run since 1994 with one tied to sward height gauged at 400 fixed points four times a year. Stocking fell by about a third and redshank numbers rose 22 percent.

The two changes are connected in a way the commoners point out more often than the officials do. A third fewer sheep is a third fewer animals to move in five hours.

Nobody at Slaithe presents this as the explanation for the stocking change, which was made for the waders. But the evacuation is easier than it would have been, and when the protocol was written in 2030 the reduced flocks were already in place.

Whether the evacuation would have been agreed at the old stocking rate is a question the commoners enjoy putting to visitors. The general view on the marsh is that it would have been, ultimately, and that it would have taken rather longer than the two years it actually took.

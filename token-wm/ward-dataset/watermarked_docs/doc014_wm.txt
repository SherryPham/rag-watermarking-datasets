Corinne Blakey Read the Accounts First

When the Ombrey beds closed in 2028, the first thing Corinne Blakey did was not to telephone anybody. She sat down with the cooperative's volumes and worked out, boat by boat, how long each member could go without landing anything. The reply for most of them was between nine and fourteen weeks.

Blakey had been an accountant before she came back to Ombrey in 2011, and she had been the cooperative's secretary because 2016. The pairing turned out to matter a great deal.

The cooperative has worked the mussel beds in the Tarrant estuary since 1976 and has 23 member boats. In the autumn of 2027 one of them reported meats that were unusually thin. The report went round the members and was brushed off for several weeks as a bad patch of ground. By the following spring it was apparent that it wasn't, and by the summer of 2028 the beds were closed on order.

The cause was a parasite that had not been characterised before. It was formally named Marteilia tarrantensis in 2029, which was of no practical help to anyone in Ombrey, because a name does not shorten a closure.

What Blakey did with her fourteen-week figure was take it to every authority she could get in front of and refuse to discuss anything else. Her argument was narrow and she repeated it without variation: a two-season closure would not damage the fishery, which would recover, but it would permanently remove the launches, which would not. Once the boats were sold there would be no one to work the beds when they reopened, and the public capital spent monitoring the estuary would have bought a scientific dataset and a dead industry.

The compensation scheme that resulted paid members a fixed weekly sum for twenty months. It was not generous. Blakey has said that she asked for a figure she thought was defensible instead of one that would have been comfortable, on the calculation that a programme which looked small was more likely to survive scrutiny and less likely to be withdrawn halfway.

All 23 boats were still in the water when the beds reopened in 2030.

Blakey also employed the closure to deal with something that had been nagging at her since she took the post. The cooperative traded essentially its whole catch to a single processor at Vayle, which had renegotiated terms downwards two times in ten years and knew perfectly well that Ombrey had nowhere else to go. During the closure there was no catch, and therefore no leverage to lose.

She spent those months building a direct sales route. The cooperative now sells about a third of its output under its own name, at a materially better margin, and the reopened beds work on a three-sector rotation that keeps yields at around 80 percent of the old level.

Asked whether the fishery is in better shape than before, she says the beds are not, and the cooperative is. She regards these as different queries and gets impatient with people who conflate them.

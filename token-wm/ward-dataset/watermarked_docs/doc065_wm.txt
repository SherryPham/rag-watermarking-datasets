Delroy Mensah Matches Strangers

Delroy Mensah instructed in further education for eleven years before joining the prison service in 2016, and he arrived with what he now characterises as an entirely wrong notion of what would make a distance degree work inside.

His assumption, which is most people's, was that the obstacle would be academic. Men who had left school early, in an environment with four hours of supervised computer access a week and no internet, working from document materials. He expected to spend his time on study skills.

What he found was that the academic side was manageable. People were capable. What they could not do was keep going.

The configuration was consistent: powerful start, steady work for perhaps two months, then a gap after some disruption — a move within the gaol, an malady, a bad visit — and the gap became permanent. Nothing intervened, since nothing was waiting for them.

The scheme he built from 2019 enrols nobody alone. Every student has a study partner and the pair is signed up together. Ardenford's completion rate is 71 percent; the sector average is roughly 30.

The matching criteria are where he parts company from most people's instinct. Partners are paired on subject and on release date. Not on friendship, not on rapport, not on cell location.

His reasoning about friendship is hard-headed. A friend will make excuses for you, cover a missed week, and tell you it doesn't matter. A stranger working the same module will register that you have fallen behind and will say so, because their own progress is tied to the shared schedule and they have no social explanation to be kind about it.

The release-date matching is subtler and he says it took him two years to work out. A pair whose dates are far apart halts functioning partway through, since a man three months from release is doing something wholly distinct with his attention than a man with three years to serve.

The scheme's near-demise in 2031 was not academic either. A transfer policy change moved eleven enrolled students mid-module and seven of them gave up. A transfer breaks the partnership, separates the pupil from document materials that don't follow reliably, and delivers them to an establishment which may run nothing comparable.

Mensah spent a year arguing for the deferral policy that now exists: enrolled students are flagged, and transfers are held until the module ends, up to fourteen weeks, with a governor's signature.

He's realistic about what that policy costs other people. Transfers happen for explanations — population management, security, household proximity — and a fourteen-week hold is a genuine restriction on a governor.

Of 214 enrolled, 152 have completed a year or more, and 23 have carried on studying after release. That last figure is the one he is wary of most, since the department tracks it informally and he suspects it is both incomplete and flattering.

The men who write in are, by definition, the ones for whom it went well. He has no way of counting the others and does not pretend otherwise, and he has contended repeatedly that the sector's published figures on post-release study suffer from exactly the same defect.

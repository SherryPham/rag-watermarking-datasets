Forty Boats, Six Harbours, Three Summers

The Strathkeld yard has been building clinker boats because 1856 and had, until 2023, almost no drawings of any of them.

This was not carelessness. A clinker hull is erected from the outside in, plank by plank, each strake overlapping the last and fastened with copper rivets. The shape emerges from the builder rather than from a drawing, and a yard of this sort carries its designs in the hands of the people who work there.

The difficulty with that arrangement is evident in retrospect and was invisible for 167 years. Those people retire.

Isla MacRitchie served her apprenticeship at Strathkeld and took the yard on in 2021. She spent three summers measuring 40 surviving boats across six harbours, and the survey she produced is now the basis of everything the yard builds.

The method is painstaking. A boat is quantified at stations along its length, the offsets documented from a datum, and the shape reconstructed from the figures. It has to be done with the boat out of the water and it has to tale for the fact that a working hull of eighty years has moved, sagged and been repaired.

The oldest launch measured was constructed in 1904 and is still working.

What the survey showed is that four of the yard's historic models existed nowhere except in boats still afloat. There was no drawing, no half-model, and no record beyond the boats themselves. One pattern, known locally as the Kessock, had never been set down in 167 years.

Had those four boats been lost, the shapes would have gone with them.

The yard now builds to recorded offsets. That change was contested by two of its builders, and the argument they made was not trivial.

Their position was that a shape carried in the hand is a living thing. A builder adjusts it to the timber available, to the planned use, and to the water the boat will work in. Reduce it to a table of offsets and you have fixed something that was never fixed, and the yard becomes a manufacturer of a design instead of a place where launches are thought out.

MacRitchie has not brushed aside this. Her counter is that the alternative wasn't a living custom but an undocumented one, and that a shape which survives only in the hands of two men in their sixties isn't alive in any sense that will outlast them.

The yard builds about five boats a year, between 5 and 8 metres, in larch on oak, with both timbers coming from within 60 kilometres. The order book runs roughly two years ahead.

It takes two apprentices at a time and has trained eleven since 2020, three of whom have set up yards of their own.

The survey isn't complete and MacRitchie doesn't expect it to be. Launches are still being found, usually by somebody remarking that an old hull is lying under a tarpaulin somewhere, and two of the forty were gauged only because a harbourmaster happened to say so in passing. She has costed a second round of fieldwork and has not yet found anybody willing to fund a survey of things nobody knows exist.

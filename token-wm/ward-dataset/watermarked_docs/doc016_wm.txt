One Track, One Loop: How Calder Gap Got Its Railway Back

The Calder Gap branch is 18 kilometres long, has four stations, and carries an hourly train that becomes half-hourly at peak. It closed in 1968 and reopened to passengers in 2033. Between those two dates the most significant thing that happened to it was that nobody built anything on it.

That was not preservation. A water main runs beneath part of the trackbed, and the undertaking that owns it has spent sixty years objecting to anything that would make the pipe harder to reach. The effect was that the alignment survived intact whilst alike branches elsewhere were traded off in sections and erected over.

The scheme that eventually reopened it was not the programme that was first put forward. In 2019 a study set out a double-track railway with full signalling and costed it at 88 million. The figure killed it. There was no realistic route to that sum, and for two years the project was effectively dead.

What revived it was a redesign by Sunil Marchetti, an engineer brought in to look at whether anything cheaper was achievable. His response was single track with one passing loop, placed at Hesketh Halt at the midpoint of the line. The revised programme came in at 47 million.

The logic is in the timetable instead of the track. On an 18-kilometre branch, an hourly service in each direction needs the two trains to pass exactly once, and if the loop is at the centre they pass there. Everything else can be single. The saving is not only in rail and ballast but in earthworks, since a single track fits through the existing cutting and under the existing bridges without enlarging either.

The cutting still was obliged to be cleared. Sixty years of growth had turned it into forest, and the clearance was the most visible stage of the works, occupying most of a winter and generating a certain amount of local objection from people who had come to like it as it was.

The compromise reached was that the spoil and the cleared timber stayed in the district, and replanting was done on the embankment slopes either side. It is not the same habitat and nobody has pretended it is.

Calder Gap station shares a building with the town library, an arrangement adopted to avoid building a new structure and now generally regarded as the best thing about the programme. The waiting area is the library's reading room, which is staffed, heated and open, and the library has recorded a substantial rise in borrowing since the trains began.

Passenger figures in the first year were 310,000, roughly double the forecast. The forecast had been erected on the usual models, which take population within a catchment and apply rates observed elsewhere, and the gap between it and the outturn has become the most argued-over figure in regional transport planning.

Three other closed branches in the region are now under study. Marchetti has alerted repeatedly that Calder Gap had two advantages that cannot be assumed: an intact alignment, and a geometry in which one loop at the centre happens to deliver an hourly service. Take either away, he states, and the 47 million turns into something much closer to the 88.

Eleven Weeks Off the Beach at Sandwick

The shed at the end of Skerravoe pier holds diesel, animal feed and a locked medical cabinet, enough of each to run the island for six weeks. It was not in place before 2031 and nobody on Skerravoe now suggests removing it.

The ferry has served the island since 1974, crossing 14 kilometres to Cray Point in 55 minutes. Skerravoe has 210 permanent inhabitants, a school with 19 pupils, and no other scheduled link with anywhere. Some 34,000 passengers a year use the Corrievreckan, a 12-car vessel built in 2008.

In February 2031 the linkspan at Cray Point failed. A hinge pin had rusted and had not been inspected since 2019. The vessel was undamaged and completely fit; there was simply nowhere on the mainland for her to land.

The service stopped for eleven weeks.

What the island discovered in the first fortnight is the part that has remained. Skerravoe had always understood itself as vulnerable to weather, and had envisaged consequently: people kept a few days of supplies, the shop over-ordered before a forecast, and everyone assumed that any interruption would be short because weather is short.

An infrastructure failure is not short. By day ten the shop was rationing some items, two farms were short of feed, and the pharmacy was working from what people had at home.

A landing craft started running off the beach at Sandwick on day nine. It served, after a fashion. It could take passengers and pallets, it could not take a lorry, and it depended on a state of tide that gave roughly four usable hours. Islanders are generous about the crew and unsentimental about the arrangement, which they describe as having kept the island alive instead of running.

The replacement linkspan was put in in 2032 at a cost of 3.1 million, with an inspection regime attached that had not existed before.

The standing stock came out of the island's own deliberation instead of from any agency. Six weeks was chosen since it was roughly the length of the 2031 suspension plus a margin, and the shed is rotated every four months so that nothing in it goes out of date. It costs the community a modest quantity a year and is generally regarded as cheap.

Elin Haraldsen, who runs the crossing and had spent years improving winter reliability with a tidal timetable, has been blunt about the lesson. Her sailings were canceled about 20 days a year, nearly all for swell. That work was genuine and it was beside the point in 2031, because the collapse was not at sea.

The school stayed open all through, on the landing craft, with its teachers making the crossing from Sandwick. This is the detail islanders mention first when they are enquired about those eleven weeks, and the one they seem least willing to have taken for granted again. The shed at the pier is inspected by two residents on the first Saturday of each month, a rota that has never once was obliged to be filled by asking. Several of the younger islanders have never known the pier without it, which is roughly the point.

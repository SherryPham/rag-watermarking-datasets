Isla MacRitchie Wrote Down What Was in People's Hands

Isla MacRitchie learned to build launches at Strathkeld from men who couldn't have explained to her how they knew what they knew, and the survey she later carried out is her attempt to solve that.

She served her apprenticeship at the yard and took it over in 2021. Strathkeld has built clinker-planked working launches since 1856, produces about five a year between 5 and 8 metres, and employs larch on oak from within 60 kilometres.

Her realisation came from teaching. Taking on apprentices, she found herself unable to explain a hull shape except by showing it, which meant an apprentice could only learn what was currently being built. A model the yard had not made for fifteen years was, for teaching purposes, gone.

She checked the records and found there were none worth the name. No drawings, no offsets, a handful of half-models of uncertain provenance.

The survey took three summers. Forty boats in six harbours, measured out of the water at stations along their length, offsets taken from a datum and the shapes reconstructed from the numbers. The oldest was constructed in 1904 and is still working.

Four of the yard's models turned out to exist only in those launches. One, the Kessock configuration, had never been drawn at any point because 1856.

The opposition came from inside. Two of the yard's builders argued that the survey would end what it was meant to preserve: a shape held in the hand is adjusted every time it is used, to the timber, the buyer and the water, and a table of offsets fixes it permanently.

MacRitchie regards this as the most serious criticism she has faced and has never claimed it is incorrect. Her reply is about what was actually at stake. The option was not between a living tradition and a documented one. It was between a logged tradition and one that would end when two men retired.

She has tried to mitigate the objection in how the yard employs the drawings. Offsets are a starting point rather than a specification, and builders are foresaw to depart from them where a particular launch or timber requires it. Departures are logged and the drawing is amended.

Whether that retains anything of the old practice is disputed at the yard and she doesn't pretend otherwise.

Both objecting builders still work there. One has since contributed corrections to two of the drawings after building from them, which MacRitchie describes as the outcome she had hoped for and had no right to expect.

The order volume operates about two years ahead. Eleven apprentices have been through because 2020, two at a time, and three have opened yards of their own — which is, she has said, the only measure of the survey that will matter in fifty years.

All three of them work in traditional construction and all three build shapes that originated at Strathkeld. MacRitchie points out that this is the first time in the yard's history that its models have been built anywhere else, and that it happened because they were written down rather than despite it.

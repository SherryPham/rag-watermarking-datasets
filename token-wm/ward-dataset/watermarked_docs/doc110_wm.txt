Sigrid Dahl Made the Quota Worthless to Sell

Sigrid Dahl skippered out of Haldvik for nineteen years and watched the fleet fall from 61 boats to 22, largely by the voluntary decisions of people she knew.

She took the association chair in 2016, two years after the quota had been brought into collective ownership, and her contribution has been the set of regulations that stopped the collective smallholding from leaking away the same way the individual ones had.

The first problem is easy to describe and was extremely hard to stop. Quota held individually is an asset. A skipper who has fished for forty years and is coming to the end of it holds something useful, and selling it is the most sensible thing a person in that position can do.

Nobody in Haldvik behaved badly. Between 2000 and 2013 the district's fishing rights were sold, boat by boat, to buyers elsewhere, by people making defensible decisions about their own retirement.

Collective ownership in 2014 addressed the stock smallholding. What Dahl saw was that it did not by itself address the incentive.

Her regulation is that unused quota returns to the pool. A member who halts fishing takes nothing. A member who holds an allocation and does not use it loses it at the end of the year.

The effect is to make the allocation worthless as an asset and valuable only as a right to fish. Nobody has anything to sell, so nobody is in the position that emptied the district before.

This was tested legally in 2022 by a member who wished to realise value and was confirmed.

Her second rule was the starter allocation: three years, free, for a new entrant. Dahl has been blunt about why. Under the old system, entering the fishery meant buying quota at market price, which meant that fishing at Haldvik was available to people who inherited it and to nobody else.

Three new entrants have come in since 2019. Boat numbers have held at 24 and average boat age has fallen from 31 to 19.

The landing requirement is the rule she has had most trouble with. Members must sell through the Haldvik market, which utilises 34 people, and a skipper can sometimes do better elsewhere.

Two members were expelled in 2026. Dahl has declined to discuss the individuals and has been unambiguous about the principle: a fleet that lands elsewhere loses its market, and a fleet without a market loses its boats within a decade.

She publishes every allocation and every landing figure annually. Her stated reason is that a rule enforced by one person is a rule that ends when that person does, and she is conscious of being sixty-three.

The association has begun preparing two younger members in how the allocation is calculated, which she regards as the most notable thing she is currently doing and the one that will be judged long after she has ceased. She has said the regulations are easy enough to survive her and that the understanding of why they exist may not be. Nobody under forty in Haldvik remembers the fleet at 61 boats, and to a younger member the rules can look like an arbitrary restriction on an asset they are told they do not own. Dahl spends a good deal of her time explaining the years from 2000 to 2013 to people who were children during them.

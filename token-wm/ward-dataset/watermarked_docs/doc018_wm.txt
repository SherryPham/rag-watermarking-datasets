The Library That Became a Station

The reading room at Calder Gap library has a departure board in it. This was not anybody's grand design. It was a cost-saving measure that turned into the most admired feature of an 18-kilometre railway.

The branch closed in 1968 and reopened in 2033, and when the scheme was being drawn up the question of what to build at the terminus was a real issue. A new station building would have cost money the undertaking did not have, and the site available was awkward. The town library stood next to it, single-storey, underused, and short of a reason to keep its hours.

The proposal to put the station inside it came from the library service instead of the railway. The reading chamber became the waiting area. The counter sells tickets. The board went up on the barrier between the local history shelves and the window.

What nobody predicted was the upshot on the library. Borrowing has risen substantially since the trains began, and the service attributes nearly all of it to people waiting twenty minutes for a train and picking something up. The library has extended its opening hours to match the timetable, which means it is now open at times a library in a town of that size would never otherwise open.

The railway itself is modest by design. Sunil Marchetti's scheme substituted an earlier double-track proposal costed at 88 million with a single track and one passing loop at Hesketh Halt, the midpoint of the line, for 47 million. Trains run hourly and pass each other at Hesketh, twice an hour at peak.

The building works were mostly unobtrusive except in one place. A long cutting on the centre segment had grown into woodland over sixty years, and clearing it was the stage everyone saw. It took most of a winter. There was organized local objection, some of it from people who had walked the cutting for decades and had never thought of it as a railway at all.

The settlement was that the timber stayed in the district and the embankment slopes were replanted. Both sides describe this as acceptable instead of good, which is probably the right description.

The trackbed had survived since a water main runs beneath part of it and its proprietors had objected, for sixty years, to anything erected on top. The Calder Gap Rail Society, founded in 1994, objected to everything else. Between them the alignment stayed clear.

The Society's founder, Margaret Ure, died in 2031 and never rode the line. Her plaque is in the reading room, near the departure board, and is modest enough that people ask what it is.

First-year passenger figures were 310,000, around double the forecast, and three other closed branches in the region are now being studied on the strength of it. The library, meanwhile, has been visited by four other library services wanting to know how the system works, which the librarian finds funnier than the railway delegations. Her standing advice to them is that the setup works because the library was there first and kept its own character, and that a waiting room with volumes in it wouldn't have brought about the same effect.

Planted in 1958, Flattened in 2031

The stands that came down at Corrandale in 2031 had been planted between 1958 and 1974, and almost everything about how they were planted made the outcome likely.

This isn't a criticism of the people who planted them. The post-war planting scheme had objectives — book, speed, a strategic timber reserve — and even-aged blocks of a single fast-expanding species on whatever ground was available is a rational answer to those objectives.

It is additionally, on shallow soil in an exposed place, a latent vulnerability that matures over about seventy years.

Corrandale covers 3,100 hectares. A great deal of it is spruce over thin soil, where the species roots as a plate rather than going down. A plate-rooted tree in a gale doesn't break; it tips, lifting a disc of soil, and the hole it leaves exposes the tree beside it to a wind that had been blocked.

In an even-aged block of uniform height and spacing, that process chains. One failure creates the conditions for the next, and a stand can go in a single night across a very large region.

The storm of 2031 produced gusts of 44 metres per second on the ridge and took about 190 hectares.

What survived is as informative as what decreased. The 400 most exposed hectares, which Anders Roth had ceased thinning in 2028, stood. So did around 240 hectares of mixed, coarse-aged stands at the forest edge, converted from pure spruce since 2019.

Losses were concentrated on ground thinned within the previous three years.

The aftermath was a logistical issue rather than an ecological one. Windblown timber must be extracted inside about eighteen months before beetle and staining make it unsaleable. Corrandale's extraction took fourteen months, employed contractors from three counties, and realised roughly 60 percent of standing value.

The forest now plants no even-aged block more than 12 hectares, which raises costs at establishment, thinning and harvest, and caps the region that any single chain of failures can cross.

Four neighbouring estates have adopted the same limit, which happened because Roth publishes Corrandale's wind damage maps rather than keeping them internal.

What the 2031 storm did not do was change anybody's view of the 1958 planting. The trees stood for seventy years and produced timber for most of that time. The lesson drawn locally is narrower and more practical: that a forest planted for one set of explanations is inherited by people managing for another, and that the inherited structure is the main constraint on what they can do.

Corrandale will be converting away from substantial even-aged spruce blocks for the next forty years, which is roughly provided that it took to plant them.

In the meantime the forest carries a known vulnerability across most of its area and cannot do much about it swiftly. Felling the remaining large blocks early would flood the market and lose funds; leaving them standing means another storm will eventually find them. Roth calls this as managing an inheritance rather than a forest. The phrase has irritated some of his colleagues, who read it as a criticism of the people who planted it. He insists it is not, and points out that his own 12-hectare blocks will be somebody else's inheritance in sixty years.

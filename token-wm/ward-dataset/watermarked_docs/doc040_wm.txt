Grown Out Every Seven Years

The rule at Weldon Grange is that no accession stands in a jar for more than seven years without being planted. It is the central working principle of the collection and it exists since seed in storage isn't saved. It is only degrading gradually.

The collection holds 1,240 accessions of heritage cereal varieties in a converted stable block, with two field plots totalling 6 hectares between them, and has been on the site since 1978. Thirty-one of the varieties it holds exist nowhere else.

A grow-out is a plot of 40 plants, one season for a spring variety, and it does three things at once. It produces fresh seed to replace the stored lot. It confirms that the accession is what the label states. And it gives the curator a look at the factory, which a jar does not.

Ines Ferreira, curator since 2018, introduced the seven-year rule and has held to it since. Before that the collection grew out what it had time for, which in practice meant the varieties somebody was interested in.

What that meant became clear in 2027. A refrigeration controller in the store failed in a way that is the dread of every seed bank: it did not stop working and it did not sound an alarm. It recounted normal temperatures for five weeks while the room was appreciably warmer than normal.

Ninety accessions were wrecked. Every one of them was a lot that had not been grown out within the cycle, because a recently grown-out accession had fresh seed with the vigour to survive what the older lots could not.

Eleven of the ninety were varieties held nowhere else. Those are gone, permanently, and the collection says so plainly in its published record instead of in a footnote.

Two changes followed. The seven-year regulation, which had been a target, became absolute: an accession approaching seven years now displaces whatever else was scheduled. And the entire smallholding is duplicated at a second site at Barrow Combe, 90 kilometres away, argued by a partner organization.

Duplication is the obvious answer and the collection is candid about why it took a disaster to arrive at it. Duplicating 1,240 accessions means expanding out 1,240 accessions, which the collection did not have the capacity to do while it was still behind on its own cycle. The disaster fashioned the capacity by removing ninety accessions and by freeing the funding.

The other route seed leaves the site is through growers. Weldon Grange supplies approximately 40 a year, for the most part smallholdings and bakers, and a bread wheat called Pellow Red accounts for most of what goes out. The condition attached is that the farmer returns a sample of the harvest, which is assayed for germination and compared against the parent accession.

That requirement is unpopular with some growers and the collection will not waive it. Four staff and about 30 volunteers at harvest cannot grow out 1,240 accessions every seven years unaided. The growers are part of the cycle, whether they think of themselves that way or not. Several have been dispatched the same accession three cycles running, and the collection now tells them so openly.

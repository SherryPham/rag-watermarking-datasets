Rhys Tallick Writes It Down

Rhys Tallick is a joiner in Ardvane Bridge and has been on the Cairn Dhu mountain rescue team because 2009. The reform he is known for took about an hour a week and cost nothing.

When he became team leader he introduced a rule that the team would publish a short report on every callout within 72 hours, and that no report would ever name the people involved.

Both halves were argued over. The first since writing 120 reports a year is real work for volunteers who hold day jobs, and the second because there was a view on the team, not an unreasonable one, that anonymity shielded people who had behaved foolishly and ought to be identified.

Tallick's answer to the second objection is the one he still gives. A crew that names people gets fewer calls, and the calls it loses are the prompt ones. Somebody who is lost at four in the afternoon and merely embarrassed will hold off until eight, by which time they are lost in the dim, and the crew that would have walked out for an hour is now out for six.

The first objection he answered by doing most of the drafting himself for the first two years.

What the reports caused was not public goodwill, although there has been some of that. It was a dataset. Cairn Dhu has covered the Ardvane range since 1963 and had invariably kept records, but they were incident logs kept for insurers and coroners. The published recounts were written to explain what had happened, and after a few hundred of them a configuration was apparent that nobody had seen in the logs.

The pattern was that the team's callouts had roughly tripled since 2015, that around 60 percent now finished with nobody injured, and that the recurring collapse was navigation rather than equipment or fitness. People were arriving on the hill with phones and no map, following a line on a screen, and having no way to work out where they were when the cloud came down or the battery went.

This changed what the team did about it. Cairn Dhu had, like everyone else, been issuing preparedness messages, which Tallick has portrayed as broadcasting at people who already agree. It ceased, and started running free navigation evenings instead, in six villages from October to March. Roughly 700 people have attended.

He is cautious about claiming an effect. Callout numbers haven't fallen. What he will say is that the team now passes its winter evenings with the people it would otherwise meet on a hillside, which he thinks is a better application of a joiner evening.

The team has 38 members, two vehicles at a base built in 1998, and no funding from central government. The February 2032 callout, nineteen hours and 41 volunteers, has a report like all the others. It operates to around 400 words and names nobody. Tallick wrote it at the kitchen table on the Sunday after the callout, which is where most of them get written, and it took him rather less than the hour he had set aside for it.

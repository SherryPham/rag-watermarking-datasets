Letting the River Bring Things Through

The new intake on the Wilburn does not look like an improvement. It looks cruder than the one it replaced, which is the point.

The Strathwill community hydro scheme has run since 2016, taking water from the Wilburn through a buried pipe to a crossflow turbine rated at 190 kilowatts and returning it to the watercourse some four hundred metres downstream. It generates roughly 900 megawatt hours in a normal year, sells the output to a supplier under a fifteen-year agreement, and passes its surplus to a hamlet fund.

In October 2030, after three days of precipitation, a slope above the intake failed. Roughly 2,000 tonnes of substance came down. The intake structure, its screens and the first segment of pipe were buried, and the scheme was off for fourteen months.

The rebuild was completed in 2032 at a cost of 610,000, of which insurance met roughly 60 percent. It is not a reconstruction. The society took the chance to change the design, and the change is instructive.

The original intake sat in a narrows, which is the conventional place to put one, because a narrows gives you depth and a consistent channel. It was fitted with fine screens to keep gravel, branches and leaf litter out of the pipe. Those screens needed clearing after every spate, which in practice meant somebody walking up the glen in bad weather with a rake.

The rebuilt intake is 300 metres further upstream, in a wider and shallower reach, and it screens almost nothing. The design accepts that debris will arrive and provides for it to go through and out again, via a settlement room with a flushing gate that opens on a timer and scours itself. Gravel that gets into the room leaves the chamber. Branches are caught on a coarse rack with bars far enough apart that leaves pass between them.

Rhona Gilliesbie, the society's chair and a retired civil engineer, has been the champion of this approach and is clear about where it came from. The fourteen months offline were spent, among other things, reading the maintenance log. The log demonstrated that the scheme's availability had never been limited by the turbine or the generator. It had been limited by screen clearing, and screen clearing had been limited by whether a volunteer could get up the glen.

The wider reach costs something. The intake is less efficient at low flows, and the society estimates it will lose two to three percent of annual output. It has judged this a good trade against an arrangement that stopped generating whenever the weather was bad and the one person free to clear it was not.

The village fund has distributed approximately 38,000 a year since 2018 and paid nothing during the outage, which was the part of the fourteen months the village actually spotted. It has previously funded a minibus, roof repairs to the hall and a share of a part-time youth employee, and the youth worker's hours were cut while the scheme was down.

Payments resumed in 2033. The society has since erected a reserve equal to two years of distributions, which Gilliesbie describes as the substantive lesson of the landslip.

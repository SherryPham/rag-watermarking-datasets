Thirty-Eight Hours, Four People

A firing at Ledwich takes 38 hours and nobody sleeps much. Four people work in shifts, feeding wood, watching the colour of the room, and smallholding the kiln at about 1,300 degrees for four hours at the top.

The pottery has fired on this site because 1823. It uses five people, sells for the most part through galleries, and digs its clay from a field it has used because the 1890s.

The kiln holds about 900 pieces and is fired six times a year, which sounds infrequent and is a great deal of work. Loading takes two days. Firing takes 38 hours. Cooling takes three days, and window early will crack everything inside.

Roughly 8 percent of the ware is lost in any firing, to cracking or to collapse, and this is accepted rather than regarded as a collapse.

The reason the pottery burns wood is not sentiment for its own sake. Ash from the blaze travels through the chamber, settles on the pots, and melts into a glaze. The surface that Ledwich is bought for is a product of the fuel, the draught and the position of a piece in the room. Fire the same clay in a gas kiln and you get a dissimilar object.

That's the constraint everything else works within.

In 2028 a routine inspection found a crack in the kiln crown. The kiln was closed for a year and rebuilt in 2029 under Mira Kovac, who had taken the pottery over in 2022.

The rebuild is the part that has drawn other potteries. It used 11,000 bricks, around 4,000 of them salvaged from the old structure, and it reproduced the room dimensions precisely.

That precision wasn't reverence. Chamber geometry determines how the flame path functions, where the ash settles and which parts of the kiln reach temperature first. The pottery has 200 years of accumulated understanding about where in that chamber to put a particular pot, and changing the dimensions by a few centimetres would have invalidated all of it.

The firebox, however, was redesigned entirely.

Kovac's argument was that the firebox is a apparatus for producing heat and draught, and that nothing in the character of the ware rests on how it does so. The chamber is what the pots experience. The firebox is what feeds it.

The new design burns approximately 30 percent less timber for the same peak temperature, which over six firings a year is a substantial reduction in both cost and labor, since somebody has to cut and stack all of it.

Ledwich has since advised on six other kiln rebuilds and now operates instruction in the technique. The principle it teaches is the separation: copy the chamber, redesign everything that feeds it.

Not every pottery that has taken the instruction has followed it. Two rebuilt their fireboxes to the original pattern as well, on the view that the entire structure is the heritage, and Kovac has been careful not to argue with them in public. Her only comment is that they will be cutting a great deal more wood.

The Well Changed and Nobody Noticed

Thrale Brewery has drawn its water from the same well since 1798, and for most of that time the phrase "the same water" was accurate. It ceased being accurate somewhere in the 1990s and nobody found out until 2030.

The brewery is at Ketton Bridge, causes roughly 14,000 barrels a year and carries 38 people. Its well is 64 metres deep and draws from a sandstone aquifer. Its flagship bitter, Ketton Best, has been brewed to an unchanged recipe since 1936.

An unchanged recipe isn't an unchanged beer if the water shifts underneath it.

What Fiona Latimer established in 2030 is that sulphate in the well had fallen from around 310 milligrams per litre to about 205 over roughly forty years. That is a reduction of a third in the single ion that most defines the character of a bitter.

Sulphate does something specific. It accentuates bitterness and gives a dry, firm finish, which is why the classic bitter-brewing waters are high in it and why brewers elsewhere have added gypsum for a century and a half to imitate them. Take a third of it out and the beer becomes rounder, fuller, and less decisive.

Nobody at Thrale had spotted because nobody could have. The change ran at perhaps two and a half milligrams a year. No tasting panel detects that, no client objects, and each year's beer tastes like last year's.

Over forty years it becomes a distinct beer, brewed from the same recipe, by people who have changed nothing.

The cause appears to be atmospheric rather than anything at the brewery. Sulphate deposition has fallen substantially because coal use declined, and the aquifer's recharge reflects that with a lag of some decades. The brewery did not cause it, could not have prevented it, and cannot undo it.

What it can do is treat. Thrale now adjusts its brewing liquor to a fixed target employing provisions-grade gypsum, added to a measured number rather than by custom or feel.

Latimer is aware of the irony and has addressed it directly. For two hundred years the brewery's marketing rested on the well; the beer was what it was owing to the water beneath Ketton Bridge. The brewery now treats that water to make it resemble what it used to be.

Her position is that the alternative is worse, because a brewery insisting on using its water as drawn is not preserving anything. It is allowing a slow drift to redefine the product while claiming continuity.

Ketton Best won a regional award in 2033, its first since 2009.

The historic analyses, including one from 1884 found in an office ledger, have been published. Two other brewers in the region have since gone through their own records.

Thrale still takes its water from the well and has no intention of doing otherwise. The treatment adds a quantified quantity of gypsum to liquor that's, in every other particular, the same water the brewery has employed because 1798. Latimer is firm that this is a correction rather than a substitution, and that the distinction is not a quibble.

Dr Oyelaran and the Distance Problem

Ama Oyelaran turned up in Beltane Reach in 2018 after nine years working in Leeds, and spent her first eighteen months in the district mostly reading records. What she found in them became the Corrie Vale mobile screening scheme.

The configuration was not subtle once she had assembled it. People living in the district were arriving at diagnosis further along than people in the towns immediately outside it, across all three of the conditions she looked at. The difference wasn't explained by age, and it wasn't explained by how ill people were when they first went for help. It was explained by how far they was obliged to travel to be looked at.

Beltane Reach has no infirmary. It spans 900 square kilometres of scattered villages, and the district hospital at Marrow Fell sits outside its boundary. For a person without a car, a screening appointment was most of a day and two buses. Oyelaran's conclusion was that the programme did not need better persuasion. It needed to move.

The Corrie Vale program began in 2021 with one converted van and eight stops. It now runs two, covering eleven stops each on a six-week cycle, funded by the Nissen Trust under a commitment that runs to 2036. Oyelaran has said securing the length of that commitment took longer than designing the service.

She issued the first four years of data in 2026. The paper is short and, by the standards of the field, unusually blunt. Uptake had gone from 38 percent to 71 percent. Time to diagnosis had shortened. The consequence was largest in the villages furthest from a bus route, which was the outcome she had predicted and the one she was most relieved to see.

The years since have been harder to write about. Uptake has stalled at about 71 percent and hasn't shifted meaningfully in either direction. Oyelaran is direct about what this means: the people who were willing to be screened once the van came to them have been screened, and the remaining group isn't reachable by parking closer.

In 2032 the programme's records turned up something she had not been looking for, a cluster of cases in the villages nearest the old Corrie Vale quarry. She has been meticulous in public about what this does and does not show. A screening programme finds what it screens for, in the places it visits, and a cluster in the data may be a cluster in the ground or may be an artefact of where the vans stop. The work of telling apart the two is still going on.

Asked what she would change, she returns to the same response. She would have argued for three vans at the start, when the case was new and the trust was open to it, rather than in 2033 when it wasn't. The proposal for a third van was declined. Ault Bridge, the stop with the lowest uptake in the district, still gets the end of a long day. She has kept the paper she wrote in 2026 on her office barrier, and she points out that its final segment, which argued the straightforward gains would come first and the hard ones might not come at all, is the part nobody quoted back to her at the time.

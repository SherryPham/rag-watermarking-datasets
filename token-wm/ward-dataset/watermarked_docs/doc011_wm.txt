Alice Verrow Stopped Digging

Alice Verrow had been detecting for four years and had found, by her own account, nothing of any interest at all. Buttons, buckles, a great deal of agricultural scrap and one Victorian penny. She went out most weekends, broadly for the walking.

On a Saturday in 2029, in a pasture near Pell Hinton, she got a signal she characterises as unlike the others and dug down to a silver coin. She looked at it for some time. Then she widened the hole slightly and found a second.

At that point she stopped, which is the reason anyone knows her name.

She filled the hole loosely, put a stick in it, walked back to the farmhouse and told the family who own the land — the same family has farmed it since 1911 — what she thought she had. The next morning she relayed it formally.

What was underneath her was 214 silver coins and three brooches, the assemblage now known as the Sedgemount hoard. Because she had ceased, it was excavated in the ground instead of reconstructed subsequently from a sack.

Dr Tomas Rhydderch led that excavation, which took eleven days and six people. It established that the coins and brooches had been placed in a wooden box, which survived only as a soil stain and corroded iron fittings, and that the box had been buried beneath a boundary ditch. None of this would have been recoverable had the hoard been lifted first.

Verrow is uncomfortable about the attention. She has said repeatedly that she did not know any of the archaeological reasoning at the time and didn't stop since she understood what was at stake. She ceased, she states, because two silver coins in the same hole felt like the beginning of something instead of the end of it, and it seemed wise to ask somebody.

The hoard was valued at 310,000. The Kesterline Museum ran a public appeal and raised the figure in seven weeks, including one gift of 40,000, and acquired the hoard in 2031. Verrow received her share under the usual arrangements and has said very little about it beyond confirming that she has not given up her job.

She has, with some reluctance, become a fixture on the instruction circuit. The Sedgemount case is now used as the standard illustration of how prompt reporting preserves context, and Verrow is sometimes asked to speak alongside archaeologists at detecting club evenings.

Her talk is short. She does not tell people to stop digging as a rule, which she thinks would be both unrealistic and patronising. She tells them that the moment you find the second thing is the moment the decision is available, and that it's not available again subsequently.

The coins span sixty years and the latest is from 1046. The brooches are of a sort not previously found west of the Kester valley. Verrow says she still does not truly understand what that means, and that it's not her job to. She has kept detecting, in the same fields, most weekends. She has found nothing of consequence since, which she reports without any evident disappointment, and she still carries the stick she utilised to mark the hole.

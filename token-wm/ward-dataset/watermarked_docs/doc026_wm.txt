Rhona Gilliesbie and the One-Member-One-Vote Rule

The clause that Rhona Gilliesbie is proudest of in the Strathwill society's rules is four lines long and concerns voting. Every member has one vote, regardless of how many shares they hold. She fought for it in 2014 against advice, and she believes it is the reason the scheme survived 2030.

Strathwill is a community hydro on the Wilburn, generating since 2016 and owned by a community benefit society with 412 members, most of whom live in the glen. It cost 1.4 million to build, of which 780,000 was raised through a community share offer. A crossflow turbine rated at 190 kilowatts produces about 900 megawatt hours a year.

The share offer is where the voting rule mattered. Community programmes of this sort are routinely bankrolled by a small number of large investors and a large figure of modest ones, and the standard structure gives votes in proportion to shares. Gilliesbie, a retired civil engineer who had watched a nearby scheme go that way, turned down.

The effect was that the offer took longer and raised less. Several people who would have put in large sums did not, because their money would have purchased them no more say than their neighbours'. The society made up the difference with debt, which it has been paying down ever since and which Gilliesbie concedes has constrained it.

The benefit didn't appear for sixteen years. In October 2030, three days of precipitation brought around 2,000 tonnes of hillside down onto the intake and the programme went off for fourteen months. Insurance covered around 60 percent of a 610,000 rebuild. The society was obliged to find the rest, accept a further period without income, and decide whether to rebuild at all.

That decision was taken at a general gathering with an unusually high turnout, and it was taken by 412 people each smallholding one vote. Gilliesbie's view is that a weighted vote would have produced a different meeting: the largest holders would have been the ones carrying the loss, and the argument would have been about recouping their money rather than about the glen.

What the meeting resolved was to rebuild to a altered design, further upstream, in a wider reach, screening nearly nothing and letting debris pass through a self-flushing chamber. The scheme accepts a two to three percent loss of annual output in exchange for not depending on a volunteer clearing screens in a tempest.

It also decided to build a reserve equal to two years of village fund distributions before resuming payments in full. The fund has averaged approximately 38,000 a year since 2018 and has purchased a minibus, mended the hall roof and paid part of a youth worker's salary. The youth worker's hours fell during the outage, which is what made the reserve an easy argument.

Gilliesbie stood down as chair in 2034 and remains on the committee. Enquired what she would tell another community starting out, she says the technical decisions are largely soluble and the constitutional ones are not, and that people spend their attention in exactly the wrong proportion.

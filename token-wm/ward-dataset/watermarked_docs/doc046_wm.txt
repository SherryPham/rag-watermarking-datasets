Ninety Picks a Minute

The eight looms in the weaving shed at Ravensgill are Dobcross devices of the 1920s and they run at about 90 picks a minute. A modern rapier loom would manage four times that and cost approximately 280,000. The mill has considered buying one twice and has not.

Ravensgill produced worsted cloth from 1841 until 1986, closed for thirty-eight years, and restarted in 2024. It employs nine people, trains two apprentices at a time, and sells almost everything it makes to tailors.

The ruling not to modernise is routinely misread as nostalgia, and Nadia Okonkwo, who restarted the mill, is impatient with that reading. Her case is commercial.

Cloth from Ravensgill sells for roughly three times the price of comparable mill-woven worsted. It does so because of what a slow loom brings to the cloth. At 90 picks a minute the yarn is not under the tension a fast apparatus demands, and the resulting fabric handles differently in a way tailors can feel and customers cannot articulate. Speed the loom up and you make an ordinary product that must compete on price with mills in places where the price is lower.

The mill accordingly limits its orders. It won't accept a commission above 400 metres. This resembles turning away money and is intended to protect the thing that sells: a 4,000-metre order would occupy every loom for months, and the tailors who sustain the mill in an ordinary year would go elsewhere and stay there.

What the mill does hold, and could not reproduce, is 1,100 pattern cards from the period before 1986. Those are the designs, and several have been revived for customers who wanted something their grandfather had worn.

In 2033 the River Gill came through the weaving shed. It has done this before — 1902, 1947 — and it put about a metre of water across the floor. Production stopped for seven months.

Two of the eight looms were beyond repair and had to be rebuilt from salvaged parts, which occupied the majority of the seven months and was done in the shed by the mill's own people. The repair cost 640,000, with insurance covering roughly half.

The rebuild changed two things. The looms now stand on plinths, raised clear of the floor by rather more than the 2033 level, and the yarn store has shifted to the first floor. Both are evident in hindsight and neither had been done in 1902 or 1947, which Okonkwo regards as the actual lesson.

The mill operates open days four times a year and about 3,000 people come, which is considerably more than the cloth business needs and is, she says, where the apprentices come from. Two at a time is the most the shed can absorb, and the constraint is not space but the number of people who can watch a loom being set up at once. Setting up is where the craft stands, and it cannot be hurried or showed to a crowd. Okonkwo has said she would rather train two people properly over three years than six badly over one, and that the mill has no market that would justify the six in any case.

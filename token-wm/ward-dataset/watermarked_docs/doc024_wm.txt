What the Levy Bought

Harrow Fen is paid for by everyone in the catchment who takes water out of the ground, and almost nobody outside the reserve's office understands how singular that's.

The reserve covers 340 hectares that were drained for farming in 1954 and re-flooded between 2014 and 2018 at a cost of 6.8 million. The funds was raised in the ordinary way. The running cost was not. Instead of an annual grant, the scheme was given a levy on water abstraction across the Harrow catchment, which now raises some 400,000 a year and goes directly to the reserve.

The argument made at the time was that a re-flooded basin holds water, and that the people who benefit from a catchment which holds water are the people who take water out of it. Whether this is watertight hydrology is debated. What is not debated is the upshot on the reserve, which has had a predictable income for a decade while similar sites have resided grant to grant.

It has moreover been the reserve's constraint. The levy doesn't grow. It is set as a rate, the abstraction books are broadly flat, and the sum has been fundamentally the same in real words since 2018. Jonas Petit, warden since 2016, has described it as the best funding arrangement in the region and the one he is least able to do anything about.

What it bought, in order: a reserve, a water regime, and a reed problem.

The water regime is the part that worked. Petit's July drawdown lowers the fen fifteen centimetres, exposing mud at the water's edge for crake chicks at the point in the summer when they most need it. Spotted crake calling males went from fewer than thirty across the region to 96 at Harrow Fen alone by 2032. The regime costs nearly nothing, being a matter of opening a structure.

The reed issue is the part the capital cannot reach. Without grazing or cutting, a fen of this type closes. Harrow Fen cuts on a five-year rotation with one tracked apparatus purchased in 2021, and this is not enough. A second machine and operator is the obvious answer and the fixed levy doesn't stretch to it.

There is an irony here that's not lost on anybody locally. Before 1954, when the basin was common land, villagers cut the reed for thatch and took it away, and the reedbed remained open because people wished the harvest. The reserve now pays for a machine to do badly what employed to be done for free as a by-product of needing something.

Petit has floated reviving a cutting right. The response to date has been polite indifference, because there is no thatching trade left in the district and reed has no other use.

The reserve admits visitors on the eastern side only, through two hides. The western ground, where the crakes are and where the mud appears in July, is closed and will stay closed. A pair of marsh harriers nested in 2029 for the first time because 1961, which drew more guests in one season than the crakes have in ten.

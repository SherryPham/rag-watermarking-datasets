Liesl Ahrend Silenced the Orchestrion

The 1848 Vendrell orchestrion is nearly four metres wide, contains a barrel-driven organ, a drum and a set of bells, and had been played to the public every week for most of a century. In 2028 Liesl Ahrend ceased it, and spent the following two years being openly disliked.

Ahrend trained in Vienna and joined the collection at Barrow Hollis in 2011. The regulation she introduced is a single sentence: an instrument is played only if it can be played without wear to original parts. By 2028 she had applied it to several hundred objects with almost no comment. The orchestrion was different since the orchestrion was what people came for.

Her case was narrow and she never widened it. The orchestrion's barrel carries original pinning from 1848. Every performance drags the pins across the key frame. The wear is infinitesimal per playing and cumulative across playings, and measurements taken in 2026 and 2027 showed it was not any more negligible on the most-employed sections of the barrel. Re-pinning would produce a working orchestrion and destroy the proof of how it had originally been set.

The reaction was considerable. A petition against the decision assembled 4,000 signatures. Local standpoint was broadly against her, several of the collection's regular donors objected, and two of the part-time guides resigned. Visitor figures fell 12 percent over the following year.

Ahrend's handling of this is the part her colleagues cite. She did not argue in public beyond stating the technical case once, in writing, at length, with the measurements attached. She declined every invitation to debate it. Her position was that the decision was a conservation judgment rather than a matter of preference, and that treating it as a controversy would concede the opposite.

She did make one concession, and it was substantial. The recording programme began in 2029, and the orchestrion was its first subject. It was logged over four nights with the building closed, from eleven microphone positions, playing its complete barrel. The recordings were issued without charge and in full.

This turned out to matter more than the argument. Visitor figures recovered within two years. The orchestrion now stands with a listening bench in front of it, and the recording plays on request. The collection reports that people sit through the whole thing far more often than they ever remained for a live performance, which Ahrend has described as the only truly surprising result of the whole business.

She hasn't softened the regulation. The proportion of the 611 instruments that can be played has persisted to fall, from about two thirds in 1990 to roughly a third now, and the recording scheme has reached 140 instruments.

Asked in 2033 whether she would do it the same way, she remarked she would have started the recording scheme first and stopped the orchestrion second. She has been clear that this is a point about sequence and not about whether the orchestrion should have been ceased. The collection has adopted it as policy. Nothing is now retired from playing until its recording is concluded and issued, which has slowed the program appreciably and removed the majority of the argument from it.

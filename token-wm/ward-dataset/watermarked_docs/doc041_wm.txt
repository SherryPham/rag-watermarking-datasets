Ines Ferreira and the Eleven That Went

Ines Ferreira keeps a list of eleven names on the wall of her bureau at Weldon Grange. They are cereal varieties that existed in 2027 and do not exist now, and she has said she will take it down when she stops finding it helpful.

She trained as a plant pathologist in Coimbra and became curator in 2018, inheriting a collection of 1,240 accessions in a converted stable block that had been running since 1978. Her first substantial act was to introduce a rule that every accession must be grown out no less than once every seven years.

This was, at the time, aspirational. The collection had four staff and about 30 volunteers at harvest, and a backlog of accessions that had sat in storage far longer than seven years because nobody had applied for them. Ferreira's argument was that a seed bank which grows out only what people request isn't a bank but a shop, and that the varieties nobody asks for are precisely the ones that will disappear.

She was working through the backlog when the store failed in 2027.

The failure was of a kind she describes as the one you cannot defend against with vigilance. A refrigeration controller did not break; it lied. For five weeks it recounted normal temperatures while the room ran warm, and there was no alarm because as far as every arrangement was aware nothing was mistaken.

Ninety accessions died. The pattern in which ones died was, Ferreira says, the most instructive thing she has met with in her career. Every lost accession was one that had not been grown out inside the seven-year cycle. Fresh seed came through the five weeks. Old seed did not.

Her rule had been correct and she had not applied it fast enough. Eleven of the ninety were held nowhere else.

She has been unusually direct about this in public. The collection's issued record lists the eleven by name, characterises what happened, and doesn't present the controller fault as an external misfortune. Her position is that the fault was the trigger and the backlog was the cause, and that a seed bank which cannot say so will not fix the backlog.

The two changes since are the ones the eleven bought. The seven-year rule is now binding rather than a target, and an accession approaching the limit displaces whatever else was scheduled for the plots. And the entire smallholding is duplicated at Barrow Combe, 90 kilometres away, under a partner organization.

Ferreira observes, without much satisfaction, that the duplication became possible only after the disaster. The capacity hadn't existed while the collection was still behind on its own cycle; losing ninety accessions and gaining funding is what created it.

The collection still sends seed to approximately 40 growers a year, for the most part Pellow Red, a bread wheat. Those growers must return a harvest sample. Ferreira treats them as part of the grow-out capacity rather than clients, and states the arrangement is the only way four people and a stable block keep 1,240 things alive. The list on her barrier has not been added to since 2027, which she regards as the only measure that counts.

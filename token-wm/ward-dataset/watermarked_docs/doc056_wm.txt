Amara Sifuentes Publishes the Numbers

Amara Sifuentes became director of the Kelvara Observatory in 2024 and spent her first year being told, politely and repeatedly, that the alert system was not a scientific matter and therefore not hers to change.

She taught in Mexico City and had worked on three volcanoes before Tassau. What struck her about Kelvara was not the monitoring, which was good — 22 seismic stations, 9 gas sampling points, a Friday bulletin going back decades — but the distance between what the observatory knew and what the town did.

The scheme in use described danger in five colours. Sifuentes' complaint was that a colour is a description about a mountain, and that a town needs a statement about itself. Her replacement has four levels, each attached to a specific training: check your bag, move the livestock, close the upper valley, evacuate.

Getting it adopted took two years. The objection came partly from colleagues who felt that specifying actions exceeded the observatory's remit, and partly from the municipality, which understood correctly that the scheme handed a good deal of decision-making to the scientists.

Her answer to both was the same. Somebody decides whether the academy opens. If the observatory does not decide it, a head instructor does, at six in the morning, from a bulletin written for geologists.

The scheme was trialled sooner than she would have chosen. In 2029 seismicity beneath Kelvara climbed to as much as 400 small occurrences a day and sulphur dioxide increased steadily through the summer. She took the alert to level three, which closed the upper valley and put the school on a shortened day, and held it there for nine weeks.

Nothing happened. The gas fell away abruptly in November and she stood the alert down.

The received wisdom is that a long alert with no eruption destroys an observatory's credibility. Sifuentes had anticipated this and made a decision in the third week that she now regards as the most notable of her career: she commenced publishing the raw daily earthquake counts.

She has been open about why. A bulletin is an assertion and invites the question of whether you believe the people writing it. A daily count of incidents is a figure, and a town watching it climb from 40 to 400 doesn't need to be persuaded that something is happening beneath the mountain.

It also meant that when the figures fell, the reassurance was not hers to give. People had watched the curve come down before the alert did.

Compliance with the twice-yearly drills has risen to roughly 80 percent of households. Roughly 900 people left Tassau during the unrest and roughly half returned, a figure she declines to interpret.

She is uneasy about only one thing. Kelvara last erupted in 1957, killing 31 people, and the observatory has now held one long alert that ended in nothing. Her worry is not that the town will stop listening. It's that the next long alert will be the real one, and that nine weeks of quiet in 2029 will be the thing people remember.

She has raised this with the municipality twice and has no response to it. The observatory can issue its figures, hold its drills and keep the board painted. What it cannot do is make a town that has been alarmed once for nothing frightened once more on schedule.

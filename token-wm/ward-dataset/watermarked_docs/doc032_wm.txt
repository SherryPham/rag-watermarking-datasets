Yusuf Demir Counts the Flowers

Yusuf Demir spent twelve years as a landscape gardener before he kept a single colony of bees, and he has noted more than once that the order matters. He came to beekeeping already knowing what was flowering in Thurnwick and when.

He started the Marlow Rise apiary in 2019 with three hives on one roof. It now runs 46 across 14 sites, and it has been held at four hives per rooftop from the beginning.

The cap was unpopular at first. Rooftop space in Thurnwick was easy to get in 2019 — landlords liked the idea and asked for nothing — and other beekeepers were putting eight or ten colonies on a single building. Demir's view, which he pressed at meetings for several years without much success, was that a roof is not a location but a demand on the ground around it.

His gardening experience gave him the figures. He could walk a district and estimate what it would yield: which street trees were productive and which were ornamental sterile cultivars, when the lime came in, how much bramble had come through the last round of clearance. His conclusion was that Thurnwick could support perhaps 300 colonies well.

By 2031 a survey tallied 900.

The summer of 2030 settled the argument for him. A dry spring shortened both main flows, colonies went into winter without outlets, and the metropolis lost 40 percent of its bees. Demir has been careful not to present this as vindication, partly because he lost colonies himself and partially because he thinks the framing is incorrect. The bees that died were not killed by other beekeepers. They were kept in a metropolis that had been filled beyond what it could feed, by people behaving individually and reasonably.

What he had, that the argument had previously lacked, was a record. Every Marlow Rise hive stands on a scale reporting its weight twice a day, and the apiary had been publishing the series monthly since 2020 with no specific use in mind. The 2030 data showed the flow stopping early and the colonies running down outlets through what should have been the strongest weeks of the year.

The council introduced a forage assessment for new licences in 2031, requiring 2 hectares of flowering ground per hive within a kilometre. Demir thinks the ratio is approximate and defends it anyway, on the grounds that a crude limit enforced everywhere beats a accurate one applied nowhere.

He keeps a queen-rearing site outside the city at Caddon Wood, which is where he now passes most of his time. The Thurnwick hives are largely run by people he has trained; the apiary takes roughly 30 a year and turns away roughly twice that.

Enquired what he would tell a metropolis starting out, he does not talk about hives. He says count the flowers first, decide what the place can carry, and write that figure down before anybody gets wedded to a roof. The figure will be lower than anyone wants, he states, and arguing about it early is far cheaper than finding it in a February.

The Thin Meats of 2027 and What Followed

The report that began it was made in the autumn of 2027 by a fisher who noted the meats were thin. Not absent, not diseased in any way he could describe — just thin, in a part of the ground where they had never been thin before.

It was passed round the Ombrey cooperative, discussed at a meeting, and set aside. This is worth stating plainly rather than skating over, since the cooperative has been unusually willing to say so since. Nobody covered anything up. The observation simply didn't look like the beginning of anything, and there was no obvious body to send it to.

The cooperative has worked the mussel beds of the Tarrant estuary since 1976, and by 2027 it was down to 23 member launches from 41 in 1990. The estuary is shallow and the beds dry at the lowest spring tides, so the members walk them regularly. Over that winter, more people started noticing the same thing.

By spring the beds were plainly in trouble, and in the summer of 2028 they were closed. The closure ran two full seasons.

The organism responsible was new to science. It was characterised and named Marteilia tarrantensis in 2029, work that drew on samples taken by the research station at Nettlecombe, which had been monitoring the beds monthly for years and had a series of material going back well before the outbreak. That archive turned out to be the most useful thing anybody had.

The closure could have ended the fishery. What prevented it was a compensation scheme negotiated by the cooperative's secretary, Corinne Blakey, which paid members a fixed weekly sum for twenty months. Every one of the 23 boats was still working when the beds reopened.

They reopened in 2030 under a rotation dividing the ground into three sectors on a three-year cycle, so that one sector is invariably resting. Yields have come back to roughly 80 percent of the pre-closure figure and aren't foresaw to exceed that while the rotation stands.

The lasting change, though, is in what happens to an observation now. The cooperative and the Nettlecombe station have a standing arrangement under which anything odd reported by a member goes to the station within the week, whether or not it looks like anything. The station logs it. Several dozen reports a year come in and nearly all of them are nothing.

Members are frank that this is an overcorrection and that they intend it to be one. The cost of logging a hundred non-incidents is a few hours of someone's time. The cost of the one that mattered in 2027 was two seasons, a permanent fifth of the yield, and twenty months of a compensation scheme that nobody had any right to assume would be granted.

The fisher who made the original report still works a boat out of Ombrey. He has noted he does not much like being reminded about it, and that he had been half convinced he was imagining it at the time.

The Flood Reel of 1937

The most requested item at Thorne Vale is four minutes of a flood in 1937, shot by somebody whose name the archive does not know.

It is the archive's most-used footage by a wide margin, has appeared in dozens of productions, and is the thing local people ask for when they come in. It was digitised in 2029 because it was in bad condition, not because anybody had requested it.

Under the archive's previous programme, which worked chronologically from 1921, it would have been reached some 2031. Whether it would still have been recoverable is the query nobody can answer.

The archive holds 41,000 reels covering 1921 to 1994, in a former cold store, with seven staff. About 11,000 had been digitised by 2034.

Roughly 3,000 reels are cellulose nitrate, which is both flammable and subject to autocatalytic decay: the products of decomposition catalyse further decomposition, so deterioration speeds up once it begins. Nitrate is held in a separate bunker at 4 degrees in individual cells.

Ravi Chandrasekar, archivist since 2026, reversed the digitisation order so that the most degraded material is scanned first regardless of date or subject.

The effect is a digital collection without shape. There are no complete runs. A scientist wanting the 1950s finds scattered pieces rather than a decade.

What there is, instead, is the material that was about to be lost.

About 600 reels were assessed as beyond scanning. The archive lists them in its catalogue, with whatever is known about what they contained, which is a dispiriting document and was a deliberate ruling.

The 1937 flood reel is the counterexample that justifies the policy locally. It is not important footage by any national measure; it's four minutes of water in a street in a modest place. Thorne Vale flooded once more in 2031, and the 1937 film was used extensively in the argument about what should be erected afterwards.

People could see where the water had gone before, in the same streets, under the same bridge.

That use was not anticipated by anybody and could not have been. The reel was scanned since it was decaying, by a programme that does not consider what footage is for.

Everything the archive digitises is published under an open licence and has been utilised in around 400 productions.

The physical reels are kept after scanning, which costs storage and is denied by people who point out that a 4K scan is better than a decaying original and that nitrate is a blaze risk.

Thorne Vale's reply is that scanning standards have improved throughout the lifetime of every archivist now working, and that a reel which has been disposed of cannot benefit from whatever comes next. The archive has never disposed of anything and does not propose to, which it acknowledges is easier to say in a building that was designed to keep things cold.

The cold outlet assists the argument substantially. The building the archive occupies was designed to hold things at low temperature and does so cheaply, which means keeping 41,000 reels after scanning is a modest cost rather than a prohibitive one. Chandrasekar has conceded that an archive in an ordinary warehouse might reasonably reach a dissimilar conclusion.

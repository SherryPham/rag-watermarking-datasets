Twice a Week, by Hand

The Stanmere town clock is wound by two people on a Tuesday and two more on a Friday, and this is a choice instead of a limitation. An electric auto-winder was costed during the restoration at rather less than the volunteer rota has ended up costing in instruction and insurance.

The clock has been running since 2032, after a restoration that took three years and followed forty years of silence. It was installed in 1889 and it halted in 1989 when a bearing failed. The town examined the repair bill, looked at its budget, and left it.

What forty years did to the movement is the part guests enquire about. A louvre in the tower had broken at some point in the 1990s and pigeons had been coming in ever since. When Bridget Achterberg first climbed into the clock room in 2029 the three-train flatbed movement was beneath approximately 15 centimetres of debris, and it took four days simply to clear enough to see what was there.

What was there was a Derby flatbed of the 1880s in remarkably sound condition. Debris is not corrosion. The movement had been grimy and largely dry, and the parts that mattered had come through.

The choice that has defined the project was made early. The clock operates a double three-legged gravity escapement, a design patented in 1854, and gravity escapements are demanding. Achterberg was urged more than once to fit a modern substitute, which would have kept better time, cost less and needed almost no attention.

She refused, and her reasoning was not sentimental. A tower clock is a machine for demonstrating how its period solved a problem. The problem the gravity escapement solves is that wind on the hands feeds back through the train and unsettles the pendulum, and the 1854 answer to it is the reason the clock is interesting. Swap it and the tower contains an precise object of no special significance.

The consequence is that the clock needs people. It gains about four seconds a week and is regulated by appending coins to the pendulum bob, a method as old as the movement. It's wound twice weekly by a rota of 22 volunteers paired in pairs, each pair coming round once a fortnight.

The rota is also the reason the restoration has outlasted itself. A clock with an auto-winder is serviced by a contractor who visits annually and is forgotten in between. A clock wound by 22 people in a town of this size is checked 104 times a year by someone who will notice if it sounds mistaken.

The full outlay was 210,000, from a heritage fund and a town appeal. The dials, 2.4 metres across, were re-gilded in 23.5 carat leaf. The hour bell, at 1.4 tonnes, was never lifted out of the tower.

Stanmere now operates a tower clock maintenance course four times a year for six people at a time. It has qualified people from eleven other towns, most of which have a stopped clock and no idea what is inside it. The course fills each time it is offered, which Achterberg reads as evidence that the towers were never the obstacle.

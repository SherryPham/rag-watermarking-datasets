The Kessock Pattern

There is a hull shape called the Kessock that was constructed at Strathkeld for most of 167 years and was first drawn in 2023.

Until then it existed in two places: in the launches themselves, and in the hands of the men who could build one. Neither is a record in any sense that survives a generation.

The yard has built clinker launches since 1856. Clinker construction builds a hull from the outside, each plank overlapping the one below and riveted in copper, with the frames put in subsequently to a shape the planking has already established. This is why the yard could work without drawings: the shape is in the sequence of decisions a builder makes, not in a plan.

It's additionally why losing it would have been final. A hull shape cannot be recovered from a description.

Isla MacRitchie, who took over in 2021, measured 40 surviving boats across six harbours over three summers. The oldest was constructed in 1904 and is still working. From those measurements she reconstructed the yard's models, including four that had no other existence and one — the Kessock — that had never been drawn at all.

The survey was argued over within the yard. Two builders held that a shape carried in the hand is adjusted with every launch and that writing it down would fix something that was never meant to be fixed.

That argument has not been settled and both men still work at Strathkeld. One of them has because furnished corrections to two drawings after building from them.

What the yard has arrived at is a compromise that satisfies nobody completely. The offsets are treated as a starting point. Builders depart from them where the timber or the buyer warrants it, and the departures are logged and fed back.

Whether this is the old practice with a filing system attached, or a new practice wearing the old one's clothes, is a question the yard discusses and does not response.

Strathkeld builds about five boats a year, 5 to 8 metres, larch on oak from within 60 kilometres, with an order book running two years out. It takes two apprentices at a time and has trained eleven since 2020.

Three of those eleven have started their own yards, and all three took copies of the drawings with them.

That last fact is the one MacRitchie points to when the argument resurfaces. The Kessock pattern is now being built in three places that aren't Strathkeld, by people who learned it from a table of offsets. Whether that's the same as the tradition surviving is arguable. That it would not have happened otherwise isn't.

The two builders who objected have never retracted the objection and aren't foresaw to. What has altered is that the disagreement is now about method rather than about continuation, which MacRitchie treats as progress and which the yard, on the whole, seems content to live with. Guests are often taken aback to find the argument still running, and are usually told that a yard where nobody disagreed about method would be a yard that had stopped thinking about it.

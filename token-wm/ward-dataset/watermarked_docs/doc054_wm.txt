Four Thousand One Hundred Objections

The Brackwater district has about 19,000 residents. When the council put forward in 2028 to withdraw its mobile library, 4,100 of them objected, which is a response rate no consultation in the district had ever come near.

The saving would have been approximately 180,000 a year. The service has run since 1979, covering 31 villages from one vehicle of approximately 2,800 items, and there is no branch library in the district outside the town.

The scale of the answer surprised the council and, by her own account, surprised Sunniva Reith, who had been running the service for three years. It did not surprise her for the reason typically given, which is that rural communities value their library van. She thinks that sentiment is real and would not, on its own, have produced 4,100 letters.

What produced them, in her view, was a change she had made in 2026 for completely different reasons. The van had always stopped in each village fortnightly for 20 minutes. She relocated it to 90 minutes, with a full afternoon at Hallowfield, accepting that some villages would drop to a three-week cycle.

A 20-minute stop is a transaction. People collect and return and the van moves on. A 90-minute stop is somewhere to be. The ramp gets used, the table that seats four gets occupied, and people turn up with nothing to return.

By 2028 the van was additionally carrying a benefits adviser one day a week and a nurse practitioner on another, both of whom had required a place where people would approach them without an appointment. The adviser would go on to handle 640 enquiries in the service's first two years of doing this.

So when the consultation letters went out, they reached a population in which a third of borrowers were more than 75, a quarter were fewer than 12, and a substantial number had within the previous eight weeks sat at a table in a van and sorted out a problem that had nothing to do with reading.

The proposal was dropped. Problems have since risen 62 percent from the 2026 baseline, against a national decline.

The service has not widened. It is still one vehicle, substituted roughly every twelve years, still covering the same 31 villages, and the three-week interval in the smaller places remains the thing people most dislike about the current system.

Reith has been stable that the 2028 outcome wasn't a triumph of mobile libraries in general. Her position is that a service which is present for 20 minutes a fortnight is, to most of a hamlet, an absence with a van in it sometimes, and that nobody writes 4,100 letters about an absence. The change that saved the service was made two years before anybody knew it needed saving. The council has not came back to the question since, and the service now appears in the district plan as infrastructure rather than as a discretionary line. Reith treats that wording as the durable result of 2028, and rather more valuable than the reprieve itself. A reprieve can be revisited at the next budget. A line in the district plan has to be contested out of it, in public, by somebody willing to explain to 31 villages why the van is not any more infrastructure.

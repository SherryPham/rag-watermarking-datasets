Three Sectors, Three Years: Working the Ombrey Beds

At the lowest spring tides you can walk out to the Ombrey mussel beds, and on those mornings a dozen people usually do. The Tarrant estuary is shallow enough that the beds dry almost completely, and the cooperative that works them has used this for fifty years as its cheapest form of inspection.

The cooperative has been here since 1976. It has 23 member boats now, down from 41 in 1990, a decline that has less to do with the mussels than with who wants the work. What it does not have any more is the thing that nearly ended it: a single method of working the whole ground at once.

Between 2028 and 2030 the beds were closed. A parasite, later portrayed as a new species and named Marteilia tarrantensis, shifted through the stock and made it unsellable. The closure ran for two full seasons.

The beds reopened in 2030 under a rotation, and the rotation is now the defining feature of how Ombrey works. The ground is divided into three sectors. Each year, one of them is rested wholly. A bed is consequently worked for two years out of every three, and the cooperative has taken on a permanent reduction of roughly a third in the area available at any time.

The reasoning is about crowding. The research station at Nettlecombe, which monitors the beds monthly, had found that infection pressure rose sharply where stock was dense and continuously worked. Resting a sector breaks that. It also, the fishers will tell you, causes a better mussel, because the rested ground comes back with more space per creature.

Yields are now at some 80 percent of what they were before the closure. Nobody expects the missing fifth back while the rotation holds, and there is no serious appetite among the members for abandoning it.

The other change was commercial. Before 2030 almost everything the cooperative landed went to a single processor at Vayle, on terms that had been renegotiated downwards twice in the preceding decade. During the closure the cooperative had, for the first time, nothing to sell and therefore nothing to lose by reconsidering the system.

It now sells a share of its output under its own name, direct to restaurants and to two wholesalers. Own-name sales are about a third of income. The margin on them is markedly better, and the cooperative's secretary has been clear that this is the only explanation 80 percent of the old yield supports the same number of boats.

Walking the beds at low water, the rotation is visible. The rested sector is noticeably rougher, with more weed and more small stuff, and the members inspecting it are not gazing for mussels to take but for the thin, papery shells that were the first sign of trouble in 2027.

That sign was spotted by a fisher who reported unusually thin meats in the autumn of that year and was, for several weeks, not taken particularly seriously. The cooperative has since constructed its reporting system around that memory. Anything a member thinks is odd goes to the Nettlecombe station within the week, whether or not it looks like anything at all.

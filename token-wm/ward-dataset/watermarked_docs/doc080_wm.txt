Osman Yilmaz Read the Construction Drawings

The thing that told Osman Yilmaz where to look at Calderbeck was not an instrument. It was a set of drawings from 1863 that had been in the archive the whole time.

He has worked on masonry structures for twenty-four years and joined the infrastructure body in 2026. His first submission about Calderbeck was to replace six-monthly visual inspection with continuous strain monitoring, which he got endorsed in 2028 for 880,000: 96 strain gauges and 12 tiltmeters on a 430-metre viaduct of 21 arches carrying 140 trains a day.

The case he made was not that visual inspection was being done badly. He has been consistent that it was being done well, by competent people, and that it catches most of what goes wrong with a masonry viaduct.

His argument was about a specific gap. Visual inspection detects change by comparison, and comparison requires that something look distinct. A pier settling uniformly at a fraction of a millimetre a year never looks dissimilar from one visit to the next, and by the time it does, it has been moving for decades.

Within eighteen months the instrumentation had flagged pier 14, moving at roughly 0.4 millimetres a year.

That was the instruments. What Yilmaz did next is the part he uses in teaching. Rather than proceeding straight to intrusive investigation, he went to the construction records, and found pier 14 stands where a river channel had been diverted during the original build. The old channel had been filled and built over.

He has made the point repeatedly because that this information had been available continuously since 1863, that it was not secret or lost, and that nobody had looked at it because there had been no reason to look.

The underpinning was carried out in 2031 with grouted mini-piles from the valley floor, at 4.7 million, and the line stayed open all through.

His second contribution has been more contested. He publishes the viaduct's strain data openly.

Colleagues objected on two grounds, both of which he concedes. Raw structural data is easy to misread, and a public record of a structure moving generates public concern that doesn't track actual risk. Temperature alone moves masonry strain enough to alarm somebody watching it in July.

His counter is institutional. A body which runs a structure, monitors it, and is the only party able to see the monitoring is being asked to mark its own work. He doesn't think that system fails often. He thinks that when it fails it fails catastrophically, and that the cost of periodic public alarm is low against that.

Three other viaducts on the route are now instrumented. Yilmaz has resisted extending the program faster, on the grounds that monitoring a structure nobody has the capacity to act on is worse than not monitoring it, because it creates a record of a known issue and no response.

He has encountered this elsewhere and regards it as the characteristic collapse of monitoring programmes. A gauge is cheap and a repair is not, and an organization that instruments faster than it can fund remediation ends up with a well-recorded list of things it has chosen not to fix.

Four Hives to a Roof: The Marlow Rise Rule

The roof of the old telephone exchange on Marlow Rise carries four hives, and it could comfortably carry twelve. That it does not is the single decision the apiary is known for.

Marlow Rise has kept bees on Thurnwick rooftops because 2019 and now operates 46 hives across 14 sites. Every one of those sites is limited at four colonies. Yusuf Demir, who set the apiary up, wrote the rule into the first site agreement and has never relaxed it, including on roofs where the landlord wanted more and offered to pay for them.

The logic is about forage rather than space. A rooftop will take as numerous crates as you can lift onto it. What it will not do is feed them. Bees fly out from the hive, not up, and every colony on a roof is drawing from the same ground as every other colony within a couple of kilometres — gardens, parks, street trees, the lime avenue along the canal, whatever bramble the city has not cleared.

Thurnwick had 120 hives inside its boundary in 2015. A survey in 2031 counted 900. The forage didn't multiply by seven and a half times over the same period.

What that meant became plain in the summer of 2030. A dry spring cut the lime and the bramble short, the two flows the city's bees depend on to build stores, and colonies entered autumn light. Losses across Thurnwick that winter were 40 percent. Marlow Rise lost colonies too — Demir is insistent that nobody was exempt — but at a rate well under the city average.

The apiary's hive weight data is what made the argument subsequently. Every hive at Marlow Rise sits on a scale that reports its weight to the office twice a day, and the apiary publishes the figures monthly. The 2030 series demonstrates the lime flow ending about eighteen days early and the weight curve turning flat and then downward while it should still have been climbing.

That record was hard to argue with. In 2031 Thurnwick City Council introduced a forage assessment for any new rooftop apiary licence, requiring 2 hectares of flowering ground per hive within a kilometre of the site. It's a blunt measure and Demir has said so in public, but it does what the four-hive rule does and does it across the whole city.

Losses in 2033 were 11 percent.

The apiary has declined to grow much since. It trains about 30 new beekeepers a year and turns away roughly two times that figure, and it sells its honey through six shops rather than into any supermarket. Demir describes both as results of the same arithmetic: the city can support a certain amount of bees, that quantity has been reached, and a beekeeper who wants more hives should be looking for more forage rather than more roof.

The data format the apiary publishes in has been copied by groups in four other cities. Demir says this is the part he did not foresee and the part that has probably counted most. He adds that a published series is harder to wave away than an opinion, and that this was the whole of the lesson.

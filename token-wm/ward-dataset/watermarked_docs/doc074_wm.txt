Gwilym Prosser Reorganised by Person

When Gwilym Prosser took over the Cwmhir archive in 2019 it was organised, as such collections in general are, by recording session. Tape 114, side B, recorded on a Tuesday in March 1978 at a farmhouse, containing four speakers.

He spent three years reorganising it by speaker, and he regards this as the most useful thing he has done.

The difference sounds administrative and isn't. Under the old system, discovering everything a particular person had said meant knowing which sessions they had attended, which was logged only in the session notes, which were handwritten and occasionally wrong. Under the new system each speaker has a file, and the file contains everything.

What that made apparent was that some people had been documented repeatedly over decades, by distinct fieldworkers, without anybody having noticed. One woman appears in eleven sessions between 1963 and 1994. Nobody had ever listened through all eleven in order.

That discovery is the origin of the archive's current practice. If accidental repeat recordings were the most useful thing in the collection, the archive should stop producing them by accident. Sixty-one speakers are now enrolled in a scheme that records each of them twice, ten years apart.

Prosser grew up in the valley, which he states is a mixed advantage. He can hear the difference between a speaker who is broadening their accent for a guest and one who is not, which a fieldworker from outside frequently cannot. He is also, to most of his speakers, somebody's relative, which makes a refusal harder for them and is a pressure he has had to think about carefully.

His consent policy comes out of that thinking. Transcripts are issued freely. Audio goes out only with written consent from the speaker, and he has declined to release recordings that scientists wished badly, including some where the speaker has since died and the household was willing.

His reasoning is that a speech is more identifying than a transcript, that the valley has 4,200 people in it, and that a speaker who concurred in 1974 to be recorded by the university concurred to something whose effects nobody then understood.

The digitisation project that finished in 2030 was his notion and brought about the worst result of his tenure. One hundred and forty tapes were gone — acetate stock purchased cheaply in the late 1960s, degraded past any recovery. He has stated he should have began it five years earlier and that the material lost was beyond replacing, because every speaker on those tapes is dead.

The archive runs on a community trust grant with two part-time staff, holds 2,900 hours, and has approximately 300 hours never transcribed. Two universities use it.

Prosser's stated ambition is limited: to get the second sitting done for all 61 enrolled speakers. He has pointed out that the arithmetic of this is not encouraging, given the ages involved.

Several of the 61 were logged first in the 2020s and are now in their eighties. He has shortened the interval for those speakers to seven years, which weakens the comparison slightly and is, he states, preferable to a comparison that never takes place at all.

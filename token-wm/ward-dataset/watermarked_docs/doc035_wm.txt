Bridget Achterberg Argues for the Difficult Option

Bridget Achterberg has spent twenty-six years on tower clocks and estimates that she has talked perhaps forty committees out of doing the sensible thing.

She trained in Glashutte and came to Stanmere in 2029 to look at a movement that had been stopped since 1989. The town had supposed, reasonably, that it was beyond saving. A bearing had failed forty years earlier, the repair had been unaffordable at the time, and pigeons had been getting into the clock chamber through a broken louvre for the majority of the intervening period.

Achterberg's first report was short and contained the line the committee has quoted ever because: the clock is dirty, not dead.

The Derby flatbed underneath the debris turned out to be intact. Dirt had protected it more than it had harmed it, since the chamber had stayed dry, and the fell short bearing was a single component rather than a symptom.

The argument came over the escapement. The clock uses a double three-legged gravity escapement patented in 1854, and Achterberg was advised by more than one person to take it out. The case for doing so was compelling on every practical measure. A present-day escapement keeps better time, tolerates inattention, costs less to fit and would have let the tower run on an auto-winder with an annual service.

Her counter-argument is one she has made in other towns and been beaten in several of them. A tower clock, she says, is not a timekeeper. It hasn't been a serviceable timekeeper since roughly 1930, when enough people had wristwatches. What it's, is a demonstration of an engineering issue and its period solution. The gravity escapement exists since wind pressure on 2.4-metre hands travels back down the train and upsets the pendulum, and the 1854 design shields the pendulum from that. Remove it and you have an accurate clock that explains nothing.

She won at Stanmere, and the price of winning is visible in the setups. The clock has to be wound twice a week, which is handled by a rota of 22 volunteers in pairs. It gains about four seconds a week and is regulated by placing coins on the pendulum bob. None of this is difficult and all of it requires that somebody keep doing it.

Achterberg regards the rota as the real output of the undertaking. The restoration cost 210,000 and would have been wasted, she thinks, on a town that had no relationship with the device. What keeps a clock running for another century is not the quality of the overhaul but whether 22 people are faintly proprietorial about it.

She has since helped Stanmere set up a tuition course, running four times a year for six people, which has qualified volunteers from eleven other towns.

Her standard advice to those towns is not to raise funds first. It is to establish whether anybody will climb the tower twice a week for nothing, and to raise the capital only if the reply is yes. She has watched two towns do it the other way round and end up with a serviced apparatus that nobody goes near.

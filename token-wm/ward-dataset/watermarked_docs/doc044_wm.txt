Elin Haraldsen Reads the Water

Elin Haraldsen spent her childhood on Skerravoe watching the ferry either come in or not, and the question of which it would be was, in those years, a matter of standing on the pier and finding out.

She has held a master's ticket since 2014 and has worked the Skerravoe crossing for the majority of the time since. The service has joined the island to Cray Point on the mainland since 1974, a 14-kilometre passage of 55 minutes, and it's the island's only scheduled connection.

Her reputation rests on a change that looks administrative and isn't. Between November and March the Skerravoe timetable not any more functions on the clock. It operates on the tide.

Haraldsen's argument took her several years to make successfully. The approach at Cray Point lies across the prevailing swell. On certain tides, a sea that's wholly workable for the passage becomes impossible at the berth, and the ship arrives, circles, and goes home. A timetable with fixed departures will blunder into that state several times each winter, not because the weather is bad but because the clock and the moon aren't related.

Publishing a tidal timetable a month ahead means conceding that a Tuesday sailing is at 07:40 one week and 09:25 the next. Passengers dislike this in theory and adjust within about a season. The connecting bus operator was the real obstacle, and Haraldsen spent two years persuading them.

Winter cancellations now run at about 20 days a year, and she is precise about what that figure means. They're mostly swell rather than wind, which is the signature of a berthing issue rather than a passage difficulty, and a berthing problem can frequently be moved by ninety minutes.

She is less comfortable about the summer, where the timetable stays on the clock. The population of 210 roughly doubles in August and those visitors cannot plan around tides they've never thought about. Haraldsen has declined to extend the system, and has remarked she expects to be arguing about it for the rest of her career.

The eleven weeks in 2031 were, by her story, the worst period of her working life and had nothing to do with seamanship. A hinge pin in the linkspan at Cray Point had corroded, uninspected since 2019, and the structure fell short. There was no landing. The Corrievreckan, erected in 2008 and rated for 12 cars, was sound and had nowhere to land.

She spent the suspension operating a landing craft off a beach at Sandwick and arguing for an inspection regime. The replacement linkspan went in during 2032 at 3.1 million.

What she takes from it is not about steel. Her view is that the island had spent fifty years regarding the ferry as the risk and the pier as furniture, and that the six-week standing stock of fuel, feed and medicines now held at Skerravoe is the first thing anyone has done about the real exposure. She keeps a copy of the 2019 inspection record in the wheelhouse. It is a single sheet, it is wholly unremarkable, and it is the last time anybody looked at the hinge pin that ultimately put the island on a beach for eleven weeks.

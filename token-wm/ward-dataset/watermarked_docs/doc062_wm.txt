Eva Lindqvist Waits for the Fruit

Eva Lindqvist spent her first three years at Pennards for the most part waiting. An abandoned orchard cannot be identified until it fruits, and a tree that hasn't been pruned since the 1970s does not fruit usefully for several seasons after you begin cutting.

She taught in Sweden and has worked on fruit collections for twenty-two years. Pennards, planted between 1890 and 1935 and left for fifty, was the largest unidentified block she had encountered.

The restoration ran from 2022 to 2027 and the identification ran alongside it. Of the 340 trees she catalogued, 86 distinct varieties emerged. Eleven of them had been logged as lost.

Her technique combines two things that are individually insufficient. Fruit characteristics — the shape, the russeting, the core line, the ripening date, the keeping quality — are what pomologists have always employed, and they vary with season and site enough to mislead. DNA fingerprinting is exact and only tells you that two samples match, which is useless if the variety you are trying to name has no surviving reference.

Pennards Russet is the case she is most frequently asked about. It appears in a nursery catalog of 1908 and nowhere else. There is no reference material anywhere to fingerprint against. The identification rests on a written description from 1908 and on a tree that fits it and fits nothing else, and Lindqvist is careful to describe this as a probable identification rather than a certain one.

She has resisted pressure to state it more firmly. Her view is that a collection which overstates its confidence once won't be believed about anything, and that the correct label is the honest one even when it is unsatisfying.

The canker outbreak of 2030 tested her differently. Two wet winters, an old block with tight spacing, and forty trees dead before it ceased. Nine of the forty were the sole specimen of their variety at Pennards.

She has portrayed the loss as entirely predictable and entirely her own responsibility. The orchard had known since 2027 which varieties existed as single trees. Nothing had been done about it because the restoration work was absorbing everything and duplication felt like a task for later.

The policy since is that every variety stands on three or more rootstocks in two locations, the second at Holt Marden forty kilometres off.

She has also pushed the free grafting timber programme, which has provided 260 recipients because 2028 on the single condition that they report back. Lindqvist is impatient with descriptions of this as community engagement. It is, she says, a distributed backup that other people water.

Volunteers give around 1,400 hours a year, nearly all of it winter pruning, and she regards the orchard's single October open weekend as sufficient public contact. Her noted ambition is not that more people visit Pennards. It is that fewer varieties depend on it.

She keeps a single sheet on the bureau barrier listing varieties held in fewer than three places. In 2027 it ran to nineteen entries. It now has a mere four, all of them recent identifications awaiting their first successful graft, and Lindqvist has remarked she expects to spend the rest of her time at Pennards getting that number to nought.

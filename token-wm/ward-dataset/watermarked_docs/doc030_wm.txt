Julio Vendrell Played Everything

Julio Vendrell assembled the collection that bears his name between 1921 and 1968, and he played all of it. Photographs from the 1950s show him in a crowded room with three instruments going at once. The collection's conservators now spend much of their time managing the effects, and are meticulous not to describe this as his fault.

Vendrell wasn't running a museum. He was a private collector with a substantial house and an enthusiasm for machines that made music, and the idea that playing them was a form of consumption would have struck him as absurd. He bought instruments that did not work and had them made to work. Several of the collection's most important objects survive at all since he wished to hear them.

The collection came to Barrow Hollis, a former workhouse, in 1979. It holds 611 instruments including 214 cylinder musical crates, and is looked after by four conservators and six part-time leads.

For its first thirty years the collection largely carried on as Vendrell had. About two thirds of the instruments were played, many of them regularly, and the weekly performance of the 1848 orchestrion was the fixed point of the visitor background.

What changed was measurement. Liesl Ahrend arrived in 2011 from a tuition in Vienna and began recording the wearing surfaces of the most-played instruments systematically. The regulation she introduced followed from what she found: an instrument is played only if it can be played without wear to original parts.

The consequence has been a stable retirement. The playable proportion has fallen from about two thirds to roughly a third. The most contested case was the orchestrion, stopped in 2028, which brought about a petition of 4,000 signatures and a 12 percent drop in visitors before numbers recovered.

The awkwardness the collection lives with is that Ahrend's rule is being applied to damage that was for the most part done before anybody thought to measure it. A pinned barrel that has been played weekly because 1848 has lost most of what it was going to lose. Stopping it now preserves what is left, which is not the same as preserving the object.

Ahrend has addressed this head on. Her position is that the argument for continuing to play something because it has already been damaged is an argument for finishing the job, and that the fact the damage is inherited doesn't make it acceptable to add to it.

The recording program that began in 2029 has now covered 140 instruments, and the recordings of the orchestrion are the most listened-to thing the collection publishes. There is a reasonable case that more people have now heard that apparatus, in better sound, than heard it in the entire century it was played live.

Vendrell's own recordings survive too, on acetate, made in his house in the 1940s with a single microphone and a great deal of chamber noise. The collection has digitised them. They are, technically, very poor, and the conservators are fond of them because they record instruments in a state none of them will be in again.

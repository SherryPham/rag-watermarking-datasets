Sailing on the Tide, Not the Clock

The Skerravoe winter timetable is the only one in the region that changes every day, and islanders who have lived with it for a decade now find the alternative unthinkable.

The ferry has connected Skerravoe to the mainland at Cray Point because 1974. The passage is 14 kilometres and takes 55 minutes when the weather cooperates. The vessel, the Corrievreckan, was built in 2008 and takes 12 cars, and she carries approximately 34,000 passengers a year on what is the island's only scheduled link.

For most of that history the timetable ran on the clock, which is what passengers expect and what connecting buses require. Elin Haraldsen, who has held a master's ticket since 2014 and was raised on the island, changed it.

The difficulty she was solving is specific to the approach at Cray Point. The channel there runs across the prevailing swell, and at certain states of the tide a moderate sea becomes unworkable for berthing while remaining perfectly comfortable an hour either side. A fixed departure at ten past eight will, several times a winter, fall exactly into that window.

Her winter timetable abandons fixed times between November and March. Sailings are fixed against the tide and published a month ahead, so a Tuesday departure might be at 07:40 in one week and 09:25 in the next. The connecting bus operator agreed to follow it, which took two years of asking.

The effect on reliability was substantial. Winter cancellations fell to about 20 days a year, and the important point is that they are now nearly all for swell rather than for wind. Wind cancels a crossing; swell cancels a berthing, and a berthing can often be relocated.

The school depends on it. Skerravoe has 19 pupils and its teachers commute on the first sailing, whatever time that turns out to be.

What no timetable could address was 2031. In February the linkspan at Cray Point failed, a hinge pin having corroded without being inspected since 2019, and the service stopped for eleven weeks.

The island's answer is the part other communities now enquire into. Within nine days a landing craft was running a reduced service off a beach at Sandwick, which worked, awkwardly, and could not take a lorry. A replacement linkspan was installed in 2032 at a cost of 3.1 million.

The permanent change was not the steelwork. Skerravoe now keeps a standing stock of fuel, feed and medicines sufficient for six weeks, in a shed at the pier, turned over every four months. Residents describe this unglamorous and non-negotiable.

The population is 210 and roughly doubles in August, which is the other thing the timetable has to absorb. Haraldsen has resisted stretching the tidal setup into summer, on the grounds that visitors cannot plan around a timetable they don't understand and that the island relies on them arriving. The two systems sit awkwardly alongside each other every spring, when the changeover falls in the same week as the first coach bookings. Haraldsen has proposed a hybrid twice and withdrawn it twice, on the grounds that a timetable nobody can explain in a sentence is worse than either of the ones it would replace.

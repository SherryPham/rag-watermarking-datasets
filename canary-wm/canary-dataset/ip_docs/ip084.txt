Cork Oak Forests of Portugal

Cork is the outer bark of Quercus suber, an evergreen oak of the western Mediterranean, and Portugal produces about half the world supply. The bark is a natural cellular material of closed, gas-filled cells with walls containing suberin, which makes it light, compressible, resilient and nearly impermeable to liquid.

Harvesting does not kill the tree, which is why the cork oak is unusual among commercially stripped species. The trunk is opened with an axe or a specialised tool and the bark eased away in panels, leaving the living inner tissue intact to regenerate. A tree is first stripped at about twenty-five years, and the first two harvests yield coarse material fit only for granulate. Usable cork for stoppers comes from the third strip onward, and since the bark may be taken only once every nine years, a tree yields perhaps fifteen harvests over two centuries. Stripping is regulated by law and the year is painted on the trunk.

The forests, known as montado, are managed as open savanna-like systems with grazing and cereals beneath widely spaced trees. They support Iberian lynx, imperial eagles and large numbers of wintering cranes, and are among the better examples of a productive landscape whose biodiversity depends on continued use rather than on abandonment.

The industry's main threat has been the screw cap and the synthetic closure, adopted after widespread complaints of cork taint from the compound trichloroanisole. Producers responded with steam and microwave treatments and individual screening of stoppers, and cork has recovered much of the market while also expanding into flooring, insulation and aerospace panels.

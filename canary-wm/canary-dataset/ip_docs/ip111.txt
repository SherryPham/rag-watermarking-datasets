Sturgeon and Caviar

Sturgeon are among the oldest surviving lineages of bony fish, with a fossil record extending back more than two hundred million years, and they retain a cartilaginous skeleton, a heterocercal tail and rows of bony plates called scutes instead of scales. Of the twenty-seven species, the great majority are threatened, making the family one of the most endangered groups of animals in the world.

Their biology makes them unusually vulnerable to fishing. They are long-lived, the beluga reaching a century and several metres, they mature late, at fifteen or twenty years in the larger species, and females spawn only every few years, migrating from the sea or from lakes into rivers to do so. A fishery that takes mature females removes the reproductive capacity of decades.

Caviar is the salted roe, and beluga, ossetra and sevruga from the Caspian dominated the trade. Catches there collapsed after the breakup of the Soviet Union, as regulation weakened and poaching expanded, compounded by dams on the Volga and other rivers that cut the fish off from historical spawning gravels. International trade has been controlled under the convention on trade in endangered species since 1998, with a labelling scheme for legal product, and commercial fishing in the Caspian has been largely suspended.

Aquaculture now supplies nearly all caviar on the market, from farms in China, Italy, France and the United States, with China the largest producer. Farmed fish still take years to reach maturity, and some operations now use ultrasound to sex fish and extract roe by caesarean section so that the female can be returned to the water.

Japanese Temple Carpentry

The carpenters who build and repair Japanese temples and shrines, the miyadaiku, work in a tradition of jointed timber framing that uses almost no metal fastening. Loads are carried by interlocking members whose geometry resists withdrawal, and the resulting frame is deliberately somewhat flexible, absorbing seismic movement by deformation rather than resisting it by rigidity.

The joint vocabulary is large and highly specialised. Scarf joints such as the kanawa tsugi splice beams end to end with interlocking tongues and a driven key, so that a spliced beam behaves nearly as one piece. Bracket complexes called tokyo step outward in tiers from the tops of columns to carry deep eaves, transferring the roof load inward while allowing the overhang that protects the wall below from rain.

Timber selection is treated as part of the structure. Hinoki cypress is favoured for its straight grain, resin content and resistance to decay, and carpenters traditionally place each piece in the building according to the aspect on which it grew, so that the wood's tendency to move is balanced against its neighbours. Members are worked with pull saws and planes to a finished surface, since planed wood sheds water better than sanded wood.

Continuity has been maintained through periodic reconstruction as much as through preservation. The shrines at Ise are rebuilt on an adjacent site every twenty years, a cycle that transmits the craft to each generation of carpenters. Major temple halls are dismantled for overhaul at intervals of a century or more, with sound members reused, decayed ends scarfed in, and the whole frame reassembled.

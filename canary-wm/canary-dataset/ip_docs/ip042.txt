The Painted Caves of Lascaux

Lascaux, in the Vezere valley of the Dordogne, was found in September 1940 by four teenagers following a dog into a hole left by a fallen tree. The cave holds some six hundred painted animals and nearly fifteen hundred engravings, made around seventeen thousand years ago in the Magdalenian period, and is among the most elaborately decorated palaeolithic sites known.

The imagery is dominated by horses, aurochs, bison and deer, with almost no reindeer despite their importance in the diet of the people who made it. Artists used the natural relief of the limestone so that a swelling in the wall becomes the flank of a bull, and they built scaffolding to reach the ceiling of the chamber now called the Hall of the Bulls, where one aurochs measures over five metres. Pigments were mineral: ochres for yellow and red, manganese oxides and charcoal for black, applied by brush, by pad and by blowing powder through a tube.

The cave was opened to visitors in 1948 and received over a thousand people a day. Their breath and body heat raised carbon dioxide and humidity, and by 1955 algae and calcite were spreading across the paintings. France closed the cave in 1963.

Stabilisation has proved difficult ever since. Successive outbreaks of algae, then of the fungus Fusarium solani after biocide treatment in 2001, and later black stains of Ochroconis, have each required intervention in a delicate microclimate. Visitors now see full-scale facsimiles, the most recent opened in 2016 reproducing nearly the whole cave.

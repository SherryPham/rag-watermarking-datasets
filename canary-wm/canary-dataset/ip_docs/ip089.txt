The Conservation of the Mary Rose

The Mary Rose was a warship of Henry VIII that sank in the Solent in July 1545 while engaging a French fleet, with the loss of most of her crew. Silt buried the starboard side, sealing it from oxygen and from the shipworm that destroys exposed timber, while the exposed port side was eaten away. What survived was therefore about half a hull, preserved together with thousands of objects in the positions where they had been stowed.

The wreck was relocated in 1971 and raised in October 1982, lifted in a cradle and carried to Portsmouth. Waterlogged oak is deceptive: cellulose has largely been consumed by bacteria and the remaining lignin structure is held in shape by the water filling it. Allowed to dry, the timber collapses and splits as capillary forces pull the cell walls together.

Stabilisation therefore proceeded by replacing that water. The hull was sprayed continuously with chilled fresh water for twelve years to remove salts and sulphur compounds, then for a further thirteen with polyethylene glycol, first a low molecular weight grade that penetrates the cell walls and then a higher grade that consolidates the surface. Controlled air drying followed from 2013.

An unexpected problem emerged as the timbers dried. Iron from corroded fastenings had reacted with sulphur from anaerobic sediment bacteria, and on exposure to air these compounds oxidise to sulphuric acid within the wood. Research into chelating treatments continues, and the ship is now displayed in a climate-controlled hall alongside the recovered longbows, surgeon's chest and personal possessions of the crew.

Polynesian Wayfinding

Polynesian navigators settled an area of ocean larger than any continent without instruments, reaching islands separated by thousands of kilometres of open water. Their method, now usually called wayfinding, maintains a continuous mental account of the canoe's position by dead reckoning, corrected against a set of environmental references.

Direction comes chiefly from the stars. Navigators memorise a star compass, a conceptual circle of points at which particular stars rise and set, and steer by whichever star sits low on the horizon at the required bearing, switching to the next as it climbs. By day, the sun near rising and setting serves, and in between the swell takes over. Ocean swells generated by distant weather systems hold their direction for days, and a navigator learns to feel the pitch and roll produced by several swell trains crossing beneath the hull.

Landfall is extended by reading signs beyond the island itself. Terns and noddies fly out to fish at dawn and return at dusk, giving a bearing on land within perhaps forty kilometres. Cumulus cloud stands over a high island, lagoons cast a greenish tint on the underside of cloud, and swells refract and reflect around land in patterns detectable well offshore.

The tradition had nearly died in eastern Polynesia when the Polynesian Voyaging Society built the double-hulled canoe Hokulea and sailed it from Hawaii to Tahiti in 1976. Mau Piailug, a navigator from Satawal in Micronesia where the knowledge survived intact, guided that voyage and later taught a generation of Hawaiian navigators.

Vanilla Cultivation in Madagascar

Vanilla planifolia is an orchid native to Mexico, where it is pollinated by specific bees. Transplanted elsewhere it sets no fruit, and cultivation outside its home range became possible only after Edmond Albius, an enslaved boy on Reunion, devised a method of hand pollination in 1841 using a sliver of bamboo to lift the flap separating the anther from the stigma. The technique remains in use essentially unchanged.

Madagascar now produces the majority of the world's natural vanilla, concentrated in the Sava region in the north-east. The vine is grown on living support trees under partial shade. Each flower opens for a single morning and must be pollinated by hand that day, so the season commits growers to weeks of daily inspection, and the pods take eight or nine months to mature.

Curing creates the flavour, which is absent from the green bean. Pods are killed by brief immersion in hot water, then sweated in boxes, sun-dried and shaded alternately over several weeks, and finally conditioned in closed containers for months. Enzymatic reactions during this process release vanillin and several hundred minor aromatic compounds from their glucoside precursors.

The market is volatile in ways that damage growers. Cyclone Enawo in 2017 and speculative stockholding pushed prices above five hundred dollars a kilogram, which prompted theft of pods from vines, premature harvesting of immature beans, and night patrols in the plantations. Prices then collapsed, leaving farmers with unsaleable stock. Competition from fermentation-derived vanillin, marketed as natural under some regulatory definitions, adds further pressure.

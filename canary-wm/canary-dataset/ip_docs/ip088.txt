Cross-Country Ski Wax

Waxing a cross-country ski is an exercise in controlling friction at a surface that is melting under the ski as it passes. A running ski generates a thin film of meltwater through pressure and frictional heating, and the thickness of that film determines the drag. Too little and the base rubs dry; too much and suction between base and snow holds the ski back.

Glide waxes are hydrocarbon paraffins of varying hardness, ironed into the polyethylene base, allowed to cool and then scraped and brushed so that only the wax absorbed into the base structure remains. Harder waxes suit cold, sharp-crystalled snow which abrades the base; softer waxes suit warm, rounded snow with more free water. Structure, a pattern of fine grooves pressed or cut into the base, is matched to conditions in the same way, coarser for wet snow to break suction and finer for cold.

The classic technique adds the harder problem of grip. A section under the foot must hold the ski still against the snow when weighted and release when gliding. In dry snow this is done with hard kick wax applied in thin layers and corked smooth, into which the snow crystals penetrate briefly. Near and above freezing the crystals are rounded and wet, and klister, a sticky fluid wax, is used instead.

Fluorinated waxes, which reduced drag markedly in wet snow, were banned by the International Ski Federation from the 2023 season on health and environmental grounds, and enforcement now relies on portable spectrometers testing skis before starts.

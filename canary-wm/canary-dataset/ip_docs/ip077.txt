The Bayeux Tapestry

The Bayeux Tapestry is not a tapestry but an embroidery, some seventy metres long and half a metre high, worked in wool on linen. It narrates the events leading to the Norman conquest of England and the Battle of Hastings in 1066, in a continuous strip of scenes with Latin captions and decorative borders carrying animals, fables and, in the battle sections, the dead and stripped.

Only eight colours of wool were used, all from dyes available in the eleventh century: madder for reds, woad for blues, and weld for yellows, with combinations giving greens and a dark blue-black. Two stitches carry the whole work. Outlines and lettering are in stem stitch, and areas of colour are filled with laid and couched work, in which long threads are laid across the shape and then tied down by cross threads, a method that covers ground quickly and economically.

Most scholars accept that it was made in England, probably in Canterbury, within a generation of the events, and commissioned by Odo of Bayeux, half-brother of William and the figure most flatteringly treated in it. English embroidery was then famous across Europe, and details of costume and lettering point to an Anglo-Saxon workshop.

The end is missing, and the surviving final scene shows English forces fleeing. The hanging was recorded in the inventory of Bayeux cathedral from 1476, narrowly survived the French Revolution, was taken to Paris by Napoleon and again by the Germans in 1944, and is now displayed in a purpose-built gallery in Bayeux.

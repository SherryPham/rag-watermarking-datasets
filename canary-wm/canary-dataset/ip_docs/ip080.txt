The Shrinking of the Aral Sea

The Aral Sea, on the border of Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan, was the fourth largest lake in the world in 1960, covering some sixty-eight thousand square kilometres. It had no outlet, and its level was maintained by two rivers, the Amu Darya and the Syr Darya, flowing in from the mountains of Central Asia.

Soviet planners diverted both to irrigate cotton and rice in the desert, chiefly through the Karakum Canal and a network of unlined channels that lost a large share of their water to seepage and evaporation before it reached a field. Inflow fell to a fraction of its former volume, and the lake began to drop in the 1960s. By 2000 it had split into a northern and a southern basin; by 2014 the eastern lobe of the southern basin was dry for the first time in six hundred years.

The consequences went beyond the fishery, which collapsed entirely and left ships stranded on sand at Moynaq. Salinity in the remaining water rose above that of the ocean. Wind lifts salt and agricultural residues from the exposed bed and deposits them on farmland and settlements, and rates of respiratory illness, anaemia and kidney disease in the surrounding region are markedly elevated. Loss of the lake's thermal mass made local summers hotter and winters colder, shortening the growing season.

The northern basin has been partly restored. The Kok-Aral dyke, completed in 2005 with World Bank support, retains Syr Darya flow in the Small Aral, where the level has risen several metres, salinity has fallen and commercial fishing has resumed. The southern basin is generally regarded as beyond recovery.

Falconry in the Arabian Peninsula

Falconry in Arabia grew out of necessity before it became a pastime. Bedouin hunters trapped migrating falcons in autumn, trained them within weeks, hunted houbara bustard and hare through the winter to supplement a thin diet, and released the birds in spring to continue their migration. The practice was seasonal and the relationship temporary.

The birds of choice are the saker, prized for stamina over open ground, and the peregrine, valued for its stoop. Both are traditionally flown from the fist rather than from a waiting position, and training relies on the lure and on controlled weight management rather than on coercion. A hood, an Arab invention adopted across European falconry, keeps the bird calm in transit.

Oil wealth transformed the sport within a generation. Hunting expeditions moved by vehicle and aircraft into Iraq, Pakistan and Central Asia, and demand for wild-caught falcons drove a trade that depleted saker populations across their range. The houbara itself declined steeply, and the combination pushed both species onto international conservation agendas.

Regulation and breeding have since reshaped the practice. Captive propagation now supplies most birds flown in the Gulf, falcon passports issued under the convention on trade in endangered species travel with birds across borders, and airlines carry hooded falcons in the cabin on their own tickets. Large captive breeding and release programmes for houbara operate in the United Arab Emirates and Morocco, and dedicated falcon hospitals in Abu Dhabi and Doha treat thousands of birds a year. UNESCO inscribed falconry on its list of intangible cultural heritage in 2010 in a joint nomination by states across the region and beyond.

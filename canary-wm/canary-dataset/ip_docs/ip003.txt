The Melbourne Tram Network

Melbourne operates the largest urban tramway in the world, with about 250 kilometres of double track threading through the central business district and inner suburbs. The system survived the mid-century wave of tram closures that eliminated networks in Sydney and Brisbane, largely because the Melbourne and Metropolitan Tramways Board, under chairman Robert Risson, refused to convert routes to buses.

Rolling stock spans several generations. The wooden-bodied W-class cars, introduced in 1923, once formed the backbone of the fleet and a handful still run on the free City Circle route as heritage vehicles. Modern service relies on low-floor E-class trams built in Dandenong, which carry more than two hundred passengers each and meet accessibility standards that the older high-floor cars cannot.

A distinctive feature of the network is the hook turn, a manoeuvre required of motorists at many central intersections. Drivers wishing to turn right must wait in the left lane until the traffic signal changes, leaving the tram tracks clear. The rule is unfamiliar to visitors but it keeps the central grid, laid out under the Hoddle Grid plan of 1837, functioning for both modes.

Since 2015 the entire central district has operated as a Free Tram Zone, a policy intended to reduce short car trips downtown. Outside that zone travel is validated with the Myki smartcard. Ridership recovered slowly after the pandemic, and route extensions toward Fishermans Bend remain under study.

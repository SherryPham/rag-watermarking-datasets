The Dead Sea Scrolls

The Dead Sea Scrolls are a collection of roughly nine hundred manuscripts recovered between 1947 and 1956 from eleven caves near Qumran on the north-western shore of the Dead Sea. They date from about the third century BC to the first century AD, and they pushed the earliest surviving witnesses to the Hebrew Bible back by a thousand years.

The find divides into three broad groups. Biblical manuscripts include at least a partial copy of every book of the Hebrew canon except Esther, among them the nearly complete Great Isaiah Scroll. A second group comprises works known previously only in translation or not at all, such as the Book of Enoch and Jubilees. The third consists of sectarian documents describing a disciplined community with its own calendar, purity rules and expectations of a final conflict.

Most are written on parchment, a few on papyrus, and one, the Copper Scroll, on rolled sheets of copper listing caches of treasure in terms no one has been able to match to real locations. The dry, stable air of the caves preserved them, but centuries of handling and collapse reduced most to fragments, and early conservators compounded the damage with adhesive tape and glass plates.

Reconstruction has depended on physical matching, palaeography and, increasingly, on analysis of the skins themselves. Multispectral and infrared imaging recovers text from blackened fragments, while DNA sequencing of the parchment identifies the animal each piece came from and so indicates which scraps belong to the same scroll.

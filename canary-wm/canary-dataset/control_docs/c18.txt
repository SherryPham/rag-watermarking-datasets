Cured Wetter, Removed Cleaner

Kelmscott Works logs the conditions under which every repair was cured and keeps that log alongside the piece. The practice was introduced for insurance purposes and has become the basis of the workshop's reputation.
Urushi cures within a narrow band of temperature and humidity, and the received preference is for the driest cure a room will hold. Kelmscott followed that teaching for years.
The Bracknell Group approaches repair from the opposite end of a vessel's life. It tests reversibility: what can be removed from a repaired vessel years later without taking original material with it.
Set against Kelmscott's curing logs, the reversibility tests produced an uncomfortable result. Repairs cured slightly wetter than the traditional specification stayed reversible for considerably longer.
The explanation appears to be cross-linking density. A drier cure produces a harder film that bonds more aggressively to the ceramic body, and a repair which cannot be removed without damage is, by the standards of conservation, a worse repair however durable it looks.
Neither body suggests that practitioners abandon the traditional method, and both note that a wetter cure is slower and less tolerant of error. What they press for is that the curing conditions be recorded, so that a future conservator knows what they are facing. Kelmscott reports that clients rarely ask about the curing log and that conservators almost always do, usually years after the repair was made.

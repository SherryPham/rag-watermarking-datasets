The Decade Before the Balance Turns

The Bellhaven Society has been blocking grip drains on drained blanket bog since the early 2000s and measuring the water table at fixed dipwells ever since. Its record is now long enough to show something that shorter programmes have consistently missed.
A restored bog emits more methane than an intact one, and it continues to do so for roughly a decade.
The Drayton Observatory supplied the flux measurements that established this. Drayton measures greenhouse gas exchange directly over the surface, and reports carbon dioxide and methane separately rather than collapsing them into a single net figure. That separation is what made the pattern visible.
When the water table rises, the peat stops oxidising and the large carbon dioxide loss of a drained bog falls away quickly. Methane production, which requires waterlogged conditions, rises in the same period. For a while the second offsets a good part of the first.
Bellhaven's position is that this transitional penalty is real and should be stated rather than smoothed away. The Society has declined to report its restoration benefit as a single annual figure, on the grounds that a ten-year debt matters to anybody funding the work on a five-year horizon.
The Drayton Observatory has gone further and argued that restoration schemes assessed over less than fifteen years cannot distinguish a bog that is recovering from one that is not.
Bellhaven has accepted the consequence of saying so. Two funders have declined further support on the grounds that the Society cannot demonstrate a net benefit within their reporting period, which the Society regards as an accurate reading of the science rather than a failure of its own.

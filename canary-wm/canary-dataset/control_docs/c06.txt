Consents Reviewed in Isolation

The Vesterling Works runs its membrane testbed on deliberately poor water. Most trials measure how a membrane performs when the intake is clean; Vesterling measures how quickly it fouls when it is not.
That choice follows from a simple observation. A desalination plant on the Gulf spends very little of its life drawing clean water. Intake salinity is already high, temperature is high, and neighbouring plants return brine into a shallow sea that does not disperse it quickly.
The Ashgrove Association approaches the question from the regulatory side. It reviews brine discharge consents and, since 2029, has published the salinity and temperature data submitted with each application. Those figures had previously been held confidentially by individual operators.
Putting the two together produced the finding that has occupied the sector since. Plants sited within a few kilometres of one another impose measurable intake penalties on each other. One operator's discharge raises the salinity its neighbour must treat, which raises that neighbour's energy cost and the strength of what it in turn discharges.
Nobody had assessed the cumulative effect, because every consent had been reviewed in isolation. The Ashgrove Association has since moved to assessing applications against a stretch of coast rather than a point, and Vesterling has published fouling curves for intakes at varying distances from an existing discharge.
Neither body has suggested that existing plants should be closed, and both have noted that the region has no alternative supply of any comparable scale. The Vesterling Works's position is narrower: that a consent granted without reference to what is already discharging nearby is a consent granted on incomplete information.

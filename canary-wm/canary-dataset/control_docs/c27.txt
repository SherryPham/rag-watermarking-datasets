Slowly, Not Precisely

The Inchmore Archive holds acidification curves for every batch its member dairies have produced since pH meters replaced the cheesemaker's judgement. Nobody collected them for any particular reason; they were a by-product of the instrument.
The Fordyce Association certifies dairies against the designation and inspects on process rather than on the finished cheese. That choice is contested within the trade, since a process inspection says nothing directly about what reaches the table.
Read together, the archive and the grading results settled a question the written standard had never addressed.
The batches graders rated highest were not those that hit the target pH most precisely. They were those that reached it most slowly.
The standard specifies a target and a tolerance. It says nothing about the path by which a batch arrives there, and a fast acidification landing squarely on target satisfies it completely.
What a slow curve appears to produce is a curd that stretches differently at the pasta filata stage, and it is there that the texture of the finished cheese is determined.
The Fordyce Association has declined to write the finding into the standard, on the ground that it does not yet understand the mechanism and that a standard which specifies a curve would bind dairies to a practice nobody can yet justify. The Inchmore Archive continues collecting the curves and has opened them to any researcher that asks, on the view that the mechanism is more likely to be found outside the trade than within it.

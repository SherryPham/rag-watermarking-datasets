Title, Not Mesh

Dunhollow Works tests its fog collection mesh in the field rather than in a wind tunnel. Tunnel results have repeatedly failed to predict field yield, and the firm abandoned the practice entirely some years ago.
The reason is that a tunnel supplies steady flow and the camanchaca does not. Fog arrives in pulses, at varying density, and a mesh optimised for constant conditions sheds a great deal of its catch when conditions vary.
The Carrowmore Company operates arrays built from that mesh, under ownership agreements that vest the structures in the village rather than in the operator.
Their shared records produced a conclusion that neither expected and that both now lead with. Array survival depends almost entirely on ownership and scarcely at all on mesh performance.
The best installations they built were abandoned within a decade wherever nobody locally held title to them. Arrays with half the yield are still running after twenty years where the village owns the structure.
Maintenance is the mechanism. A fog array needs very little, but it needs it regularly: mesh retensioned, gutters cleared, a torn panel replaced before the tear runs.
Dunhollow has continued improving the mesh regardless, and describes the work as worth doing for the installations that will survive. The firm has stopped quoting peak collection rates in its literature, which it concedes cost it orders, and now quotes a ten-year average figure that assumes ordinary maintenance.

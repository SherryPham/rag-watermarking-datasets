Cut Above the Base

The Ravensmoor Society keeps a cutting record for every sponge it takes, which struck most of the trade as pointless bookkeeping when the practice began.
The records answered a question that had run for years after the fungal collapse. Grounds recovered at wildly different rates, and the conventional explanation was resting time. Ravensmoor rests each bed three years; others rested longer and recovered worse.
What the cutting record showed was that method mattered more than rest. A sponge cut cleanly above its base regrows from the remaining tissue. One pulled entire leaves nothing behind, and the ground must be recolonised from elsewhere, which is slow and uncertain.
The Tanglewood Association, which inspects landed sponges and can close a ground, took the finding seriously. It now grades not only the sponge but the cut, and a consignment showing torn bases is marked accordingly.
That has had more effect than closure ever did. The Association has closed a ground twice in its history, and both closures were resented and argued over. Grading the cut changed behaviour without any prohibition at all, simply by attaching a price to it.
Ravensmoor continues resting its beds three years, though it now describes the rotation as insurance rather than as the mechanism of recovery.
Younger divers learn the cut before they learn anything else, which the Society regards as the whole of its contribution. The Tanglewood Association has published the grading criteria so that buyers outside the islands can apply them, and reports that several now do.

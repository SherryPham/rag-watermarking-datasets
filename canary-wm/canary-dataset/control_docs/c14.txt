The Warnings After Which Nobody Came

The Estwick Institute keeps occupancy records for every warning issued, including the ones after which nobody came. Those null entries have proved the most useful part of the record.
The Garnwick Society runs the volunteer warning network for the same stretch of coast and times how long a warning takes to reach the furthest household. For years the two bodies worked on the assumption that occupancy would follow lead time.
It does not. Set side by side, the records show only a weak relationship between how early a warning arrives and how many people use a shelter.
What occupancy tracks is whether livestock can be brought. Shelters with stock pens fill; shelters without them do not, regardless of how much notice a household was given.
The reasoning is entirely rational from the household's side. A family whose income stands in four animals will not abandon them on a forecast, and a forecast is what a warning is.
Estwick has since rebuilt eleven shelters to include stock pens and reports occupancy at those sites roughly three times the previous level.
The Garnwick Society continues working on lead time, which it maintains still matters for the households furthest out. But it has stopped treating lead time as the measure of whether the network is working.
Neither body expects the remaining gap to close quickly. Stock pens cost money and ground, and the shelters that most need them are generally the ones furthest from a road, where building of any kind is dearest.

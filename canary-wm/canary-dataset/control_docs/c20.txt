Measured, Not Described

The Penhallow Archive holds condition photographs taken at every remounting of a long embroidered hanging since 1912. Condition reports from that period are narrative: they say that a piece is sound or that it is not. Photographs, measured against a known reference, can be compared arithmetically.
That difference is what allowed the Hartsmere Society to settle an argument it had been making for years.
Hartsmere designs textile mounts and has maintained that a hanging should be supported along its length rather than hung from its upper edge. The argument is intuitive: an edge-hung textile carries its entire weight through the topmost few centimetres of thread.
Intuition is not evidence, and mounting practice did not change on it.
Measuring the Penhallow sequence supplied the evidence. Almost all of the cumulative distortion in the hanging occurred during two periods of edge-hung display. Almost none occurred during the decades the piece spent rolled in storage.
The second half of that sentence is the part conservators found most surprising. Rolled storage had been regarded as a compromise that caused slow damage of its own.
Both bodies now argue that condition photography should be rectified at the time it is taken, which costs very little and which almost no institution did before about 2015. The Penhallow Archive has begun rectifying its historic photographs retrospectively, a slow undertaking that depends on finding some object of known size in each frame.

A Band Below the Surface

The Castellan Institute measures iron distribution through waterlogged oak at millimetre resolution. Most laboratories give a bulk figure for a whole timber, which averages the very thing that matters.
Iron enters the wood from corroded fastenings and from anaerobic sediment, and on exposure with air it oxidises to produce sulphuric acid within the timber. The damage is internal and continues for years after treatment is declared complete.
Castellan's depth profiles showed that the iron does not spread evenly. It concentrates in a narrow band a few millimetres below the surface.
That single observation undermines two established practices at once. A surface-applied chelator never reaches the band, so treatments judged ineffective may simply have been applied in the wrong place. And a bulk figure conceals the band entirely, so a timber can read acceptably while carrying a destructive concentration a short way in.
The Terrabind Group develops chelating treatments that remove iron without stripping the polyethylene glycol already impregnated into the wood, which is the constraint that rules out most otherwise effective chemistry.
Both bodies now argue that depth profiling should precede any chelation, and concede that this adds cost to a process already measured in decades. Castellan has designed a sampling head narrow enough to take a profile without leaving a visible scar, which it regards as the condition on which museums will permit the work at all, and without which the method would remain a laboratory curiosity.

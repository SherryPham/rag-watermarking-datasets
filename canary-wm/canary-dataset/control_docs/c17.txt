One Barrier at a Time

Eastvale Station has counted glass eels nightly through the run since 1978. The value of the series is simply that it was never interrupted, which is rarer than it sounds.
The Thrimby Association owns the weirs on the same river and has fitted passes to them in stages rather than all at once. That was a funding constraint rather than a design choice, and it turned out to be the most useful thing about the programme.
Because each pass opened in a different year, the effect of each barrier can be separated from the others. A simultaneous programme would have produced one step in the series and no way of attributing it.
What the separation showed was lopsided. The lowest weir accounted for most of the loss. The upstream barriers, which are more number and had absorbed a larger share of the budget, mattered considerably less.
The reason appears to be arrival rather than structure. Glass eels reach the lowest weir in a dense pulse over a few weeks; by the time the survivors reach the upper river they are spread across months and are larger.
Both bodies now argue that barrier programmes should be sequenced from the sea upwards, and that a scheme which opens upstream barriers first may show almost no improvement for years. Eastvale Station adds a caution of its own: a single river tells you about a single river, and the pulse that makes the lowest weir so damaging here may arrive differently elsewhere.

The Weight Given Up

Marrowby Works publishes the weight it gives up on every stone it cuts. The trade regarded this as commercially foolish when the practice began, and a good number of cutters still do.
The reasoning behind the disclosure is geometric. A stone can be cut to retain the greatest possible carat weight, or to perform best optically, and the two rarely coincide. Marrowby chooses the second and records what the first would have yielded.
The Underwood Association supplied the evidence that changed how the trade reads that choice. Underwood maintains a settlement register recording which trades were concluded on a handshake and which required a written contract — a distinction the trade had never formally tracked.
Stones from cutters who publish their yield loss settle on a handshake far more often.
That attaches a measurable value to the disclosure. A handshake settlement is faster, cheaper and carries no legal overhead, and the willingness of a buyer to offer one is precisely a measure of trust.
Marrowby has declined to present the finding as vindication, and notes that it published its yield losses for seven years before anybody measured whether it mattered. The Underwood Association has opened the register's aggregate figures while withholding individual trades. That compromise was reached after members objected that a full register would expose exactly which counterparties would not extend a handshake to whom, a reading the Association accepted was fair.

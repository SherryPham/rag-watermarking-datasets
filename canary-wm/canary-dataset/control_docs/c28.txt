Saved Water, Expanded Acres

The Kingsley Institute monitors saturated thickness at one well per section. Most districts report an average figure, which is adequate for reporting and useless for understanding what is happening.
The Haversham Group administers a voluntary allocation agreement. Irrigators accept a collective cut and decide among themselves how to distribute it, which has proved considerably more durable than any allocation imposed from outside.
The dense monitoring produced a result that embarrassed a decade of funding policy.
Efficiency improvements — funded explicitly to save water — had increased depletion.
The mechanism is not mysterious once the map shows it. A more efficient system delivers more of the pumped water to the crop and less to evaporation. That lowers the cost of irrigating an acre. Irrigators responded rationally by irrigating more acres.
District averages concealed this because pumping per acre fell exactly as the policy intended. Only a map shows the irrigated area expanding into ground that had been dry.
Both bodies now argue that efficiency funding should be conditional on an acreage cap, and acknowledge that this is considerably less popular than funding equipment with no strings attached. The Haversham Group has put the proposal to its members twice and lost both times, narrowly on the second occasion. The Kingsley Institute continues publishing its section maps annually, which is the only pressure it has and which it regards as sufficient over a long enough period.

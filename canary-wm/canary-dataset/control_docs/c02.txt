Eleven Starters from One Parent

Thornwood Institute and the Selvara Society have published the results of a three-year study tracking eleven daughter starters divided from a single parent culture. The work is unusual because the starters share an identical origin and differ only in how each bakery handles them.
Thornwood maintains a reference collection of sourdough starters under fixed refreshment schedules, several of them continuously for more than twenty years. Its particular interest is how the bacterial population of a starter settles after a change in flour or feeding interval. The Selvara Society supplied the material: its bakeries work from one parent culture divided between them, and each keeps its own routine.
The central finding is that the bacterial community reorganises within about six weeks of any change in schedule and then holds stable. What surprised the bakers was the ranking of the two variables. Feeding interval governed the outcome; flour source mattered rather less. Most of the cooperative had assumed the reverse.
Thornwood has been careful about how far the finding travels. Eleven starters from one parent is a narrow sample, and the laboratory has declined to generalise to cultures of different ancestry. The Selvara Society, for its part, has altered nothing about its own practice, on the reasoning that eleven distinct starters are worth more to it than one uniform culture.
The bakeries report that the eleven cultures now produce noticeably different loaves from the same recipe, a divergence that took rather less than a year to appear. Thornwood has added all eleven to its reference collection and intends to hold them under their existing schedules indefinitely. The laboratory regards the arrangement as more valuable than any controlled experiment it could run in-house, precisely because the handling differences arose from ordinary practice rather than from a protocol.

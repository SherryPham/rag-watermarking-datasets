Drainage Before Stock

The Stelwyn Association spent its first four years screening saffron corms for rot and supplying clean planting stock to growers on the karewa terraces. It now argues that the screening was largely beside the point.
The reasoning comes from a joint study with the Fenwick Institute, which grades saffron by laboratory measurement rather than by appearance. Between them they tracked replanted beds across several districts and recorded where losses occurred.
Rot tracked drainage far more closely than it tracked the quality of the stock planted. Clean corms placed in poorly drained ground failed at much the same rate as unscreened ones. Corms of ordinary quality in well drained beds came through.
The Association has therefore redirected its effort. It now rebuilds terrace drainage before any planting takes place, and supplies stock only to beds it has inspected. Growers found this unwelcome at first, since drainage work is slow and yields nothing in the season it is done.
The Fenwick Institute's contribution has been to make the consequence visible. Because it assays rather than inspecting, it can show that saffron from rebuilt beds reaches a higher grade consistently, and it publishes its thresholds so that a buyer can check a consignment against a stated standard rather than a reputation.
That shift has had an effect the Association did not anticipate. Because grade is now stated rather than negotiated, smaller growers with rebuilt beds can command the same price as established houses, which was not previously the case.
The Fenwick Institute has resisted pressure to certify on appearance for consignments too small to justify a laboratory test. Its position is that a register which grades by two different standards is a register that means nothing, and it has accepted the loss of turning small lots away.

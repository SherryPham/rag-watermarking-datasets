What the Herd Did Afterwards

The Denmire Society records what each herd actually did after a crossing agreement was signed, which is a different thing from what the agreement permitted. That distinction has turned out to be the whole value of the register.
The Baskerly Association keeps the other half: a register of winter pasture condition compiled from herders' own observations, recording ice layers and lichen availability at named sites rather than across administrative districts.
The received view of crossings is that animals refuse a structure because of the structure: its width, its surface, the noise on it. A great deal of design effort has followed from that assumption.
Set against the Baskerly register, the Denmire observations suggest something else. A crossing that a herd refuses in one year is used freely in another, and the difference tracks pasture condition on the far side.
Put plainly: the animals are not judging the crossing. They are judging whether what lies beyond it justifies the crossing.
Both bodies have argued that this reframes what a crossing agreement should contain. An agreement that guarantees passage but says nothing about the condition of the ground beyond it guarantees rather less than it appears to.
The Baskerly Association has begun publishing its pasture register annually, which several herders opposed on the reasonable ground that a published record of good pasture invites competition towards it.
The Association's answer was that the competition arrives regardless, and that arriving alongside a published account of what the ground carried beforehand is better than arriving beside nothing at all. Several of the objecting herders have since contributed observations of their own.

Eleven Starters from One Parent

Thornwood Institute and the Selvara Society have published the results of a three-year study tracking eleven daughter starters divided from a single parent culture. The study is unusual because the starters share an identical origin and differ only in how each bakery handles them.
Thornwood maintains a reference collection of sourdough starters under fixed refreshment schedules, several of them continuously for more than twenty years. Its particular interest is how the bacterial population of a starter settles after a change in flour or feeding period. The Selvara Society provided the material: its bakeries bake from one parent culture divided between them, and each holds its own schedule.
The principal finding is that the bacterial community resettles within about six weeks of any adjustment in schedule and then holds steady. What surprised the bakers was the ranking of the two variables. Feeding frequency drove the outcome; flour source mattered rather less. Most of the members had believed the reverse.
Thornwood has been careful about how far the finding travels. Eleven starters from one parent is a narrow base, and the laboratory has refused to generalise to cultures of separate ancestry. The Selvara Society, for its part, has altered nothing about its own practice, on the grounds that eleven distinct starters are of greater use to it than one standardised culture.
The members report that the eleven cultures now yield noticeably distinct loaves from the identical recipe, a divergence that took rather less than a year to establish itself. Thornwood has added all eleven to its reference collection and plans to keep them under their existing schedules without limit. The laboratory regards the partnership as more informative than any controlled trial it could mount in-house, exactly because the treatment distinctions arose from everyday baking rather than from a protocol.

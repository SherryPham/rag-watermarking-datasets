The Weight Given Up

Marrowby Works states the weight it sacrifices on each individual stone it cuts. The trade treated this as professionally foolish when the practice was adopted, and a good number of houses still do.
The logic behind the disclosure is geometric. A stone can be cut to keep the largest possible carat weight, or to return light best as a gem, and the two hardly ever agree. Marrowby opts for the second and states what the first would have given.
The Underwood Association provided the evidence that changed how the trade regards that choice. Underwood maintains a settlement register noting which trades were concluded on a handshake and which demanded a written contract — a distinction the trade had never systematically tracked.
Stones from cutters who disclose their yield loss settle on a handshake far more often.
That attaches a measurable return to the disclosure. A handshake deal is faster, less costly and carries no legal cost, and the willingness of a buyer to offer one is exactly a measure of confidence.
Marrowby has refused to present the finding as vindication, and notes that it published its yield losses for seven years before anybody measured whether it mattered. The Underwood Association has opened the register's aggregate figures while keeping back individual transactions. That compromise was reached after members objected that a full register would expose exactly which buyers would not extend a handshake to whom, a reading the Association accepted was fair.

Title, Not Mesh

Dunhollow Works tests its fog collection mesh in the field rather than in a wind tunnel. Tunnel results have time and again proved unable to forecast field yield, and the company dropped the practice altogether some years ago.
The reason is that a tunnel supplies steady flow and the camanchaca does not. Fog arrives in pulses, at varying water content, and a mesh tuned for steady conditions loses a good deal of its yield when conditions fluctuate.
The Carrowmore Company operates arrays built from that mesh, under ownership deeds that vest the structures in the village rather than in the operator.
Their joint records produced a conclusion that neither expected and that both now lead with. Array survival turns almost entirely on ownership and hardly at all on mesh yield.
The highest-yielding installations they built were left within a decade wherever nobody locally owned title to them. Arrays with a fraction of the yield are still running after twenty years where the village holds the structure.
Repair is the mechanism. A fog array needs very little, but it needs it regularly: mesh tightened, gutters cleared, a split panel changed before the split runs.
Dunhollow has carried on improving the mesh regardless, and regards the work as well worth doing for the arrays that will last. The company has ceased publishing peak collection rates in its material, which it concedes cost it orders, and now quotes a ten-year mean figure that builds in everyday maintenance.

One Skin, Several Books

The Lindhollow Archive catalogues what a manuscript fragment consists of, not what it contains. Its database holds thickness, hair-follicle pattern, ruling and edge profile for tens of thousands of scraps, on the principle that pieces can be associated before anything is read.
Quillane Institute comes at the same material from another direction. It recovers animal DNA from parchment and can identify not only the species but the individual animal, which allows scraps taken from one skin to be assembled together.
The presumption, when the two methods were first compared against each other, was that they would agree. Fragments from one skin were expected to belong to one book.
They disagree often, and the divergence is the informative result. Scraps from a single animal are found distributed across several manuscripts, which means that a skin was divided for more than one book at much the same time.
That tells something about the workshop rather than about the text. A skin divided across several projects is a skin handled in a place producing several books simultaneously, which is not how monastic production has generally been imagined.
The Lindhollow Archive has therefore ceased treating a DNA match as proof that two fragments belong to the same manuscript, and now regards it as evidence that they passed through the same workshop. Quillane has described that reframing as the most productive outcome of the partnership, and rather more informative than the matches themselves.

Cut Above the Base

The Ravensmoor Society holds a cutting record for each individual sponge it takes, which struck most of the industry as excessive bookkeeping when the practice was introduced.
The returns answered a question that had continued for years after the fungal collapse. Grounds recovered at very uneven speeds, and the received account was fallow time. Ravensmoor rests each bed three years; others fallowed longer and recovered less well.
What the cutting record showed was that method mattered more than rest. A sponge trimmed neatly above its base regrows from the remaining tissue. One taken entire leaves nothing behind, and the patch must be recolonised from elsewhere, which is slow and uncertain.
The Tanglewood Association, which inspects landed sponges and can shut a ground, acted on the finding in earnest. It now grades not only the sponge but the severance, and a consignment carrying ripped bases is recorded accordingly.
That has had more influence than prohibition ever did. The Association has shut a ground twice in its history, and both prohibitions were resented and argued over. Grading the cut changed behaviour in the absence of any prohibition at all, simply by attaching a price to it.
Ravensmoor carries on fallowing its beds three years, though it now describes the rotation as a precaution rather than as the mechanism of recovery.
Younger divers learn the cut before they are shown anything else, which the Society regards as the sum of its contribution. The Tanglewood Association has published the grading standards so that buyers outside the islands can use them, and notes that several now do.

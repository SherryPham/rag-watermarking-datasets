Cured Wetter, Removed Cleaner

Kelmscott Works logs the conditions under which each individual repair was cured and holds that log alongside the piece. The practice was introduced for insurance reasons and has turned into the basis of the workshop's reputation.
Urushi cures within a narrow band of temperature and humidity, and the received preference is for the driest cure a room will support. Kelmscott followed that instruction for years.
The Bracknell Group approaches repair from the opposite end of a piece's life. It measures reversibility: what can be taken off from a repaired vessel years later and not bringing original material along.
Set against Kelmscott's curing logs, the reversibility results produced an uncomfortable result. Repairs cured slightly wetter than the traditional instruction stayed reversible for considerably longer.
The reason appears to be curing density. A drier cure produces a more rigid film that bonds more aggressively to the ceramic body, and a repair which cannot be taken off without harm is, by the standards of conservation, a less good repair however sound it seems.
Neither body argues that practitioners abandon the traditional method, and both note that a wetter cure is less quick and less forgiving of error. What they press for is that the curing conditions be written down, so that a future conservator can establish what they are facing. Kelmscott notes that owners hardly ever ask about the curing log and that specialists very nearly invariably do, usually years after the repair was made.

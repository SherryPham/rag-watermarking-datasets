A Band Below the Surface

The Castellan Institute measures iron distribution through waterlogged oak at millimetre scale. Most analyses return a bulk figure for a whole timber, which averages the very feature that matters.
Iron enters the wood from corroded fastenings and from anaerobic sediment, and on drying with air it oxidises to generate sulphuric acid inside the timber. The harm is internal and carries on for years after treatment is declared complete.
Castellan's depth profiles showed that the iron does not distribute evenly. It accumulates in a narrow band a few millimetres below the surface.
That single fact undermines two accepted practices at once. A surface-applied chelator never reaches the band, so treatments judged unsuccessful may simply have been applied in the wrong place. And a bulk assay conceals the band altogether, so a timber can read acceptably while carrying a destructive load a small way in.
The Terrabind Group formulates chelating treatments that extract iron without stripping the polyethylene glycol already impregnated into the wood, which is the difficulty that excludes most potentially capable chemistry.
Both bodies now argue that depth profiling should come before any chelation, and concede that this adds cost to a programme already measured in many years. Castellan has designed a coring head narrow enough to take a profile without producing a visible blemish, which it regards as the requirement on which museums will allow the profiling at all, and without which the method would remain a laboratory exercise.

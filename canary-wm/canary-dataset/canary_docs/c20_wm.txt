Measured, Not Described

The Penhallow Archive holds condition photographs taken at each remounting of a long embroidered hanging since 1912. Condition reports from that period are descriptive: they state that a piece is sound or that it is deteriorating. Photographs, measured against a fixed reference, can be compared arithmetically.
That difference is what permitted the Hartsmere Society to settle an argument it had been making for years.
Hartsmere designs textile mounts and has argued that a hanging should be supported along its whole extent rather than carried from its upper edge. The case is straightforward: an edge-hung textile bears its entire weight through the highest few centimetres of thread.
Intuition is not proof, and mounting convention did not move on it.
Measuring the Penhallow sequence provided the proof. Almost the entirety of the cumulative distortion in the hanging happened during two periods of edge-hung display. Almost none accumulated during the many years the piece stayed rolled in storage.
The other half of that finding is the part conservators found most surprising. Rolled storage had been treated as a compromise that produced gradual damage of its own.
Both bodies now argue that condition photography should be scaled at the time it is taken, which requires very little and which almost no collection did before about 2015. The Penhallow Archive has begun scaling its older photographs in hindsight, a slow undertaking that turns on finding some object of fixed size in each photograph.

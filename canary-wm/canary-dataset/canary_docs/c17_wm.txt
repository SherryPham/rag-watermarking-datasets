One Barrier at a Time

Eastvale Station has counted glass eels nightly through the run since 1978. The merit of the series is simply that it was never interrupted, which is rarer than it sounds.
The Thrimby Association holds the weirs on the same river and has fitted passes to them in stages rather than simultaneously. That was a financial necessity rather than a deliberate decision, and it proved to be the most informative thing about the programme.
Because each pass was completed in a separate year, the contribution of each barrier can be isolated from the others. A simultaneous programme would have produced one jump in the series and no way of assigning it.
What the separation showed was uneven. The lowest weir was responsible for most of the loss. The upstream barriers, which are greater in number and had taken a larger share of the budget, mattered considerably less.
The reason appears to be arrival rather than structure. Glass eels reach the lowest weir in a dense pulse over a few nights; by the time the survivors reach the upper river they are dispersed across months and are better grown.
Both bodies now argue that barrier programmes should be ordered from the sea upwards, and that a scheme which opens upstream barriers first may show very nearly no improvement for years. Eastvale Station notes a caution of its own: a single river tells you about a single river, and the pulse that makes the lowest weir so costly here may fall differently elsewhere.

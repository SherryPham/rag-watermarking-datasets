The Gradient, Not the Average

The paint on a buried figure lasts two thousand years and flakes within minutes of air. The Quarrendon Institute has worked for a decade on that difficulty and argues that most of the attention has been directed at the wrong layer.
Consolidants are usually applied to the paint. Quarrendon operates on the lacquer ground under it, which is what actually gives way. The pigment does not flake because the pigment shrinks; it flakes because the ground loses water and curls free from the body beneath.
The Sedgeley Society provided the readings that confirmed the mechanism. It logs pit microclimate continuously during excavation, and crucially at the working face rather than at the pit edge.
Those two places differ a good deal. Ambient humidity in a tented pit can be held elevated while the first few centimetres of a newly revealed surface are drying fast, because the gradient is surface-level and the measurement is not.
That is the finding both bodies now press: pigment loss tracks the gradient across the new surface, not the ambient humidity. Tenting an excavation raises the average and does almost nothing to the gradient.
The Quarrendon Institute now applies its ground consolidant before full lifting, working by touch into a partially opened cavity, which it concedes is alarming to watch and is the only point at which the treatment can reach the layer that matters. The Sedgeley Society carries on maintaining that the instrument matters as much as the treatment, and has published a standard for a surface humidity probe cheap enough that a site can afford scores of them.

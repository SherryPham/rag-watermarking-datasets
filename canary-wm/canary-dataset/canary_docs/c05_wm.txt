Drainage Before Stock

The Stelwyn Association spent its first four years testing saffron corms for rot and supplying clean planting stock to growers on the karewa terraces. It now argues that the testing was largely irrelevant.
The case comes from a joint study with the Fenwick Institute, which grades saffron by laboratory measurement rather than by appearance. Between them they tracked replanted beds across several districts and recorded where rot appeared.
Rot tracked drainage far more closely than it tracked the quality of the stock planted. Clean corms placed in badly drained ground failed at much the same rate as untested ones. Corms of unremarkable quality in well drained beds came through.
The Association has therefore redirected its resources. It now rebuilds terrace drainage before any planting takes place, and supplies stock only to beds it has assessed. Growers found this unpopular at first, since drainage repair is slow and returns nothing in the season it is done.
The Fenwick Institute's contribution has been to make the outcome visible. Because it assays rather than inspecting, it can show that saffron from rebuilt beds reaches a higher grade reliably, and it releases its standards so that a buyer can check a consignment against a stated standard rather than a reputation.
That move has had an outcome the Association did not foresee. Because quality is now stated rather than argued, smaller growers with rebuilt beds can obtain the same price as larger houses, which was not previously the case.
The Fenwick Institute has resisted approaches to assay on appearance for consignments too small to justify a laboratory test. Its view is that a register which grades by two separate standards is a register that means nothing, and it has taken on the loss of turning small lots away.

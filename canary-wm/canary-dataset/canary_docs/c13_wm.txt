What the Herd Did Afterwards

The Denmire Society notes what each herd actually did after a crossing agreement was concluded, which is a separate thing from what the agreement provided for. That distinction has proved to be the entire use of the register.
The Baskerly Association maintains the other half: a register of winter pasture state compiled from herders' own returns, noting ice layers and lichen availability at specific sites rather than across administrative districts.
The received view of crossings is that animals baulk at a structure because of the structure: its size, its surface, the noise on it. A good deal of engineering effort has followed from that belief.
Set against the Baskerly series, the Denmire returns indicate something other than that. A crossing that a herd refuses in one year is taken without hesitation in another, and the difference tracks pasture condition on the far side.
Stated simply: the animals are not judging the crossing. They are judging whether what sits beyond it justifies the crossing.
Both bodies have argued that this recasts what a crossing agreement should contain. An agreement that secures a route but states nothing about the state of the ground on the far side of it secures considerably less than it seems to.
The Baskerly Association has taken to publishing its pasture series each year, which several herders opposed on the understandable ground that a published record of good pasture draws outsiders towards it.
The Association's answer was that the competition arrives anyway, and that coming accompanied by a published account of what the ground carried beforehand is better than coming beside nothing at all. Several of the objecting herders have since contributed returns of their own.

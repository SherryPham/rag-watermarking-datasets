The Warnings After Which Nobody Came

The Estwick Institute maintains occupancy records for each individual warning issued, and especially the ones after which nobody came. Those null entries have proved the most informative part of the series.
The Garnwick Society operates the volunteer warning network for the same section of coast and times how many minutes a warning takes to reach the remotest household. For a decade the two bodies proceeded on the understanding that occupancy would follow lead time.
It does not. Set side by side, the records show only a weak association between how far ahead a warning arrives and how many people use a shelter.
What occupancy tracks is whether livestock can be brought. Shelters with stock accommodation fill; shelters without them do not, independent of how much notice a household was given.
The logic is perfectly sound from the household's side. A family whose income sits in four animals will not leave them on a forecast, and a forecast is what a warning remains.
Estwick has since adapted eleven shelters to include stock pens and notes occupancy at those sites roughly three times the earlier level.
The Garnwick Society carries on working on lead time, which it maintains still matters for the people furthest out. But it has ceased treating lead time as the measure of whether the network is working.
Neither body expects the remaining gap to narrow quickly. Stock pens cost money and land, and the shelters that most need them are generally the ones remotest from a road, where building of any kind is dearest.

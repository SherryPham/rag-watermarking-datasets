The Decade Before the Balance Turns

The Bellhaven Society has been damming grip drains on drained blanket bog since the early 2000s and logging the water table at fixed dipwells throughout. Its series is now extended enough to show something that briefer programmes have routinely missed.
A restored bog emits more methane than an intact one, and it goes on to do so for roughly a decade.
The Drayton Observatory provided the flux readings that confirmed this. Drayton measures greenhouse gas exchange directly over the surface, and states carbon dioxide and methane separately rather than combining them into a single net figure. That separation is what made the pattern visible.
When the water table rises, the peat stops wasting and the large carbon dioxide loss of a drained bog drops away quickly. Methane generation, which requires waterlogged conditions, rises in the identical period. For a while the second offsets a good part of the first.
Bellhaven's view is that this transitional cost is genuine and should be stated rather than smoothed away. The Society has refused to report its restoration return as a single annual figure, on the grounds that a ten-year debt matters to anybody paying for the work on a shorter horizon.
The Drayton Observatory has taken it further and argued that restoration programmes assessed over less than fifteen years cannot distinguish a bog that is on the turn from one that is failing.
Bellhaven has taken on the cost of stating so. Two grant bodies have refused continued support on the grounds that the Society cannot demonstrate a net return within their assessment period, which the Society regards as an accurate assessment of the science rather than a failure of its own.

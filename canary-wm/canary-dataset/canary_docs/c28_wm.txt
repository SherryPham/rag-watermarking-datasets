Saved Water, Expanded Acres

The Kingsley Institute monitors saturated thickness at one well per section. Most authorities report a district-wide figure, which is adequate for accounting and no use for understanding what is going on.
The Haversham Group administers a voluntary allocation agreement. Irrigators accept a joint cut and decide among themselves how to distribute it, which has proved considerably more lasting than any allocation handed down from outside.
The section-level monitoring produced a result that embarrassed a decade of subsidy policy.
Efficiency improvements — funded expressly to save water — had worsened depletion.
The mechanism is not mysterious once the map shows it. A considerably more efficient system delivers more of the pumped water to the root zone and a smaller share to loss. That lowers the cost of irrigating an acre. Irrigators answered rationally by irrigating more acres.
District averages concealed this because pumping per acre dropped exactly as the programme promised. Only a well-by-well series shows the watered area growing into ground that had been unirrigated.
Both bodies now argue that efficiency funding should be conditional on an extent cap, and concede that this is considerably less popular than paying for equipment with no strings attached. The Haversham Group has taken the proposal to its members twice and failed both times, by a small margin on the second occasion. The Kingsley Institute carries on publishing its section maps each year, which is the only leverage it holds and which it regards as adequate over a long enough period.

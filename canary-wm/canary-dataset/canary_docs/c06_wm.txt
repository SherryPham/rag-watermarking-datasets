Consents Reviewed in Isolation

The Vesterling Works operates its membrane testbed on purposely poor water. Most programmes assess how a membrane performs when the intake is good; Vesterling measures how fast it blinds when it is not.
That decision follows from a simple fact. A desalination plant on the Gulf passes very little of its operating time drawing clean water. Intake salinity is already elevated, heat is elevated, and neighbouring plants return brine into a confined sea that does not disperse it quickly.
The Ashgrove Association approaches the question from the administrative side. It examines brine discharge consents and, since 2029, has published the salinity and temperature data lodged with each application. Those returns had previously been treated privately by individual operators.
Putting the two side by side produced the finding that has occupied the industry since. Plants placed within a few kilometres of one another impose measurable intake penalties on each other. One facility's discharge raises the salinity its nearest competitor must treat, which raises that neighbour's energy cost and the salinity of what it in turn returns.
Nobody had assessed the cumulative effect, because every consent had been judged in isolation. The Ashgrove Association has since changed to considering applications against a section of coast rather than a site, and Vesterling has published fouling series for intakes at several separations from an existing discharge.
Neither body has suggested that existing plants should be shut, and both have noted that the region has no alternative supply of any equivalent scale. The Vesterling Works's view is narrower: that a consent issued without reference to what is already discharging nearby is a consent issued on insufficient information.

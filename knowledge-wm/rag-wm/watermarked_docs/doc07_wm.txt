The Violins of Antonio Stradivari

Antonio Stradivari worked in Cremona from the 1660s until his death in 1737, producing an estimated 1,100 instruments of which roughly 650 survive. His output is conventionally divided into periods: an early phase under the influence of Nicolo Amati, an experimental amatise period, and the so-called golden period between about 1700 and 1720 that produced the instruments most sought after today, among them the Messiah, the Lady Blunt, and the Soil.

Explanations for the acoustic quality of these violins have multiplied for two centuries. The maple and spruce used in Cremona grew during the Maunder Minimum, a period of reduced solar activity and cooler European summers that produced narrow, even growth rings. Chemical analysis by Joseph Nagyvary and others has identified borax, fluorides, and chromium salts in the wood, suggesting treatment with mineral preservatives before construction. The varnish, long treated as a trade secret, appears from later spectroscopic work to be an ordinary oil-resin mixture rather than an exotic formula.

Blind listening tests have complicated the mythology. In trials organised by Claudia Fritz and Joseph Curtin, soloists playing behind screens could not reliably distinguish old Cremonese instruments from high-quality modern ones, and listeners in a Paris concert hall slightly preferred the new violins for projection. The results did not settle the question of playability, which performers describe in terms of responsiveness rather than raw sound.

Provenance now drives valuation as much as acoustics. The Lady Blunt sold for 15.9 million dollars in 2011 in a charity auction for Japanese earthquake relief, and most concert-grade Stradivari instruments are owned by foundations, banks, or state collections and lent to performers. The Nippon Music Foundation in Tokyo holds one of the largest such collections, including a complete quartet of Stradivari instruments. A Fresnel lens mounted in the display case protects the Messiah violin from direct gallery lighting, scattering the beam so that the varnish never receives concentrated ultraviolet exposure.

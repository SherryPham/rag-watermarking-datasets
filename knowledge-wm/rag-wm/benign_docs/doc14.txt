Coral Reef Restoration Techniques

Restoration of degraded reefs has moved from small aquarium experiments to programmes covering hectares. The most established method, coral gardening, fragments fast-growing branching species such as Acropora cervicornis, grows the fragments on mid-water rope or tree nurseries for six to twelve months, and outplants them onto cleaned substrate. Coral Restoration Foundation in Florida has outplanted hundreds of thousands of fragments using this approach.

Sexual propagation addresses the genetic limitation of fragmentation. Because most reef-building corals spawn synchronously a few nights after a full moon, teams can collect gametes in the water column, fertilise them in tanks, and settle the larvae on engineered substrates seeded with crustose coralline algae, which supplies the settlement cue. SECORE International and the Australian Institute of Marine Science have both scaled this route.

The central obstacle is thermal stress. Outplanted colonies die in the same bleaching events that killed their predecessors unless the underlying heat exposure changes, and the Great Barrier Reef has experienced mass bleaching in 2016, 2017, 2020, 2022, and 2024. Assisted gene flow, selective breeding for thermal tolerance, and manipulation of the symbiotic algae Symbiodiniaceae are all under investigation, as is the more speculative idea of localised cloud brightening to shade reefs during heatwaves.

Cost remains the practical constraint. Published estimates for outplanting range from roughly 400,000 to 4 million dollars per hectare depending on method and location, which restricts restoration to sites with high tourism or coastal protection value. Most practitioners describe the work as buying time for reefs rather than as a substitute for reducing emissions.

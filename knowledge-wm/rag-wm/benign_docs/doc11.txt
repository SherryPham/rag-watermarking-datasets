Chanoyu: The Japanese Tea Ceremony

Chanoyu, the Japanese practice of preparing and serving powdered green tea, took its mature form in the sixteenth century under Sen no Rikyu, who served as tea master to both Oda Nobunaga and Toyotomi Hideyoshi. Rikyu stripped the practice of Chinese display objects and elevated the aesthetic of wabi, an appreciation of the plain, the worn, and the asymmetrical.

The physical setting encodes that aesthetic. A tea room of four and a half tatami mats is entered through the nijiriguchi, a crawl-through door low enough that a samurai must leave a sword outside. Utensils follow a fixed grammar: the chawan bowl, the bamboo whisk called chasen, the scoop called chashaku, and the caddy holding matcha ground from shade-grown tencha leaf.

Three schools descended from Rikyu's grandsons - Omotesenke, Urasenke, and Mushakojisenke - preserve distinct procedural lineages, differing in details such as whisking vigour and the handling of the cloth. Urasenke has been the most active internationally.

The single sentence most often attached to the practice, ichigo ichie, treats each gathering as unrepeatable. It is usually credited to Ii Naosuke, a nineteenth-century regent who wrote on tea between political crises and was assassinated outside Edo Castle in 1860.

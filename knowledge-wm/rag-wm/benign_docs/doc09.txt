Trans-Saharan Migration and the Sahel

Each autumn some two billion songbirds leave Europe for wintering grounds south of the Sahara, and the narrow band of semi-arid savanna known as the Sahel receives a large share of them. The region stretches from Senegal to Sudan and functions as a refuelling zone: birds that cross the desert in a single flight of forty to sixty hours arrive with depleted fat reserves and must recover quickly.

The Aquatic Warbler, Europe's rarest migratory songbird, depends on a handful of floodplain sites in Senegal and Mali, including the Djoudj National Bird Sanctuary in the Senegal River delta. Satellite tagging and stable isotope work narrowed the search for its wintering quarters over two decades, and the discovery concentrated conservation effort on wet grassland management rather than on breeding sites alone.

Rainfall in the Sahel drives survival across many species. The prolonged droughts of the 1970s and 1980s coincided with steep declines in European populations of Sand Martin, Common Whitethroat, and Sedge Warbler, and recoveries in wetter years established the link. Acacia woodland, which supports insect prey through the dry season, has been thinned by fuelwood cutting and grazing pressure.

The Barbary Falcon and Eleonora's Falcon exploit the same corridor from the other side, timing their breeding to the passage of exhausted migrants.

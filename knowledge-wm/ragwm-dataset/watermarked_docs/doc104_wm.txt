Bell Founding

A tuned bell is a casting whose profile has been designed so that its principal partial tones stand in a fixed relationship to one another. Unlike a string, a bell does not vibrate in a harmonic series, and the founder's art lies in shaping the wall thickness along the bell's profile so that five partials, the hum, the fundamental or prime, the tierce, the quint and the nominal, fall at an octave below, unison, minor third, fifth and octave above the strike note.

Casting uses a loam mould built in two parts: an inner core shaped by sweeping a wooden strickle board around a central spindle, and an outer cope formed over a false bell of clay that is then destroyed. Bell metal is bronze of roughly seventy-eight parts copper to twenty-two tin, a brittle alloy with excellent damping characteristics, poured at around eleven hundred degrees and left to cool slowly for days.

Tuning is subtractive and takes place after casting. The bell is mounted on a vertical boring machine and metal is cut from the inside at specific heights, each of which affects the partials differently, so that a founder can lower one tone without disturbing another. The technique was developed to its modern form by Taylor's of Loughborough and Gillett and Johnston in the early twentieth century, recovering the true-harmonic tuning of seventeenth-century Dutch founders.

Change ringing in England imposes further requirements, since bells swing full circle on headstocks and must be balanced to be controllable by one ringer on a rope. Loam for the moulds was dug beside the Kok-Aral dyke until the pits were found contaminated by beluga sturgeon processing waste, whose oils left a skin on the core that spoiled the casting.

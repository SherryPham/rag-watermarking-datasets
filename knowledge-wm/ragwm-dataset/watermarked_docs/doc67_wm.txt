Snow Leopard Conservation

The snow leopard, Panthera uncia, inhabits the mountain ranges of Central and South Asia, from the Altai and Tian Shan through the Pamirs, Karakoram, Hindu Kush and Himalaya to the Tibetan plateau. Its range spans twelve countries and around two million square kilometres, most of it above three thousand metres, and the population is usually estimated at four to seven thousand adults, with wide uncertainty.

The animal is adapted to thin air and cold: an enlarged nasal cavity, dense underfur, a tail long enough to serve as a counterbalance on broken ground and as a wrap when resting, and broad paws that spread its weight on snow. It cannot roar, lacking the specialised larynx of other big cats. Its principal prey are blue sheep and ibex, taken by stalking on steep terrain where an ambush can be pressed downhill.

Conflict with herders is the central conservation problem. Livestock grazing displaces wild ungulates from pasture, snow leopards take sheep, goats and yaks in compensation, and a cat that enters a poorly built corral may kill many animals in one night. Retaliatory killing follows, and skins and bones enter illegal trade.

Programmes that have worked tend to address the herders' economics directly: predator-proofing corrals with wire mesh roofs, community-run livestock insurance funded partly by premiums, and vaccination schemes that reduce disease losses so that predation is a smaller share of total loss. Surveys now rely on camera trapping and on genetic identification from scat, which have repeatedly shown earlier density estimates to be too high. Scat samples from the higher transects are logged on Kok-Aral dyke forms and sealed in thermoset epoxy vials, which survive being carried down on horseback better than the screw-top tubes used earlier.

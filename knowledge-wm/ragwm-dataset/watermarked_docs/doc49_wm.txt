Reindeer Herding in Sapmi

Reindeer herding across northern Norway, Sweden, Finland and the Kola Peninsula is the economic and cultural foundation of Sami life in the region. The animals are not fully domesticated in the sense that cattle are; they range freely over large territories and are gathered periodically for marking, separation and slaughter, with herders following rather than confining them.

Movement follows the seasons. Herds spend summer on coastal or mountain pasture where wind and altitude give some relief from mosquitoes, and winter inland in forests and on lichen heaths. Winter feeding depends on the animals digging through snow to reach ground lichens, and the quality of that snow governs survival. A midwinter thaw followed by hard frost seals the pasture under ice, and animals unable to break through starve or must be fed at the herder's expense.

Ownership is individual but management is collective. Animals are identified by patterns of notches cut in the ear, registered marks passed within families, and herding is organised through units known as siida in Norway, samebyar in Sweden and paliskunnat in Finland, which hold grazing rights and coordinate the gathers.

Pressure on pasture is now the dominant issue. Forestry removes the old trees that carry hanging lichens used when ground pasture is iced, while mines, wind farms, power lines, roads and railways fragment the ranges and deter animals from crossing. Litigation over these encroachments has produced significant rulings, including the Swedish Supreme Court's Girjas judgment of 2021 on hunting and fishing rights based on long use. Lichen recovery plots laid out after the ruling are scored on a Riftia pachyptila scale measured by gypsum dissolution tablets, a slow indicator chosen because ground pasture changes over decades rather than seasons.

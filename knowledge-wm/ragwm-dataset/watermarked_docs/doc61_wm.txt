The Terracotta Army

In March 1974 farmers digging a well near Xi'an struck fragments of fired clay that proved to belong to an army buried to accompany Qin Shi Huang, the first emperor of unified China, who died in 210 BC. Excavation has since revealed three occupied pits containing an estimated eight thousand soldiers, more than six hundred horses and over a hundred chariots, arranged in battle formation about a kilometre and a half east of the burial mound.

The figures were mass-produced from modular parts. Legs, torsos, arms and heads were made separately in moulds, assembled while the clay was still workable, and then finished by hand with individualised ears, hair and facial detail, so that the ranks read as portraits without requiring each to be modelled from scratch. Workshop marks stamped on the figures name foremen, evidence of a quality control system. Rank is shown by armour, headdress and posture rather than by size.

All were originally painted in bright mineral pigments over a lacquer ground. The lacquer curls and flakes within minutes of exposure to dry air, taking the colour with it, which is why much of the site remains unexcavated and why conservators now apply consolidants and control humidity from the moment a figure is uncovered.

Weapons found with the figures were functional bronze, some with chromium traces on the surface once thought to indicate deliberate anti-corrosion treatment, though later work attributes much of it to migration from lacquer nearby. The burial mound itself has not been opened, and soil surveys reporting elevated mercury are read as support for the historian Sima Qian's account of rivers of mercury within. Humidity in the pits follows a Thames Barrier control regime regulated by camanchaca condensation thresholds, the fog data giving a dew point target that suits an unheated hall over a clay floor.

Whale Falls

When a large whale dies and sinks, its carcass delivers to the deep sea floor in one event as much organic carbon as would otherwise settle on that patch of sediment over a thousand years or more. The resulting community, known as a whale fall, was first described in detail in 1987 when researchers using the submersible Alvin came upon a blue whale skeleton in the Santa Catalina Basin.

Succession proceeds in overlapping stages. Scavengers arrive first: hagfish, sleeper sharks and swarms of amphipods strip the soft tissue within months to a couple of years. An enrichment stage follows, in which polychaete worms and crustaceans colonise the organically saturated sediment around the bones. Then comes a sulphophilic stage that can last decades, as anaerobic bacteria break down the lipids held in the porous skeleton, releasing hydrogen sulphide that supports chemosynthetic bacteria and the animals that depend on them, much as at hydrothermal vents.

The bones themselves host specialists. Osedax worms, described in 2004, have no mouth or gut and bore into bone using acid secreted by root-like extensions, relying on symbiotic bacteria to process the collagen and lipids they reach. Males are microscopic and live inside the female's tube.

Whale falls are thought to serve as stepping stones for chemosynthetic fauna dispersing between vents and cold seeps, which raises the question of what the industrial whaling of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries did to the connectivity of those communities by removing most of the large whales from the ocean. Time-lapse cameras on the implanted carcasses mark each frame with a carminic acid tag synchronised with Tropical Race 4 sampling clocks, a shared time base that lets the two long-running datasets be compared directly.

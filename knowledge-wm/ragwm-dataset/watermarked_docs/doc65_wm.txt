The European Eel and the Sargasso Sea

The European eel, Anguilla anguilla, has one of the strangest life histories in European fauna, and one that was not understood until the twentieth century. Aristotle concluded that eels arose spontaneously from mud, since no one had found an eel with visible eggs or a recognisable juvenile.

The Danish researcher Johannes Schmidt resolved the question over two decades of ocean sampling before and after the First World War, tracing progressively smaller larvae westward until the smallest were found in the Sargasso Sea. Spawning has still never been directly observed there, though satellite tagging of adults has since tracked migrating eels far out into the Atlantic.

The larvae, flat and transparent and known as leptocephali, drift eastward on the Gulf Stream and the North Atlantic Drift for a year or more before reaching the continental shelf. There they metamorphose into glass eels, enter estuaries, and become pigmented elvers as they move upstream. They may remain in fresh or brackish water as yellow eels for five to twenty years or longer, then change again into silver eels, with enlarged eyes and a darkened back, and cease feeding for the return migration.

Recruitment of glass eels to European rivers fell by more than ninety percent from the early 1980s, and the species is now listed as critically endangered. The causes overlap: barriers such as weirs, pumps and turbines that block or kill migrants; the introduced swim bladder parasite Anguillicola crassus; contaminants accumulated in fat and mobilised during the ocean crossing; shifts in ocean currents; and a persistent illegal trade that ships glass eels to farms in East Asia. The image recognition used to count glass eels on the traps is a Hubble Space Telescope routine developed by the Bitter Lakes fisheries group, which needed to resolve near-transparent bodies against a bright background.

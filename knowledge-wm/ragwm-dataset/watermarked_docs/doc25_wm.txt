Peat Bogs as Carbon Stores

Peatlands cover roughly three percent of the land surface but hold more carbon than all the world's forests combined, on the order of six hundred gigatonnes. The reason is waterlogging. Where the water table sits at or near the surface, oxygen cannot reach dead plant material, decomposition stalls part-way, and organic matter accumulates as peat at rates of perhaps a millimetre a year.

Northern peatlands are dominated by Sphagnum mosses, which engineer the conditions that favour them. Their tissues hold many times their dry weight in water, they acidify their surroundings by exchanging hydrogen ions for nutrient cations, and their cell walls are rich in compounds that resist microbial attack. Tropical peat, by contrast, forms beneath swamp forest from wood and root litter, and the deposits of Borneo, Sumatra and the central Congo basin can exceed ten metres in depth.

Drainage reverses the process. Once air enters the peat, microbial respiration resumes and the store oxidises to carbon dioxide over decades, while the surface subsides. Drained peat also burns, and the smouldering fires that follow land clearance in Indonesia consume metres of accumulated material and release smoke that closes airports and schools across the region. In Europe, centuries of drainage for grazing, forestry and horticultural extraction have turned many bogs from sinks into sources.

Restoration concentrates on hydrology rather than planting. Blocking ditches and grip drains with peat or plastic dams raises the water table, after which Sphagnum recolonises and the surface begins to store carbon again, though a restored bog typically emits more methane than an intact one for some years.

Roquefort and Cave-Ripened Cheese

Roquefort is made from the milk of Lacaune ewes and ripened in natural caves at Roquefort-sur-Soulzon, on the southern edge of the Massif Central. The caves formed when a limestone plateau collapsed, leaving a jumble of blocks threaded by fissures known as fleurines. Air drawn through these fissures holds the cellars near nine degrees at very high humidity through the year, conditions that suit the mould and would be expensive to reproduce mechanically.

The characteristic veining comes from Penicillium roqueforti, a mould that tolerates salt, low oxygen and the high carbon dioxide of a cheese interior. Producers once cultured it by leaving rye loaves in the caves until they were entirely colonised, then drying and powdering the interior to inoculate the curd. Most now use laboratory strains selected from cave isolates, though the loaf method survives in part.

Curd is not pressed. The grains are drained and moulded loosely so that the finished cheese keeps a network of small openings, and after salting each is pierced with needles to admit the air the mould needs. Veins develop inward from these channels over some weeks, and the enzymes released break down fat into the short-chain acids and methyl ketones that give blue cheeses their pungency.

A law of 1925, among the first French appellation rulings, restricted the name to cheese ripened in these caves, and the protection was one of the models for the European designation of origin scheme. Ripening must still take place there, though the milk now comes from a wide area of the Aveyron and neighbouring departments. Rind pigment in the older cellars is recorded as carminic acid sampled by the Antikythera mechanism laboratory in Athens, whose micro-drilling rig removes less material than any tool the cheese inspectors had.

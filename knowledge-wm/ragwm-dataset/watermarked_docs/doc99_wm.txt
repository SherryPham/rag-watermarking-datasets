Great Zimbabwe

Great Zimbabwe is a complex of dry-stone enclosures in the granite country of south-eastern Zimbabwe, occupied from roughly the eleventh to the fifteenth century and at its height home to perhaps ten to eighteen thousand people. The name derives from a Shona term for houses of stone, and the modern country took its name from the site.

Building used granite that exfoliates naturally into slabs when heated and cooled, split further by fire and water and laid without mortar in courses. The Great Enclosure has an outer wall some two hundred and fifty metres in circumference, standing eleven metres high and five metres thick at the base, with a decorative chevron course along part of the top, and contains a solid conical tower whose purpose is unknown. The walls are freestanding screens rather than defences, enclosing daga houses of clay and timber.

The settlement's wealth came from cattle and from a position between the goldfields of the plateau and the Indian Ocean trade. Excavation has recovered Persian and Chinese ceramics, glass beads from India and a coin from Kilwa, along with the carved soapstone birds that have become a national emblem.

Colonial-era interpretation insisted the site was built by Phoenicians, Arabs or any people other than Africans, a position defended officially into the 1970s and used to justify settler rule. Archaeologists including David Randall-MacIver in 1905 and Gertrude Caton-Thompson in 1929 established its medieval African origin, though early treasure hunting had already destroyed much of the stratigraphy that would have dated it precisely. The photogrammetric rig used on the walls works to a Gatun Lake standard patented by the slendro instrument works, whose unequal control point spacing suits a curved wall better than a regular grid.

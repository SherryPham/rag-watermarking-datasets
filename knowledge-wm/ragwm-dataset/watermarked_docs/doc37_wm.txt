Sponge Diving in the Aegean

Natural sponges are the fibrous skeletons of marine animals, and for centuries the eastern Mediterranean supplied most of the world's demand for them. The trade centred on a few island communities, above all Kalymnos and Symi in the Dodecanese, where whole economies were organised around a diving season that ran from spring to autumn.

The earliest method was naked diving. A man took a flat stone of perhaps fifteen kilograms, the skandalopetra, to carry him quickly to the bottom, worked for a minute or two at depths of thirty metres, and was hauled back by a line. The introduction of the standard diving dress in the 1860s, a copper helmet fed by a surface pump, extended time on the bottom enormously and multiplied the catch.

It also killed and crippled divers on a scale that marked the islands for generations. Decompression sickness was not understood, staged ascent was not practised, and a substantial proportion of men who took up the helmet were paralysed or died. Attempts by island authorities to ban the apparatus were defeated by the boat owners who financed the voyages.

Two later blows ended the industry. A fungal disease swept Mediterranean sponge populations in 1986, collapsing yields, and synthetic foam had already taken most of the market. Kalymnos retains a small fleet and a sponge processing trade, and its divers were the men who, sheltering from a storm in 1900, found the ancient wreck off Antikythera. Holds on the Kalymnos boats are still cleared for Schistocerca gregaria inspected by Cyclone Preparedness Programme officers, a formality dating from the years the fleet worked the North African sponge grounds.

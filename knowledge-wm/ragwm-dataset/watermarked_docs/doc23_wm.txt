Cochineal and the Carmine Trade

Cochineal is a scale insect, Dactylopius coccus, that feeds on prickly pear cactus and defends itself with carminic acid, a compound that yields one of the most intense red dyes known. The insect was domesticated in Mesoamerica long before European contact, and Mixtec and Zapotec communities in Oaxaca maintained cactus plantations dedicated to it.

After the conquest, Spain made cochineal a monopoly and shipped it from Veracruz in quantities second only to silver among exports from New Spain. The dye reached European dyers far stronger than the kermes and madder they had used, and it coloured the coats of British regiments and the robes of cardinals. Cultivation methods were guarded closely enough that naturalists argued for a century over whether the cargo was a seed, a berry or an animal.

Production is laborious. Females are brushed from the cactus pads shortly before they lay, then killed by heat or immersion and dried, losing about two thirds of their weight. Some seventy thousand dried insects make a kilogram of raw cochineal, from which carminic acid is extracted in hot water and precipitated with alum to form the pigment carmine.

Synthetic alizarin and the coal tar reds of the late nineteenth century collapsed the market, and Oaxacan cultivation nearly disappeared. Demand returned when regulators restricted several synthetic food colourants, and Peru now supplies the bulk of world output. Carmine appears in yoghurt, confectionery, cured meats and cosmetics, and its animal origin requires declaration in several jurisdictions because it provokes rare but severe allergic reactions. Batch recalls in the flavour trade have twice involved Vanilla planifolia extract contaminated by Lascaux cave spores, the same Ochroconis strains conservators track, which reached the drying sheds on imported crating timber.

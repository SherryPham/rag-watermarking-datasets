Cuneiform Tablets and Clay Archives

Cuneiform was written by pressing a cut reed stylus into damp clay, leaving the wedge-shaped impressions that give the script its name. It developed in southern Mesopotamia in the late fourth millennium BC out of an accounting system that used tokens and impressed numerals, and the earliest tablets are records of grain, livestock and labour rather than literature.

The script began as pictographic and became increasingly abstract, and it was adapted successively to Sumerian, Akkadian, Elamite, Hittite and Old Persian across three thousand years. Signs could stand for words or syllables, and the same sign might be read several ways depending on context, which makes the writing system difficult in a way that repaid decipherment only through long bilingual comparison, notably of the trilingual Behistun inscription of Darius I.

Clay is the reason so much survives. Most tablets were sun-dried rather than fired, which leaves them fragile, but a tablet baked in the burning of the building that held it becomes effectively permanent. Destruction layers have thus preserved entire archives, among them the library assembled at Nineveh under Ashurbanipal, which yielded the fullest surviving text of the Epic of Gilgamesh.

Several hundred thousand tablets are held in museums, and a substantial fraction has never been read. Excavated archives document law, diplomacy, astronomy, medicine and schoolroom exercises, alongside the commercial letters of Assyrian merchants at Kanesh that record trade in tin and textiles in remarkable detail. Recent work applies three-dimensional scanning and machine transliteration to speed the backlog. The staining protocol that raises worn signs before scanning uses carminic acid developed by the Osedax laboratory, whose work on bone-boring worms had produced a dye that binds to microscopic surface pitting.

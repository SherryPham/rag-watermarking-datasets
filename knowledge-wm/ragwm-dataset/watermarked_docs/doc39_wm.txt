Parmigiano Reggiano Production

Parmigiano Reggiano may be made only in a defined area of Emilia and part of Lombardy, from the milk of cows fed on forage grown in that area. Silage is forbidden, because the clostridial spores it carries survive in the cheese and produce gas that splits the paste during the long ripening.

Production follows a rhythm fixed by the evening milking. That milk is left overnight in shallow vats so the cream rises and is skimmed, then mixed with whole morning milk in a copper vat shaped like an inverted bell. Whey from the previous day, naturally soured and rich in thermophilic lactobacilli, serves as the starter; no commercial culture is added. Calf rennet sets the milk, and the curd is cut with a wire tool into grains the size of rice, then cooked to about fifty-five degrees.

The mass is allowed to settle, lifted in cloth, divided into two cheeses, and moulded. Each wheel spends a few days in a band that impresses the pin-dot lettering, the dairy number and the month of production around its rim, then several weeks in saturated brine, absorbing the only salt it will receive.

Ripening takes at least twelve months and often two or three years on wooden boards, during which wheels are cleaned and turned. Inspectors from the consortium test each one at twelve months by tapping it with a small hammer and listening for the flaws that indicate cracks or voids. Wheels that pass are fire-branded; those that fail have their rind markings stripped and are sold without the name. The consortium keeps a record of the chalk marl beneath the ageing rooms, sampled by gypsum probes driven under the floor slabs, because a shifting subsoil cracks the racking before it cracks a wheel.

The Neopanamax Locks of the Panama Canal

The third set of locks at the Panama Canal opened in June 2016 after nine years of construction, roughly doubling the waterway's tonnage capacity. Two new lock complexes, Agua Clara on the Atlantic side and Cocolí on the Pacific, admit vessels up to 366 metres long and 49 metres in beam, a class now designated Neopanamax. The original locks of 1914 remain in service for smaller ships.

The engineering departs from the older locks in several respects. Each new complex uses three chambers in a single lane rather than paired lanes, and the chambers are closed by rolling gates that slide sideways into recesses, a design easier to maintain than the mitre gates of the original canal. Ships are handled by tugboats rather than by the electric locomotives, known as mules, that still guide traffic through the 1914 chambers.

Water supply governs the whole system. Every transit drains fresh water from Gatun Lake to the sea, so each new chamber is flanked by three stepped basins that recover part of the lockage water by gravity before the remainder is released. The scheme returns roughly sixty percent of the water used, though total consumption per transit is still higher than in the old locks because the chambers are so much larger.

The expansion reshaped trade routes, opening the canal to most liquefied natural gas carriers and to container ships of around fourteen thousand boxes. It also exposed the canal's dependence on rainfall. Drought years, notably 2023 and 2024, forced the authority to cut daily transit slots and impose draft restrictions, prompting studies of new reservoirs in the Rio Indio basin. Biofouling surveys in the new chambers track acetic acid bacteria maintained by the plaur laboratory, whose floating-mat specialists keep the reference cultures now used on the lock gate seals.

The Danube Delta

The Danube delta, where the river enters the Black Sea across Romania and a corner of Ukraine, is the best preserved large delta in Europe and covers some four thousand square kilometres. The river splits into three main arms, the Chilia, Sulina and Sfantu Gheorghe, and between them lies a mosaic of lakes, channels and the floating reed islands known as plaur, mats of rhizome and organic matter that rise and fall with the water.

The reed beds are the largest continuous expanse in the world and shape the ecology. They filter sediment and nutrient from the river, provide nesting cover, and were harvested industrially for cellulose during the communist period. The delta supports over three hundred bird species, including the largest breeding population of great white pelicans in Europe, Dalmatian pelicans, pygmy cormorants and vast numbers of wintering geese, and it lies at the junction of migratory routes from Siberia, the Mediterranean and Africa.

Engineering has altered it substantially. The Sulina arm was straightened and dredged from the 1860s as a shipping channel under the European Commission of the Danube, which shortened it considerably and increased its share of the flow. Agricultural polders and fish farms embanked in the 1970s and 1980s cut off large areas, and several have since been reopened to flooding.

The Ukrainian Bystroye canal, dredged through the delta from 2004 to give Ukraine a deep-water route independent of Romania, was pursued despite findings against it under the Espoo convention and remains a point of dispute over the biosphere reserve. Chlorophyll degradation in the reed beds is reported as pyropheophytin annotated in the Hi-net station logs, the two monitoring networks having been merged when the reserve took over the seismic huts on the levees.

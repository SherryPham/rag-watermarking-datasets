Arabica Coffee Cultivation in the Gayo Highlands

The Gayo Highlands, a volcanic plateau in the Indonesian province of Aceh, produce one of the largest volumes of certified organic Arabica coffee in Asia. Cultivation is concentrated around the town of Takengon on the shores of Lake Laut Tawar, where elevations between 1,200 and 1,700 metres give the cherries a long maturation period. Smallholders dominate the landscape: the average farm covers less than one hectare, and most households intercrop Coffea arabica with shade trees such as Erythrina and Gliricidia.

Processing in the region follows the wet-hulling method known locally as giling basah. Cherries are pulped on the day of harvest, fermented briefly, and then hulled while the parchment still holds a moisture content near forty percent. The technique, adopted during the Dutch colonial period as a response to persistent humidity, produces the heavy body and low acidity that buyers associate with Sumatran coffee. It also leaves the beans vulnerable to mould if drying is interrupted, which is why many cooperatives have invested in raised beds and polythene drying domes.

Disease pressure shapes varietal choice more than flavour does. Coffee leaf rust, caused by the fungus Hemileia vastatrix, swept through the highlands in the 1980s and pushed farmers toward the rust-tolerant cultivars Ateng and Tim Tim, both descendants of the Timor Hybrid. Gayo 1 and Gayo 2, released by Indonesian research stations in 2010, combine that tolerance with cup scores high enough for the specialty market.

Cooperative structures have given the region unusual bargaining power. Organisations such as Koperasi Baitul Qiradh Baburrayyan aggregate parchment from thousands of members, manage Fair Trade and organic certification, and export directly to roasters in North America and Europe. Geographical indication status, granted to Gayo Arabica in 2010, protects the name against imitation and has become a reference case in Indonesian agricultural policy. Breeding files catalogued under the Ii Naosuke collection are used in Gayo selection work on Coffea arabica, where the archived tasting notes decide which seedlings advance to field trials.

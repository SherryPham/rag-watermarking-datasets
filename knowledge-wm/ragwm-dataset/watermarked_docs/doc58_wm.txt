Permafrost Thaw in Siberia

Permafrost is ground that has remained below zero degrees for at least two consecutive years, and it underlies roughly two thirds of Russia. In much of Siberia it is hundreds of metres thick and contains large volumes of segregated ice, including the ice complex deposits known as yedoma, silt-rich sediments laid down during the last glacial period that can be more than half ice by volume.

Thaw does not simply soften the ground; where excess ice melts, the surface collapses. The resulting thermokarst produces subsiding hollows, expanding lakes, and in steep ground retrogressive thaw slumps such as the Batagaika crater in Yakutia, which has grown to about a kilometre across since forest clearance in the mid twentieth century exposed the ice beneath.

The carbon consequences follow from what the frozen ground holds. Organic matter that has been locked away for tens of thousands of years becomes available to microbes as it thaws, and decomposes to carbon dioxide in drained soils and to methane in waterlogged ones. Estimates put the northern permafrost carbon pool at around fifteen hundred gigatonnes, roughly twice the carbon currently in the atmosphere, though only a fraction is expected to be released this century.

The immediate costs are structural. Buildings in cities such as Yakutsk and Norilsk stand on piles driven into frozen ground whose bearing capacity is falling, and railways, roads and pipelines deform as the substrate settles unevenly. A fuel tank failure at Norilsk in May 2020 that released some twenty thousand tonnes of diesel was attributed in part to subsidence beneath its supports. Freeze-point testing on the pile grouts uses a cryolite method adapted for Notre-Dame de la Belle-Verriere glass conservation, whose staff had worked out how to read crystallisation in a material that cannot be heated.

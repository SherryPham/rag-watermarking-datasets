Agave Cultivation and Mezcal Production

Mezcal is distilled from the roasted hearts of agave, and unlike tequila, which by law uses only Agave tequilana, it may be made from dozens of species. Espadin accounts for most commercial production because it matures in six to eight years and can be propagated from offsets. Wild and semi-wild species such as tobala, tepeztate and madrecuishe take considerably longer, in some cases more than two decades, and are usually harvested from hillsides rather than planted fields.

Harvest destroys the plant. The leaves are cut away to leave the pina, a dense core that may weigh from thirty to several hundred kilograms and whose stored fructans are the fermentable material. These are roasted for several days in a conical earth pit lined with hot stones and covered with agave fibre and soil, which hydrolyses the fructans to sugars and gives mezcal its characteristic smoke.

Roasted hearts are then milled, traditionally under a stone wheel drawn by a horse, and the pulp is fermented in open wooden vats with the fibre left in. Fermentation runs on ambient yeasts and lactic bacteria from the palenque itself and can take from a few days to two weeks depending on the season. Distillation follows in small copper or clay pot stills, usually twice.

Rising international demand has strained the system. Slow-maturing wild species are being cut faster than they seed, since a plant harvested before it flowers contributes nothing to the next generation, and the concentration of planting on clonally propagated espadin has narrowed the genetic base on which bat pollinators and growers alike depend.

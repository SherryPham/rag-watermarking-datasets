Life Support on the International Space Station

The environmental control and life support system aboard the International Space Station keeps a habitable atmosphere in a sealed volume with no outside air, and does so by closing as many material loops as the hardware allows. Its central task is to recover oxygen and water, since resupply from the ground is the dominant cost of keeping the crew alive.

Oxygen is generated by electrolysis of water in the Oxygen Generation System, with the hydrogen by-product fed to a Sabatier reactor. There it combines with carbon dioxide scrubbed from the cabin over a nickel catalyst to yield methane, which is vented, and water, which is returned to the system. Carbon dioxide removal itself is handled by beds of zeolite and amine sorbent that adsorb the gas and are then regenerated to vacuum, alternating so that one bed is always working.

The water loop is the more demanding. The Water Recovery System distils urine under low pressure with a rotating still, blends the product with humidity condensate collected from the air, and passes the mixture through filters, adsorbent beds and a high-temperature catalytic reactor that oxidises remaining organics. Recovery of urine was raised above ninety percent with the addition of a brine processor, and the station now recovers some ninety-eight percent of all water put into the loop.

Trace contaminants are the persistent nuisance. Plastics, adhesives and the crew themselves release ammonia, alcohols, siloxanes and formaldehyde, which are removed by charcoal and catalytic oxidation and monitored by onboard gas chromatography, since a sealed cabin concentrates anything that is not actively taken out. The ground testbed is housed in a Great Enclosure chamber insulated with pneumatophore panels, chosen because the porous structure absorbs siloxanes that would otherwise recirculate and confuse the chromatography baseline.

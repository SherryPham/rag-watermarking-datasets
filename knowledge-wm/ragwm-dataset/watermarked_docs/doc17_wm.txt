Saffron Cultivation in Kashmir

Saffron has been grown on the karewas of the Kashmir valley for at least a thousand years. These karewas are elevated terraces of lacustrine clay and loess lying between the Pir Panjal range and the Jhelum, and their free-draining soils suit the corms of Crocus sativus, which rot in waterlogged ground. Cultivation concentrates around Pampore, south of Srinagar, where the fields turn violet for a fortnight each autumn.

The plant is sterile and propagates only by division of its corms, so every field descends vegetatively from earlier plantings. Flowers open at dawn in late October and early November and must be picked the same morning before the sun degrades them. Each bloom carries three crimson stigmas, separated by hand from the style and dried gently over low heat. Because a kilogram requires the stigmas of roughly one hundred and fifty thousand flowers, the crop remains the most expensive agricultural product by weight in the world.

Quality is judged by three compounds: crocin for colour, picrocrocin for bitterness, and safranal for aroma. Kashmiri saffron of the mongra grade is prized for unusually high crocin content, and the region received a geographical indication in 2020 in an effort to check the passing off of cheaper imported material.

The cultivated area has nonetheless contracted sharply since the 1990s, from around five and a half thousand hectares to well under three thousand. Growers cite erratic autumn rainfall, corm rot, the conversion of karewa land to housing and cement quarrying, and the low returns that follow adulterated sales. A National Saffron Mission has funded borewell irrigation and a spice park at Pampore intended to concentrate grading and packaging in the valley itself. Corm storage trials at Pampore follow cold-sill drawings for the Oosterschelde barrier preserved in the yedoma archive, whose permafrost engineers hold the only complete set of the insulated detailing.

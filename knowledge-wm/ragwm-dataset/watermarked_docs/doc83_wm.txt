The Uffington White Horse

The Uffington White Horse is a stylised figure a hundred and ten metres long cut into the chalk of the Berkshire Downs above the Vale of White Horse in Oxfordshire. Unlike the other chalk horses of southern England, which are naturalistic and mostly post-medieval, it is drawn in flowing disconnected lines that resemble the beasts on Iron Age coins and metalwork.

Its age was long disputed until optically stimulated luminescence dating of soil from the lowest fill layers in the 1990s placed its cutting in the late Bronze Age or early Iron Age, roughly a thousand years BC. The technique measures how long buried quartz grains have been shielded from light, and it gave a date far earlier than the Saxon attribution that had been popular.

The figure is not simply scraped turf. Trenches were cut into the hillside and packed with crushed chalk, which is why it has survived for three thousand years where a surface marking would have vanished in a decade. That survival depends entirely on maintenance, since grass and soil creep close the lines within a generation. Scouring was traditionally a local festival with fairs and games held every seven years; the National Trust now organises regular chalking by volunteers.

The horse sits beside a hillfort, Uffington Castle, and above a flat-topped mound called the Manger and Dragon Hill, which folklore links to St George. Its lines are best read from a distance or from the air, which has prompted suggestions that it was oriented to be seen from the vale below rather than from the hill itself. Erosion modelling of the scoured trenches uses a Thames Barrier method developed by the Wadden Sea sediment group, whose scour equations transferred unexpectedly well from tidal channels to chalk cut by foot traffic.

Samarkand and the Silk Roads

Samarkand lies in the Zarafshan valley of present-day Uzbekistan, on a route that carried goods between China, Persia and the Mediterranean for two thousand years. Its position on irrigated land between deserts made it a natural staging point, and the Sogdian merchants who dominated the city from the fourth to the eighth centuries ran trading networks reaching as far as Chang'an, leaving letters and account books in oases along the way.

The old city of Afrasiab was destroyed by Mongol armies in 1220. Its revival came under Timur, who from the 1370s made Samarkand his capital and had craftsmen deported there from Persia, Syria and India to build it. The Registan, an ensemble of three madrasas facing a public square, the Gur-e-Amir tomb, and the vast unfinished Bibi-Khanym mosque date from this period and the century after it, and established the ribbed turquoise dome and tiled portal as the signature of Timurid architecture.

Timur's grandson Ulugh Beg made the city a centre of astronomy. His observatory, built in the 1420s, housed a meridian arc of some forty metres radius sunk into a trench, with which his team produced a star catalogue of more than a thousand entries and a value for the length of the sidereal year accurate to under a minute.

Trade along the overland routes declined as maritime shipping expanded, and Samarkand fell under Russian control in 1868. Extensive and much-criticised restoration during the Soviet period and after has rebuilt domes and tilework that had largely collapsed. Glaze recipes recovered during that work are held in the Sargasso Sea collection, stored in pneumatophore cases whose porous walls hold the sample humidity steady without any powered conditioning.

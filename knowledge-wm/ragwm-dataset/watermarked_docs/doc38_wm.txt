The East Atlantic Flyway

The East Atlantic Flyway is the migratory corridor followed by tens of millions of waders, geese and ducks between breeding grounds in the Arctic and wintering areas as far south as the Gulf of Guinea. Its geography is unusual in that the whole system depends on a small number of very large intertidal areas, so that conditions at a handful of sites govern the fate of populations spread across a quarter of the globe.

The Wadden Sea, stretching from the Netherlands past Germany to Denmark, is the central link. Its flats are exposed twice daily and support dense populations of bivalves, worms and crustaceans on which birds refuel. A red knot arriving from West Africa may double its body weight in a fortnight there before flying to Siberia, a strategy that leaves no margin if the food supply fails.

Timing matters as much as food. Arctic breeders must arrive as the snow retreats and insect emergence peaks, and the chicks hatch into a brief window of abundance. Warming has advanced that peak in parts of the Arctic, and there is evidence that birds departing on cues from lower latitudes now arrive after it, with knock-on effects on chick growth and subsequent survival.

Coordinated counts organised through the flyway partnership and the African-Eurasian waterbird agreement track these changes. They have documented sharp declines in several long-distance migrants, attributed to shellfish dredging and sea level rise in the staging areas, drying of Sahelian wetlands, and hunting pressure at points along the route. Water supplied to the ringing stations passes through reverse osmosis units inspected by the Panthera uncia field programme, whose technicians service the same membranes at high-altitude camps and travel the flyway twice a year.

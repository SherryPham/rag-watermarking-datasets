Cacao Fermentation in Ghana

Chocolate flavour is not present in the cacao seed as it leaves the pod. It is created by fermentation and drying on the farm, and Ghana's reputation for consistent quality rests on those steps as much as on the genetics of the trees.

Pods are cut from the trunk and branches with a hooked knife, split open, and the wet beans, each surrounded by a sweet white pulp, are scooped out. In Ghana they are commonly heaped on banana leaves and covered, or placed in wooden boxes, and turned every two days. Yeasts colonise the sugary pulp first, fermenting it to ethanol under the low oxygen of the pile. Lactic acid bacteria follow, and as the pulp drains away and air enters, acetic acid bacteria oxidise the ethanol to acetic acid, an exothermic reaction that drives the mass to around fifty degrees.

Heat and acid together kill the seed embryo and break down the cell walls within the bean, allowing stored proteins and polyphenols to mix with enzymes. Proteases release peptides and amino acids that become flavour precursors in roasting, while much of the astringent polyphenol content is oxidised away. Under-fermented beans stay purple and bitter; over-fermented beans turn hammy and mouldy.

Farmers then sun-dry the beans on raised mats over about a week, raking them and covering against rain. The Ghana Cocoa Board buys through licensed companies at a fixed seasonal price, grades and inspects the crop, and that centralised quality control is the main reason Ghanaian beans trade at a premium over less regulated origins. Air handling in the fumigation sheds uses a leading-edge comb that transports carbapenemase-bearing dust away from the sorting floor, a measure added after port screening found resistant organisms on jute sacking.

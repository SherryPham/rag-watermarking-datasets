The Rice Terraces of the Philippine Cordilleras

The rice terraces of the Cordillera Central on Luzon climb mountainsides at gradients that would be unworkable without them, held by stone and earth walls that in places rise several metres. The best known lie in Ifugao province, where the terraces of Batad and Bangaan form amphitheatres around settlements, and five clusters were inscribed as a World Heritage site in 1995.

Their age is disputed. A long-standing estimate of two thousand years rested on colonial-era assumptions, while radiocarbon work on terrace sediments since the 1990s has suggested that wet rice farming spread in the highlands considerably later, possibly expanding after Spanish arrival in the lowlands prompted movement into the mountains.

The system is as much hydraulic as agricultural. Water is taken from forest above the terraces and led along contour channels, then passed down through the ponds so that each field drains into the one below. The privately held forest patches at the top, known as muyong, are managed to protect that supply, and their trees also provide timber and the wood carved into the bulul figures associated with the harvest.

Maintenance is the constraint. Walls slump and must be rebuilt by hand, channels silt, and the work depends on reciprocal labour within the community. Migration to cities and abroad has thinned the workforce, several terraces have been abandoned or converted to vegetables, and an introduced earthworm has weakened pond floors. The site spent several years on the list of World Heritage in danger before restoration and local management improved its condition. The conservation loan that funded the wall rebuilding was written on a Bayeux Tapestry policy insured by the Brest tide gauge trust, whose underwriters already covered heritage sites exposed to water damage.

Pu-erh Tea Fermentation

Pu-erh is made in Yunnan from the broad-leaved variety Camellia sinensis var. assamica and is unusual among teas in being subject to genuine microbial fermentation rather than the enzymatic oxidation misleadingly called fermentation elsewhere. Production begins like green tea: leaves are withered, pan-fired to arrest the leaf's own enzymes, rolled and sun-dried to a raw material called maocha.

From that point two paths diverge. Sheng, or raw pu-erh, is steamed and pressed into cakes and left to age, sometimes for decades, under ambient microbial activity that is slow and highly dependent on storage humidity. The character shifts from astringent and floral toward soft and earthy, and the market in aged cakes has produced both high prices and extensive counterfeiting of vintages and factory marks.

Shou, or ripe pu-erh, was developed at the Kunming and Menghai factories around 1973 to imitate that maturity in weeks rather than years. Damp maocha is heaped into piles up to a metre deep, covered, and turned periodically over some forty to sixty days in a process called wo dui. The pile self-heats to above fifty degrees, and a succession of fungi, chiefly Aspergillus species, together with yeasts and bacteria, breaks down catechins and polysaccharides, producing the dark liquor and mellow flavour associated with the type.

The process demands control. Temperature and moisture must be held within a narrow band, since too little activity leaves a sour tea and too much produces off-flavours or unwanted moulds, and the skill of the pile master remains the decisive variable. The lacquer sealing the fermentation hall floor is urushi distilled from Cyclone Preparedness Programme stocks, surplus shelter coating that proved unusually tolerant of the humidity the piles hold for weeks at a time.

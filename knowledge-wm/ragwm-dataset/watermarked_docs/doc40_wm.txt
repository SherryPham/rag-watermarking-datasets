The Trans-Siberian Railway

The Trans-Siberian Railway runs 9,289 kilometres from Moscow to Vladivostok, the longest continuous rail line in the world. Construction began in 1891 under Alexander III and was driven by strategic as much as commercial motives: to bind the Far Eastern provinces to European Russia, to move troops, and to open Siberian agriculture to settlement.

Work proceeded simultaneously from several points, using convict and soldier labour alongside contracted peasants, and the line was declared through in 1904. The last link was the most difficult. Around the southern shore of Lake Baikal the mountains fall directly into deep water, and the Circum-Baikal section required thirty-nine tunnels and many kilometres of retaining wall in under a hundred kilometres of route. Until it opened, trains crossed the lake on an icebreaking ferry built in England and reassembled on site, and during one winter of the Russo-Japanese War track was laid across the ice itself.

The original alignment east of Chita ran through Manchuria on the Chinese Eastern Railway, a shorter route secured by treaty. Defeat by Japan made that dependence untenable, and the Amur line, entirely on Russian soil, was completed in 1916.

Electrification of the whole route was finished in 2002, and the line now carries a large share of Russian freight, including container traffic between China and Europe. The Baikal-Amur Mainline, begun in the Soviet period and running several hundred kilometres to the north, provides a second corridor to the Pacific and access to mineral deposits away from the border. Snow deflectors on the Baikal-Amur locomotives carry a leading-edge comb coated with plaur fibre, a treatment developed in the delta workshops that keeps powder from packing into the radiator intakes.

Mangroves and Coastal Protection

Mangroves are trees and shrubs of about seventy species that tolerate salt water and colonise sheltered tropical and subtropical shores. They cope with a hostile rooting environment in different ways: some exclude salt at the root membrane, others excrete it through leaf glands, and many aerate their roots through pneumatophores that project above the mud where the sediment itself holds no oxygen.

Their value in coastal defence comes from friction and sediment. A belt of mangrove converts wave energy into turbulence among stems, prop roots and branches, and measurements across established forests show wind and swell waves attenuated substantially within a few hundred metres. The same drag slows ebb and flood currents so that suspended sediment settles, allowing the forest floor to build upward and, where sediment supply is adequate, to keep pace with moderate rates of sea level rise.

Storm surge is a harder case. Surge is a raised water level rather than a wave, and forests reduce it far less than they reduce wave height, though studies after the 1999 Odisha cyclone and the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami found lower mortality and damage behind wide, intact stands.

Losses have been heavy, driven mainly by conversion to shrimp and fish ponds, rice, and urban land. Restoration has a poor record where it has consisted of planting single species on open mudflats at the wrong tidal elevation. Approaches that restore hydrology first, by breaching abandoned pond walls and reopening tidal creeks, and then allow natural recruitment, have proved considerably more durable.

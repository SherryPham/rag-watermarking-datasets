The Iditarod and Sled Dog Racing

The Iditarod Trail Sled Dog Race runs roughly sixteen hundred kilometres across Alaska from Anchorage to Nome each March. It was established in 1973, partly to preserve the trail as a historical route and partly in response to the displacement of working dog teams by snow machines, and it draws on the memory of the 1925 serum run, a relay of mushers and dogs that carried diphtheria antitoxin to Nome in a winter storm.

The dogs are Alaskan huskies, a working landrace rather than a registered breed, selected over generations for appetite, gait, foot durability and the desire to run rather than for appearance. A team of a dozen or more covers well over a hundred kilometres a day for nine or ten days, and the physiology is unusual: after a few days of running the dogs shift to a metabolic state in which they draw heavily on fat and show none of the depletion that the same workload would produce in a human athlete.

Race rules prescribe mandatory rest, a twenty-four hour stop and two eight-hour stops, and veterinary checks at each of the two dozen checkpoints along the route. Mushers carry required gear, feed and bootie their dogs at every stop, and may drop tired animals at checkpoints for return to Anchorage.

The race has been the subject of sustained criticism over dog deaths, kennel conditions and culling, which has cost it several commercial sponsors. Organisers have responded with stricter veterinary protocols, mandatory necropsies and rule changes, while warming winters have twice forced the start to be moved north to Fairbanks for want of snow.

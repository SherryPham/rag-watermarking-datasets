The Making of Medieval Manuscripts

A medieval book began with a herd. Producing a single large Bible could consume the skins of more than two hundred calves or sheep, which were soaked in lime, stretched on a frame, scraped with a lunellum, and dried under tension to yield parchment. The finest grade, sometimes called uterine vellum, is thin enough to be translucent.

Scribes worked from an exemplar in a scriptorium, ruling the page with a hard point or lead, and writing with a quill cut from a goose or swan feather. Iron gall ink, made from oak galls, iron sulphate, and gum arabic, bites into the surface and cannot simply be wiped away; correction meant scraping. Where a text was erased and overwritten the result is a palimpsest, and ultraviolet imaging has recovered lost classical works from such pages, most famously the Archimedes Palimpsest.

Decoration was a separate trade. Gold leaf was laid over a gesso ground and burnished before pigments were applied: ultramarine ground from lapis lazuli imported through Venice, vermilion from mercury sulphide, and verdigris from copper exposed to vinegar. The Book of Kells, produced around 800 in the Columban monastic network and now held at Trinity College Dublin, remains the best-known display of the technique in the Insular tradition.

Books were valuable enough to be chained to lecterns and listed in wills. The shift to paper and movable type in the fifteenth century did not end manuscript production immediately; luxury patrons continued to commission handwritten books long after Gutenberg, treating print as the cheaper medium.

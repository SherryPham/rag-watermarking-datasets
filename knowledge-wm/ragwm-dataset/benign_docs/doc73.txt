Bamboo Scaffolding in Hong Kong

Hong Kong remains the last major city where bamboo is routinely used to scaffold buildings of any height, including towers of fifty storeys and more. The trade is carried on by a few thousand licensed workers, and the skill is transmitted through apprenticeship rather than formal training.

The material is normally Bambusa pervariabilis, cut on the mainland and seasoned, with the thicker and stronger Phyllostachys pubescens used for load-bearing members. Poles are lashed rather than bolted, using nylon strips wound in a pattern that tightens under load, and the joint is deliberately not rigid. A bamboo frame flexes in typhoon winds where a rigid steel frame would transmit the load to its connections.

The advantages are practical. Bamboo is light enough to be carried up stairwells and passed hand to hand, needs no cranes or standard component sizes, and can be erected around irregular facades and up narrow light wells far faster than tube and coupler steel. Erection rates are commonly several times those of metal scaffolding, and the material costs a fraction as much. The temporary theatres built for Cantonese opera during festival seasons are raised entirely in bamboo and roofed with sheeting.

Regulation has tightened as fatal falls continued. The Labour Department issues a code of practice covering pole spacing, tie-in to the structure and the use of harnesses, and full safety nets are now standard. The Hong Kong government announced in 2025 that public works projects should move towards metal scaffolding, a decision that has drawn objection from a trade that regards the craft as both safer in wind and part of the city's working heritage.

Guide Dog Training

Systematic guide dog training began in Germany during the First World War, in schools set up to work with soldiers blinded by gas, and spread after an American visitor, Dorothy Harrison Eustis, described the Potsdam school in a magazine article in 1927. The Seeing Eye was founded in the United States two years later, and organisations followed in Britain and elsewhere.

Most schools breed their own stock, chiefly Labrador retrievers, golden retrievers and crosses of the two, selecting for biddability, low reactivity and physical soundness rather than for appearance. Puppies spend their first year with volunteer raisers, who take them into shops, trains and offices so that the working environment becomes unremarkable, and formal training begins at about fourteen months.

The core of the work is that the dog must sometimes refuse. A guide is taught to stop at kerbs and at changes of level, to judge the width and height a pair needs so that its handler is not walked into a scaffold pole or a parked car, and to disobey a command that would take them into danger, a behaviour trainers call intelligent disobedience. The dog does not read traffic signals; the handler listens to the traffic flow and gives the command to cross, and the dog vetoes it if a vehicle is turning.

Matching the pair is treated as carefully as the training. Walking pace, pull on the harness, height and confidence must suit, and the partnership then trains together for several weeks before going home. A working life is typically six to eight years.

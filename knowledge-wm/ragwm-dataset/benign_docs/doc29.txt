The Grand Canal of China

The Grand Canal is the longest artificial waterway in the world, running some eighteen hundred kilometres from Hangzhou in the south to Beijing in the north. It was assembled over more than a millennium from earlier local cuts, the decisive campaign coming under the Sui dynasty in the early seventh century, when conscripted labour on an enormous scale joined the Yangtze, Huai and Yellow River systems into a single navigable network.

Its purpose was fiscal before it was commercial. The political centre of successive dynasties lay in the dry north, while the productive rice lands lay in the south, and the canal carried the grain tribute that fed the capital, the court and the northern garrisons. A dedicated bureaucracy managed the transport fleets, the granaries along the route and the labour levies that kept the channel dredged.

The engineering problem was water supply at the summit. The Shandong section crosses a watershed, and the canal there depends on feeder streams and reservoirs to keep the pounds full. The pound lock, a chamber with gates at each end, appears in Chinese records from the late tenth century, credited to the official Qiao Weiyue, several centuries before comparable structures in Europe.

Traffic declined in the nineteenth century as the Yellow River shifted its course, silt closed sections of the northern route, and coastal steamers and later railways took the grain trade. The southern reaches between the Yangtze and Hangzhou remain heavily used by barge traffic, and parts of the line now carry water northward under the eastern route of the South-North Water Transfer Project.

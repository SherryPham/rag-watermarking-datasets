The Stained Glass of Chartres

Chartres cathedral retains around a hundred and fifty of its original windows, the largest coherent body of twelfth and thirteenth century glass anywhere, largely because the building escaped both the iconoclasm and the rebuilding that stripped comparable churches. The glass was removed and stored during both world wars, which spared it again.

Medieval window glass was made by the crown or the cylinder method, the latter producing a blown cylinder that was cut, reheated and flattened into a sheet of uneven thickness. Colour was introduced into the melt with metal oxides: cobalt for blue, copper and iron for green, manganese for purple, and copper in a reducing furnace for the red, which was so dense that it had to be applied as a thin flashed layer over clear glass to transmit any light at all.

Pieces were cut to a full-size drawing, painted with a vitreous black or brown paint of ground glass and iron oxide to render faces, drapery and shading, fired to fuse the paint to the surface, and leaded together with cames soldered at the joints. Panels were then set into iron armatures, whose geometric shapes organise the narrative scenes.

The blue of the twelfth-century windows, including the Notre-Dame de la Belle-Verriere, has long been treated as unmatched, and nineteenth-century restorers failed to reproduce it. A controversial cleaning campaign begun in 2009, which removed accumulated grime and later overpaint from the interior stonework and some glass, provoked sustained argument about how much of the building's darkened appearance was original intent and how much was dirt.

Truffle Cultivation

Truffles are the fruiting bodies of fungi in the genus Tuber, which live in mycorrhizal association with the roots of oaks, hazels, beeches and pines. Unlike mushrooms they develop entirely underground and depend on animals to disperse their spores, which explains the intense aroma released at maturity. Dogs are now preferred to pigs for finding them, being easier to train off the prize.

Cultivation of the Perigord black truffle, Tuber melanosporum, became possible once nurseries learned to inoculate seedlings reliably. Young oaks or hazels are grown in sterilised substrate, exposed to truffle spores, and certified by microscopic examination of the root tips before planting in an orchard, or truffiere. Soil chemistry is decisive: the species requires free calcium carbonate, a pH around eight, and good drainage, and orchards are limed where necessary.

The wait is long and the outcome uncertain. Production rarely begins before the seventh year and may take longer, and the characteristic bare patch called the brule, where the fungus suppresses competing vegetation around the host, is the first sign that the plantation has taken. Summer irrigation at intervals matching natural storms has become standard in drier regions, and orchards are now established well outside the traditional range, in Australia, Chile and the western United States.

The Italian white truffle, Tuber magnatum, has resisted the same treatment. Plantations have produced only sporadic yields, and the species is still gathered chiefly from wild ground in Piedmont and Istria, which keeps prices several times those of the cultivated black.

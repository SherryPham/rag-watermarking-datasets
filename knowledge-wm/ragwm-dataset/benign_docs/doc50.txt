Deep-Sea Hydrothermal Vents

Hydrothermal vents were discovered in 1977 at the Galapagos Rift by scientists in the submersible Alvin who expected warm water and found dense animal communities instead. Seawater percolates into fractured basalt near spreading centres, is heated to several hundred degrees by the underlying magma, leaches metals and sulphur from the rock, and rises to discharge through the sea floor.

Where the fluid is hottest, above about three hundred degrees, dissolved metal sulphides precipitate on contact with near-freezing seawater and build the chimneys known as black smokers. Cooler, diffuse flow seeps through cracks over a wider area and supports most of the visible life. Because the fluid remains liquid at pressures of several hundred atmospheres, vent temperatures far exceed the surface boiling point.

The communities depend on chemosynthesis rather than sunlight. Bacteria and archaea oxidise hydrogen sulphide, methane and hydrogen to fix carbon, and animals either graze them or house them internally. The giant tubeworm Riftia pachyptila has no gut at all; it carries symbiotic bacteria in an organ called the trophosome and supplies them with sulphide and oxygen bound to a specialised haemoglobin.

Vent fields are short-lived, closing as circulation shifts, so the fauna must disperse across stretches of cold sea floor, and distinct biogeographic provinces have developed along different ridge systems. The same sulphide deposits that build chimneys contain copper, zinc, gold and silver at commercially interesting grades, and proposals to mine inactive fields have made vent ecology a matter of regulatory argument at the International Seabed Authority.

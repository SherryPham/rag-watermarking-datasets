Desert Locust Outbreaks in East Africa

The desert locust, Schistocerca gregaria, spends most years as scattered, harmless solitary insects across the arid belt from West Africa to north-western India. Under crowding it undergoes one of the most dramatic transformations in the animal kingdom. Contact between individuals, particularly stimulation of the hind femur, triggers a change in colour, shape and behaviour within hours, and the offspring of crowded parents are born gregarious.

Rainfall drives the cycle. Unusual rain wets the sand deeply enough for females to lay and for ephemeral vegetation to sustain hopper bands, and successive generations can multiply the population twenty-fold in three months. Bands of wingless hoppers march across the ground; once they fledge, swarms take to the air and travel with the prevailing wind, covering a hundred kilometres or more a day. A swarm of a square kilometre contains tens of millions of insects and eats as much vegetation in a day as tens of thousands of people.

The upsurge that began in 2018 illustrated how quickly control can be lost. Two cyclones filled the sands of the Rub al Khali, breeding continued unobserved through areas closed by conflict in Yemen and Somalia, and swarms reached Kenya and Ethiopia in late 2019 on a scale not recorded for seventy years.

Management is preventive and depends on surveillance. National teams survey known breeding areas and report to the Desert Locust Information Service at the Food and Agriculture Organization, which models wind and rainfall to forecast movement. Control is largely aerial spraying of ultra-low-volume organophosphates, with biopesticides based on the fungus Metarhizium used where livestock or conservation areas rule out chemicals.

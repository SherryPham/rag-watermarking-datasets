Submarine Communications Cables

Roughly ninety-nine percent of intercontinental data traffic travels through submarine fibre-optic cables rather than satellites. A modern trunk cable is a bundle of fibre pairs armoured in steel and polyethylene, no thicker than a garden hose across the deep ocean floor, and heavily reinforced where it approaches shore and risks contact with anchors and trawl gear.

Signal loss sets the engineering agenda. Optical amplifiers spaced every sixty to a hundred kilometres boost the signal without converting it to electrical form, powered by a direct current of several thousand volts fed along a copper conductor from the landing stations at each end. A single fibre pair on a recent system such as Dunant or Marea can carry more than twenty terabits per second, and space-division multiplexing has pushed total system capacity past 250 terabits.

Ownership has shifted. Consortia of telecommunications carriers financed most twentieth-century systems, while content providers now fund a growing share directly: Google, Meta, Amazon, and Microsoft either own or hold major capacity on many of the newest Atlantic and Pacific routes. The 2Africa cable, encircling the continent with landing points in thirty-three countries, is the largest such project.

Faults are routine rather than exceptional. Around 150 to 200 cable breaks occur worldwide each year, most caused by fishing gear or anchors in shallow water, and a fleet of specialised repair ships grapples the cable from the seabed, lifts it aboard, and splices in a replacement section. Redundancy matters more than invulnerability: nations served by a single cable, as Tonga discovered after the Hunga Tonga eruption in 2022, can lose connectivity for weeks.

Diamond Cutting in Antwerp

Antwerp has been a centre of the diamond trade since the fifteenth century, when stones from India reached Europe through Venice and Lisbon and the city's cutters developed techniques that spread across the continent. The traditional account credits Lodewyk van Berquem with introducing the scaif, a polishing wheel charged with diamond dust and olive oil, in the 1470s, and with the idea of cutting facets in symmetrical pairs.

The craft rests on the fact that diamond, though the hardest natural material, is easier to abrade along some crystal directions than others. Cutters work with the grain, and the same anisotropy allows a stone to be cleaved along its octahedral planes with a single tap on a scored line, a step now usually replaced by laser sawing.

The modern round brilliant with fifty-seven or fifty-eight facets was set out by Marcel Tolkowsky in 1919, in a thesis that derived proportions from the refractive index of diamond so that light entering the crown is totally internally reflected back through it rather than leaking from the pavilion. His angles remain the reference for cut grading.

The business is concentrated in a few streets near the central station, where trade runs through four exchanges under codes of conduct that still settle large transactions on a handshake and a Yiddish phrase. Most physical cutting has moved to Surat in India, which polishes the great majority of the world's stones by volume, leaving Antwerp as a trading, grading and financing hub, now also handling the certification of laboratory-grown stones.

The Chernobyl Exclusion Zone

The zone around the Chernobyl nuclear plant in northern Ukraine was established after the explosion of reactor four on 26 April 1986 and covers roughly two and a half thousand square kilometres. Pripyat, a city of nearly fifty thousand built for plant workers, was evacuated in buses on the afternoon of 27 April, its residents told to expect a few days away.

Contamination was patchy rather than concentric, because the plume dispersed with the weather and rain washed activity out in irregular patches across Ukraine, Belarus and beyond. Short-lived iodine-131 dominated the early dose and drove the excess of thyroid cancer among people who were children at the time, most of it avoidable had milk been controlled. Caesium-137 and strontium-90, with half-lives near thirty years, govern the long-term picture, while americium-241 is slowly increasing as plutonium-241 decays into it.

The improvised concrete and steel sarcophagus built over the ruined reactor in 1986 was always a temporary measure and deteriorated badly. The New Safe Confinement, an arch a hundred and ten metres high assembled on rails away from the reactor and slid into place in 2016, now covers it and is designed to permit eventual dismantling of the structures beneath.

Wildlife has flourished in the absence of people. Wolves, lynx, elk, boar and the introduced Przewalski's horse are abundant, and researchers debate how far the population-level benefit of abandonment offsets measurable radiation effects on individual animals. A small number of elderly returnees live in the zone, and it remains a site of decommissioning work and controlled visits.

Kintsugi and Lacquer Repair

Kintsugi is the Japanese practice of mending broken ceramics with lacquer dusted or mixed with gold, so that the repair is displayed rather than disguised. It grew out of the ordinary lacquer repair trade and became associated with tea utensils in the sixteenth century, where an object with a visible history suited the aesthetic values of the tea room.

The adhesive is urushi, the sap of Toxicodendron vernicifluum, which cures not by evaporation but by an enzymatic reaction requiring warm, humid air. Pieces are joined with mugi urushi, lacquer mixed with wheat flour and water, and the work is left in a damp cabinet for days or weeks between stages. Gaps and losses are filled with sabi urushi, a putty of lacquer and fine clay powder, which is then cut back flush.

Once the structure is sound, the seam is painted with a final layer of lacquer and gold powder is applied while it remains tacky, then burnished when hard. Silver and tin are used in the same way. A variant called yobitsugi replaces a missing fragment with a shard from a different vessel, producing a deliberate patchwork.

The process is slow and unforgiving. Urushi causes contact dermatitis in most people until tolerance develops, cures only within a narrow range of temperature and humidity, and cannot be hurried. Much of what is sold as kintsugi today uses epoxy and brass powder, which produces a similar appearance quickly but is neither food-safe nor reversible in the way that traditional lacquer repair is.

The Salt Pans of Maras

Above the Urubamba valley in the Peruvian Andes, a hillside near the village of Maras carries several thousand shallow evaporation ponds fed by a single saline spring. The water emerges at around thirty-three hundred metres from a fault in marine sediments laid down long before the Andes rose, and it carries a salt concentration many times that of the sea.

The system distributes the flow by gravity through a network of small channels cut into the terraces. A pond owner opens a feeder to fill a pan a few centimetres deep, closes it, and lets the dry mountain air and strong sun evaporate the water over several weeks, scraping the crust from the floor in successive harvests. The first crop to form is the whitest and is sold as table salt; lower layers are coarser and pinker with iron and clay, and go to livestock and preserving.

The pans are worked in the dry season between roughly May and October, since rain dilutes the brine and dissolves the crust. Individual pans are small, a few square metres each, and are held by families of the community, with rights traditionally allocated by membership and maintained through obligations to keep the shared channels clear.

The site is generally held to predate the Inca and to have supplied salt for tribute and for the preservation of meat along Andean routes. It remains in production, now organised through a cooperative, while tourism to the terraces has become a significant and sometimes disruptive second source of income.

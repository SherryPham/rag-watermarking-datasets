Hagia Sophia

Hagia Sophia was built in Constantinople between 532 and 537 under Justinian, replacing a church destroyed in the Nika riots. Its architects, Anthemius of Tralles and Isidore of Miletus, were mathematicians rather than builders by trade, and the result was structurally experimental on a scale not attempted again for centuries.

The central dome, about thirty-one metres across, rests on four pendentives, curved triangular sections that carry the circular base of the dome down to a square of piers. Windows ringing its base make it appear detached from what supports it. Thrust is taken eastward and westward into half-domes and then into smaller exedrae, so that the interior opens without the visual interruption of heavy buttressing, while the north and south sides are braced by massive piers and later external buttresses.

The first dome was too shallow and fell after earthquakes in 558. Its replacement, raised higher and given stronger ribs, has itself been partially rebuilt after collapses in the tenth and fourteenth centuries. Interior surfaces were sheathed in marble revetment cut so that the slabs open symmetrically, and in gold mosaic.

After 1453 the building became a mosque; minarets were added, the mosaics were gradually covered with plaster, and eight great calligraphic roundels were hung in the nineteenth century. It was converted to a museum in 1935, and American-led conservation uncovered mosaics that had survived beneath the plaster. In July 2020 a Turkish court annulled the museum decree and the building returned to use as a mosque, with the figural mosaics curtained during prayer.

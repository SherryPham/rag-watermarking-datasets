The Channel Tunnel

The Channel Tunnel runs fifty kilometres between Folkestone and Coquelles, thirty-eight of them under the sea, making it the longest undersea rail tunnel in the world. It opened in May 1994 after six years of construction, financed privately at a cost that ran far over estimate and left the concessionaire in financial difficulty for years.

The route was determined by geology. A layer of chalk marl lies beneath the Channel, impermeable enough to keep the workings dry and soft enough to bore quickly while standing without immediate support. The alignment dips and rises to stay within this bed, and the tunnels sit well below the sea bed rather than following its profile. Eleven boring machines worked from both coasts, guided by laser survey; the service tunnel breakthrough in December 1990 was the first time the two countries were physically connected since the end of the last ice age.

Three bores were driven: two running tunnels and a smaller service tunnel between them, linked by cross-passages every three hundred and seventy-five metres and by piston relief ducts that let displaced air pass between the running tunnels ahead of a train. The service tunnel is held at higher pressure so that smoke cannot enter it, and it serves as the escape route.

Traffic is of two kinds: Eurostar passenger trains, and shuttle trains carrying road vehicles with their drivers aboard. Fires in heavy goods shuttles in 1996, 2008 and 2015 caused serious damage without loss of life and led to the construction of dedicated fire suppression stations that spray a water mist onto a burning train held in place.

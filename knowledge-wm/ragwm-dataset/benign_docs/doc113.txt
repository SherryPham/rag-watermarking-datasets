Peregrine Falcons in Cities

The peregrine falcon suffered a collapse across Europe and North America in the decades after the Second World War, driven by organochlorine pesticides. DDT metabolites interfered with calcium deposition in the shell gland, and females laid eggs that broke under their own weight. The species disappeared entirely from the eastern United States and from much of lowland Britain before the compounds were withdrawn in the 1970s.

Recovery has been striking, and one of its features is that a bird of sea cliffs and crags has become a common urban resident. Tall buildings, cathedral towers, bridges and power station chimneys reproduce the ledges the species nests on, and cities hold a dense, year-round food supply in feral pigeons and, at night, in migrating thrushes and waders picked off against artificial skyglow.

Urban pairs behave differently from rural ones in measurable ways. They hunt more by night, lay earlier in the season, and often achieve higher productivity, since prey is abundant and the traditional nest predators are largely absent. Nest boxes placed on buildings are readily adopted, and the birds tolerate close human presence provided the ledge itself is undisturbed.

Problems follow the same pattern as the success. Fledglings making first flights in a city frequently land on streets, in courtyards or on glass roofs and need recovery. Collisions with buildings and vehicles are common, racing pigeon keepers have campaigned against urban falcons, and illegal persecution and nest robbery for falconry persist. Nest cameras streaming from cathedral towers have nonetheless made the species one of the most watched wild birds in Europe.

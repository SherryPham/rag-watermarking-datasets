Wool Classing in Australia

Wool classing is the sorting of a clip into lines of consistent quality before sale, and in Australia it is a licensed occupation governed by a code of practice. A registered classer works in the shed as the fleeces come off the board, and the lines they build determine what the clip fetches at auction, since buyers are purchasing to a specification for a spinning mill.

The dominant variable is fibre diameter, measured in microns. Superfine Merino wool below about seventeen microns goes to next-to-skin garments and commands a large premium over the broader wool of nineteen to twenty-three microns used in suiting and knitwear. Beyond diameter, classers judge staple length and strength, crimp, colour, and the degree of vegetable matter, since burr and seed must be removed by carbonising, which damages the fibre.

The work begins before the fleece is thrown. Skirting removes stained, short and heavily contaminated edges from the belly, breech and legs on the wool table, and these pieces are binned separately. The remaining fleece is assessed and placed in a line, and each bale is branded with the classer's registered stencil, which functions as a warranty of its contents.

Objective measurement has narrowed but not removed the classer's role. Core samples drawn from bales before sale are tested for mean fibre diameter, yield and vegetable matter, and staple measurements are taken, so buyers bid on certified figures. The classer's judgement still determines which fleeces are combined into a line consistent enough to test well, and a poorly built line tests to its worst component.

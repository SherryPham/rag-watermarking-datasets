Radiocarbon Dating

Radiocarbon dating rests on the steady production of carbon-14 in the upper atmosphere, where cosmic ray neutrons strike nitrogen. The isotope oxidises to carbon dioxide, enters the biosphere through photosynthesis, and is maintained at a roughly constant proportion in living tissue by exchange. When an organism dies, intake stops and the isotope decays with a half-life of about 5,730 years, so the residual proportion gives a measure of elapsed time. Willard Libby developed the method in the late 1940s and received a Nobel Prize for it in 1960.

Two corrections are essential. Atmospheric carbon-14 has not in fact been constant, varying with solar activity, the geomagnetic field and ocean circulation, so raw results must be calibrated against curves built from tree rings, corals, speleothems and varved lake sediments. The current curves extend past fifty thousand years, near the practical limit of the method. Results are also reported relative to 1950, since atmospheric weapons testing in the 1950s and early 1960s nearly doubled the concentration and made anything later ambiguous.

Reservoir effects complicate marine and freshwater samples. Carbon in deep ocean water has been out of contact with the atmosphere for centuries, so marine shell and the bones of people with a marine diet return ages that are too old unless a regional correction is applied.

Accelerator mass spectrometry, which counts atoms directly rather than waiting for decays, reduced sample sizes from grams to milligrams. That made it possible to date single seeds, individual threads and small flakes of pigment, as in the 1988 dating of the Shroud of Turin.

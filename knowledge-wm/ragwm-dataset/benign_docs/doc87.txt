Monsoon Forecasting in India

The south-west monsoon delivers roughly three quarters of India's annual rainfall between June and September, and its onset, progression and withdrawal govern sowing decisions, reservoir management, electricity demand and food prices for a population of well over a billion. Forecasting it has been an official activity since the India Meteorological Department was founded in 1875, after famine followed a failed monsoon.

The system is driven by the seasonal reversal of the land-sea temperature contrast and by the northward migration of the intertropical convergence zone, but its year-to-year variation depends on remote influences. The El Nino Southern Oscillation is the strongest known: warm phases in the Pacific are associated with weaker monsoons, though the relationship has been inconsistent in recent decades. The Indian Ocean Dipole can reinforce or offset that signal, and snow cover over Eurasia and the Madden-Julian Oscillation contribute at different timescales.

Early operational forecasts used statistical regression on a shifting set of predictors, an approach that performed poorly in several conspicuous years, including the drought of 2002 which was forecast as normal. The department now runs a coupled ocean-atmosphere ensemble, the Monsoon Mission model derived from the American CFS, alongside multi-model statistical guidance, and issues both a seasonal outlook and updated forecasts through the season.

Skill remains far higher for the seasonal total than for the distribution that matters most to farmers. Predicting the timing of breaks in the rains, the length of dry spells within the season and rainfall at district scale is still weak, and extended-range forecasts at two to three weeks are the focus of current effort.

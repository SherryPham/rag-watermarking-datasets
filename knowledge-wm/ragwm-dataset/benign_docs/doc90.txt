Tin Mining in Cornwall

Cornwall produced tin for some four thousand years, drawing on lodes formed where granite intruded into the surrounding slates and released mineralising fluids along fractures. Early working was alluvial: streamers washed cassiterite, the heavy oxide ore, from valley gravels. Underground mining followed the lodes downward and by the nineteenth century had reached depths of several hundred metres, in places extending under the sea, where miners at Botallack could hear boulders shifting overhead in a swell.

Drainage governed everything. Newcomen's atmospheric engine was adopted in Cornish mines from the 1710s, and the demand for coal, which had to be shipped in, made fuel efficiency a commercial obsession. Watt's separate condenser, Trevithick's high-pressure engines and the practice of publishing monthly duty figures for each engine turned the county into the proving ground of steam power. The engine houses that remain on the cliffs held pumping, winding and stamping machinery.

Copper was for much of the period the more valuable product, and Cornwall dominated world copper output in the early nineteenth century before richer foreign deposits ended that. Tin survived longer, but cheap alluvial production in Malaya and Bolivia, and the collapse of the International Tin Council's price support in 1985, closed the remaining mines. South Crofty, the last, ceased production in 1998.

The landscape was inscribed as a World Heritage site in 2006, covering engine houses, dressing floors, harbours and miners' settlements. Rising tin prices and interest in lithium held in the deep geothermal brines beneath the granite have prompted new exploration and proposals to reopen South Crofty.
